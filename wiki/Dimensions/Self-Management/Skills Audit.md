# Skills Audit

A skills audit is a structured review of your techniques against stated guidelines, so gaps can be found and improved on purpose. Doing that review regularly is how a learning system stays flexible and useful. The audit is first named at the briefing stage, then again at later checkpoints. The same steps can be used before those stages, without waiting to feel ready.

The review is recommended after several kinds of strain. Learning a skill too fast, without enough practice, makes technique mistakes and weak systems more likely. So does skipping a technique because it did not seem relevant. Those mistakes can be hard to trace even for an experienced coach. A long break can also dull a skill through lack of practice, which is called technique decline. Moving into a new stage that builds on earlier skills is another reason to check, as is the feeling of being stuck without knowing why.

The stance that makes the review work is a growth mindset. That means a willingness to notice being wrong rather than assume you are already correct. Come in intending to understand and improve, even in areas that have already taken a lot of time. Rate yourself honestly. A low rating is not a verdict on past effort, it is a sign you want a clear picture. Take the time the review needs, which can run to hours later on, and practice a skill during the review if that is the only way to see it clearly. Repeat the audit at least every two to three months, or more often when new techniques are arriving quickly.

The steps themselves are grouped as LEEP. First, map the flow of how you currently learn, using the checklist from the briefing stage, and check that the flow includes techniques from every stage you have finished. Second, evaluate each technique against the stated guidelines. Those include checkpoint videos, milestone lists, later checklists, the aims at the start of a stage, and end-of-stage quizzes. Look for gaps that came from rushing or from skipped parts. Rate each technique as a score from 1 point, poor, to 5 points, excellent, as plainly as you can. Third, correct the errors by adjusting the technique toward those targets. Practice only one or two aspects at a time so the work does not overload you, and ask for feedback from your own review, from peers, or from scheduled help. Fourth, practice with care, using small-step tracking and a reflection cycle to experiment, because fixing a technique often takes more than one try, especially after rushing or after technique decline.

The same review can be done two ways. A skill screen is faster. It looks at earlier checkpoint videos and the main lessons that introduced new techniques. It rates competence, accuracy, and consistency lesson by lesson as a score from 1 point to 5 points, and then focuses on scores below 4 points. A comprehensive audit is slower. It reviews each lesson, each checkpoint, and each quiz at the end of a stage, from the start. It looks for gaps between what you understand and what you can actually do, and then focuses on scores below 4 points, or below 5 points for foundational techniques. The faster screen is for regular use. The longer audit is for when the screen has not found the gap, or when the problem has been around for a long time.
