# Skills Audit

> Part of [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management|Self-Management]]

A skills audit is a structured review of the methods a learner currently uses. Each method is scored against a written standard. The output is a short list of what to fix. Everyday practice hides specific defects, and the audit is the pass that finds them. A score is only as good as the standard it is scored against. A full set of high marks is not a good result.

## Core takeaways

- A skills audit reviews the methods in current use, rates each one against a written standard, and produces a short list of what to fix.
- Run one after rushed or selective learning, after a long break, before a technique that depends on an earlier one, or when progress has stalled.
- The four moves are LEEP: map the learning flow, evaluate each method on a 1–5 scale, correct one or two gaps, then practise the repair over several cycles.
- A rating means something only when it is made against a written standard, a recorded attempt, or a known-good example. Beginners systematically overrate themselves, so a rating made from a feeling is not reliable.
- The skill screen is a fast pass over the methods in current use. The comprehensive audit is a slow pass over the whole system, including methods that have been stopped.
- A good result is two or more named defects and a next attempt. Stop auditing if two audits in a row give a full set of top marks and no next action.

## When to run an audit

An audit is most useful in three situations: progress has plateaued, a technique that assumes an earlier one is about to be used, or practice has been inconsistent. Four triggers make that concrete.

**After rushing or selective learning.** Techniques taken up quickly, or with steps skipped, introduce quiet defects. Those defects are hard to trace without a systematic pass.

**After a long break.** Skills degrade without use. This system calls that decay **technique decline**. The audit finds where the method has slipped.

**Before a technique that assumes an earlier one is solid.** A foundational gap that has not been detected carries forward into the later technique and compounds there.

**When stuck or plateaued.** The pass often finds a subtle technique error that is not obvious during normal use.

## The four moves, LEEP

LEEP is four moves: map the flow, rate each method, correct one or two gaps, then practise the repair.

**L, learning flow map.** Write down the actual sequence of methods used on one piece of information, from first contact to later recall. That written sequence is the flow map. A named inventory of techniques sorted by stage is not required, and the method does not supply one.

**E, evaluate.** Review each method against one of three references: a written standard for the technique, a recorded attempt, or a known-good example the learner can actually reach. Rate each method from 1 to 5, where 1 is poor and 5 is excellent. Focus on anything below 4. For foundational techniques, the ones everything else assumes, such as encoding, retrieval, and session setup, focus on anything below 5. The 1–5 scale is only as good as the standard it is scored against. People lowest in a skill lack the knowledge needed to see that they are low, so an honest attitude is not enough on its own. Rate against the standard and not against a feeling. The standard for what counts as better technique is set out in [[wiki/Concepts/Learning Efficiency|Learning Efficiency]]. A low rating means the direction of improvement has been found. It does not mean the earlier effort was wasted.

**E, error correction.** Fix the identified gaps against a target. Work on one or two aspects at a time. Feedback comes from self-evaluation, a peer, or a coach-class reviewer. When the self-rating cannot resolve the gap, use [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management/How to Ask for Feedback|How to Ask for Feedback]].

**P, perfect practice.** Apply two methods to the identified skills. The first is [[wiki/Dimensions/Mindset/Marginal Gains|Marginal Gains]]: make the next small improvement that is almost certain to hold, then stack the next one on it. The second is [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management/Kolbs Experiential Cycle|Kolbs Experiential Cycle]]: record what happened, how it felt and why, what rule that suggests, and what changes next. Expect multiple cycles. Technique decline and rushed learning do not repair in a single pass.

## The skill screen and the comprehensive audit

There are two modes. The **skill screen** is a fast pass over the methods currently in use. It does not cover every note ever taken. Rate each method 1–5 and focus on anything below 4. Use it for regular maintenance and for known gaps. The **comprehensive audit** is a slow pass over the whole system, including methods that have been stopped. Look for places where the theory of a technique and the practice of it differ. Focus on anything below 4, or below 5 for foundational techniques. Use it when the screen missed the root cause, or when the issues are long-standing.

| | Skill screen | Comprehensive audit |
|---|---|---|
| Coverage | Methods in current use | The whole system, including ones no longer used |
| Rating focus | Below 4 | Below 4, or below 5 for foundational |
| Speed | Faster | Slower, more detailed |
| Best use | Regular maintenance; known gaps | Persistent issues; long-standing defects |

## How to rate honestly

Defensiveness hides defects. A low rating is not a negative judgement on past effort. It is a sign that the direction of improvement has been found. The wish to rate generously, so that old material does not have to be revisited, is the same wish that let the defect form. A good audit result is a clear, actionable list of improvement targets. A full row of 5s is not a good audit result.

A 1–5 pass made against a feeling reproduces the defect. Hours spent rating without a standard produce nothing usable. Beginners systematically overrate themselves. [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation|Self-Regulation]] explains why an audit is a control action. An audit is not a test of personality. [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation/Metacognition - The Control Layer|Metacognition: The Control Layer]] explains why honest rating is a control skill.

## Time cost and how often to run it

A thorough comprehensive audit on a mature practice can take hours. That time is the cost of the audit. Sometimes the technique has to be applied during the audit before it can be rated. Run a pass every two to three months, or more often during a period of rapid skill acquisition. That cadence is this system's own teaching default. It is not a fixed law. Regular audits keep the learning system flexible.

## When to stop

Stop auditing if two audits in a row produce a full set of top marks and no next action. The checkable result of an audit is two named defects and a next attempt. A completed grid of scores is not the result. Generous rating is the same wish that hid the defect. The audit exists to produce the list of what to fix.

## How to practice this

1. Pick one piece of information you learned recently. Write the sequence of methods you used on it, from first contact to later recall. That written sequence is your learning flow map.
2. For each method on the map, find a written standard, a recorded attempt, or a known-good example you can reach. Rate the method from 1 to 5 against that reference, not against how it felt. Notice any method that scores below 4, or below 5 if it is encoding, retrieval, or session setup.
3. Choose one or two of the low-scoring methods. Decide the target each one should meet. Get feedback from your own evaluation, a peer, or a coach-class reviewer.
4. Practise the corrected method for more than one cycle. After each cycle, note what happened, how it felt and why, what rule that suggests, and what changes next. Notice that a method which slipped after a break or rushed learning does not repair in one pass.
5. After two or three months, run the pass again. Notice whether the result is a list of named defects and a next attempt. Two audits in a row with all top marks and no next action mean the audits should stop.

## Open questions

Which current methods should be audited first. What audit frequency fits the current rate of technique development.
