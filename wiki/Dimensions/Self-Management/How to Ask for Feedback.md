# How to Ask for Feedback

Feedback is information about how your work or your understanding is going that tells you what to change next. What comes back depends heavily on how the request is written: your situation, your goal, your own assessment and one or two focused questions get a useful answer, and "any thoughts?" gets a vague one.

## Takeaways

- Assess your own work first, every time.
- Frame the goal as a skill or process to build.
- Give enough context, then narrow to one or two questions.
- Write a real self-assessment, even when unsure.
- Feedback only counts once a next attempt uses it.
- Over a third of feedback studies found performance got worse.

## Why the request matters

A useful reply answers three questions: where you are trying to get to, how you are doing, and what to do next. Whoever replies can only answer them if the request supplies the material. Too little information gets a vague reply, and too broad a question gets a flood of points that cannot all be acted on. Replies aimed at the person, such as praise, help least, and replies about the task and the method help most.

```
                little context       much context
narrow ask      precise but vague    targeted, usable
broad ask       generic overload     detailed, unfocused
```

- Goals about how you learn improve later learning too.
- Examples: asking better questions, a technique, a study habit.
- On new or complex work, learning goals beat result goals.

## Writing the request

A good request covers context, goal, self-assessment, questions and follow-up. The self-assessment is the longest part, and it is the part people most often skip. It tells the reviewer what you have already noticed and how well you judge your own work, which can be more useful to them than the work itself.

1. Context: your situation, deadlines, and any constraints like a heavy job.
2. Goal: the skill or process you are trying to build.
3. Self-assessment: what you tried, what went well, what was hard.
4. Questions: one or two, on what matters most.
5. Follow-up: reread the reply and ask about anything unclear.

- In the self-assessment, compare results against what you expected.
- Name where you think your main problem is, even if unsure.
- Nobody improves many things at once, so pick whatever will help most.

## An example

A student preparing for a history exam in three months writes that they reread notes, make summary pages, answer textbook questions and get quizzed by friends. They grasp the big ideas but forget dates, and their overall recall is about 70%, with most of the loss in details. They suspect rereading takes time that questions or flashcards should get. They ask how to hold details without losing the big picture, and whether their own diagnosis is right.

- Plan after the reply: drop rereading, add 30 minutes of daily flashcards.
- Replace summary pages with small mind maps.
- A week later: 30 minutes was too little, and maps too hard.
- Adjusted: 45 minutes, and a mix of notes and small maps.

## Using what comes back

Read the reply slowly, especially where you disagree, and remember that it is about the work. Turn it into a short plan with the most important change first. Then try each change as an experiment and judge the results yourself. On complex problems, go back for more feedback after three or four experiments, and on simple ones weeks of adjusting alone may be enough.

## Sources of feedback

Feedback can come from four places: yourself, peers, a coach or expert, and reference guides or a search. They differ in how far they can be trusted and how fast they answer. Your own judgment answers at once and gets more reliable as your skill grows, and it is the one source that needs nobody else.

| Source | Reliability | Speed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yourself | grows with skill | immediate |
| Peers | varies | fast |
| Coach or expert | high | slow |
| Reference docs, search | good for common questions | immediate |

- Check existing guides before asking a person.
- Self-assessment is the source to build, since it needs nobody.
- Giving feedback to peers trains your own judgment.
- When giving it, be specific and name concrete next steps.
- Start with what works, then what to change.
- Comment on the work itself.
