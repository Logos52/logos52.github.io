# Reverse Goal Setting

Reverse goal setting is a way of planning toward a hard goal by first working out what kind of person would reach it, then closing the gap between that person and yourself. A goal on its own, such as passing an exam or getting a job, comes with no plan beyond "work hard". Rating the skills and habits the goal needs, and your current level in each, turns it into two or three things to work on now.

## Takeaways

- A goal is a means to an outcome you want.
- Keep the outcome fixed and let the goal change.
- Rate the skills and habits the goal needs, out of 10.
- Rate yourself on the same list and find the widest gaps.
- Work on two or three gaps at a time.
- Measure habits and skills, since hours studied mean little.

## Why ordinary goal setting stalls

Most goals come with a vague plan. There are many routes to a hard goal, most of them unclear at the start, so effort goes in without visible progress, and many people quit while on a route that would have worked. The outcome itself is outside anyone's control. What can be controlled is the set of skills and habits that raise the odds of reaching it.

- A goal serves an outcome, a feeling or a better position later.
- A doctor seeking meaningful work may find the job lacks it.
- Then the goal should change and the outcome stays.
- Beyond a year, plans rarely hold, so keep only a direction.
- Plan in detail for six to twelve months out.

## The five steps

Each step uses what the earlier steps found. The worry and self-doubt a hard goal brings are useful here, since they point at the gap between what the goal needs and what you can do now. Steps two and three turn that feeling into numbers.

```
goal needs:  time mgmt 7   learning 8   organising 4
you now:     time mgmt 3   learning 5   organising 4
gap:                   4            3              0
```

1. Set the goal, specific enough to picture and measurable enough to check.
2. Picture someone who would clearly succeed, and list their skills and habits.
3. Rate the level the goal needs for each, then rate yourself.
4. List barriers, and the strengths and resources that can beat them.
5. Pick at most two actions for now.

- Typical attributes: time management, focus, learning speed, stress control.
- Also: resilience in discomfort, sleep, planning, a support network.
- Not every attribute needs a 10, and organising might need only 4.
- Ask people who reached the goal where it was hard.
- Choose gaps where the needed level is highest and the gap widest.
- Adding lower-priority gaps on top slows all of them.

## The barrier map

Step four is a force field analysis, a drawing of what pushes you toward the goal and what holds you back. Draw a line between yourself and the goal, write the barriers on one side, and write your strengths and resources on the other, including people who could help. Then match each barrier to something that can reduce it. Barriers are often outside the goal itself, such as a daily school run that takes up the only free hours, which a partner, a relative or a babysitter might take on some days.

## Measuring progress

Performance goals are checkpoints that show whether you are on track before any final result arrives. Hours studied, pages of notes and number of flashcards measure activity, and people who study many hours can still learn little. Set a 30-day and a 14-day target for each chosen attribute, low enough to hit on a bad month. The 14-day target should look unimpressive, and the first two lines below show targets for a planning habit.

- Now: plans three days a week, follows 20% of it.
- In 30 days: plans four or five days, follows 60%.
- Self-ratings run high, so check them against results.
- Under stress people fall back on old habits.

## Review rhythm

Each part of the plan gets checked on its own rhythm. The action plan is looked at most often and the goal itself least often. The table gives the gap between checks for each part.

| What | How often |
| --- | --- |
| Action plan | every 1 to 2 days |
| Barrier map | every 1 to 2 weeks |
| Current level | every 2 to 4 weeks |
| Goal itself | every 1 to 2 months |

The skills built this way stay useful if the goal changes or is missed, because most hard goals need similar skills.
