# Marginal Gains

Marginal gains is the practice of improving a skill by about 1% at a time and making sure each small improvement builds on the last. It replaces the search for one big breakthrough with a steady run of small, tracked changes. For a learner it settles two practical questions: how many things to work on at once, and which one to work on next.

## Core takeaways

- Small gains that build on each other compound over time.
- A gain only compounds if it changes what you can do.
- Work on two or three skills at a time, no more.
- Work next on whatever currently limits everything else.
- Track progress, because small gains are too slow to feel.
- Early progress is often just finding mistakes.

## How it works

A 1% gain compounds when the next gain starts from the improved level. Improving 1% a week for a year ends at about 1.7 times the starting level, and improving 1% a day for a year ends at about 38 times. A 1% loss compounded the same way leaves almost nothing. These figures are arithmetic that shows the direction, and nobody measures study skill to the nearest 1%.

- Positive gains: each builds on the last, and progress climbs.
- Fluctuating gains: changes do not build, so effort brings no progress.
- Negative gains: each builds on the last in the wrong direction.

Fluctuating gains come from applying changes inconsistently. Negative gains come from poor feedback, where a change feels like an improvement and is not one. A quick test separates a lasting gain from a borrowed one: imagine removing it, and if you would be no less capable, the gain was borrowed. A tutor or extra study hours lift results while present and leave you where you were when taken away. A better recall technique stays with you and helps every subject at once.

## How to do it

Pick the two or three skills that matter most and stay with them long enough to find and fix your mistakes. Switching between skills every few days feels busy and produces little, because none of them gets enough practice and reflection. Each round of improvement follows the same steps: reflect on what the last attempt showed, choose a direction, define what a 1% gain in that direction looks like, and make a specific plan to try it.

- Keep a written log of each change you try.
- Set a win criterion so you can tell +1% from -1%.
- Review the log once a week.
- Early gains show up as mistakes found before results improve.
- Stay with a core skill until its mistakes are found and fixed.

## Choosing what to work on

A rate limiter is the part of your process that caps every other part. Heavy procrastination can cap a strong learner's whole output, however good the rest of the process is. The rate limiter changes as you improve, so it needs checking every week or two. Picking the wrong one costs less than never asking.

- Ask what is holding back everything else right now.
- Guessing the wrong limiter still helps more than not asking.
- When progress stalls, look for a new limiter.

## Pacing

Not every detail needs to be learned on the first pass. A detail that is hard to hold today often becomes easy once more of the surrounding structure is in place. A good learning system has several passes, and each one catches some of what the earlier ones missed. Chasing every detail early from fear of missing it tends to produce isolated facts that are then forgotten.

- Take in what the current structure can hold.
- Leave overly detailed points for a later pass.
- Later retrieval sessions catch most gaps from earlier ones.

## Limits

The idea is borrowed from sport and from manufacturing, where the rate limiter is called a constraint. No controlled trial has tested marginal gains as a named learning method, and the size of its advantage has not been measured.
