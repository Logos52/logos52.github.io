# Marginal Gains

Marginal gains are a way of seeing progress as a series of small improvements, sometimes so small they barely show. The point of that view is practical. Small steps are easier to hit, so they produce more visible progress, which can raise the wish to keep going. They also make it easier to pick the next action that is likely to move you toward a larger goal. Time spent that way fails less often, because the step is small, low-risk, and aimed at the goal that actually matters.

Larger leaps have the opposite shape. They involve more variables and a thinner margin for error, so wasted time is more likely. They also come around less often, which means mistakes show up less often. Planned in weeks or months, those leaps can be many times slower than small steps planned daily or weekly. One comparison in the notes says the difference can be as much as twenty times, so that a year's growth for one person is a few weeks for another. Larger leaps also raise the chance of hitting a goal that does not serve the larger aim. Someone can spend weeks fitting a notes app into a workflow, then find that the method they later need cannot be done in that app.

The next small step should also stack, meaning it adds value on top of progress already made. For stacking to develop a skill well, three conditions have to line up. The skill has to matter for what you can do. You have to stay with it long enough to find mistakes, work through them, and become able to do it out of habit. You also have to treat performance as several processes working together, so the best next step changes as you do. When those conditions line up, later attempts can build on earlier ones, and the small improvements compound. New habits also form slowly enough that later techniques do not demand a burst of effort and concentration each time.

Stacking fails when the next step does not build on the last one, or when attention jumps from skill to skill. A learner trying to spend less time on notes might first practice turning difficult ideas into fewer keywords. A follow-up that only targets delay does not strengthen that keyword work. A first look that picks out the big ideas does, because it helps the same kind of thinking the keyword work needs. Staying with a core skill after mistakes appear is also stacking, because finding those mistakes is already progress, and accuracy, consistency, and speed come after the skill can be done correctly. Jumping from notes to cards to delay to scheduling, after only one or two tries each, leaves no time to make mistakes, look at them, and experiment. Weeks or months can pass with little to show.

A four-step loop called REDO is one way to pick the next stacked step. Reflect asks what the last attempts taught and what the next small step might look like. Evaluate chooses a direction, whether to unblock the work by turning to another skill, or to stay with the current one through more experiments and thought. Define says what a one-percent improvement in that direction would look like, remembering that early progress is often just finding mistakes, and how long that might take. Optimise makes a specific plan, tries it, and returns to Reflect. Each try may not show an improvement. A trend toward clearer awareness, and later toward better performance, should still appear.

One worked case starts after a learner has already gotten faster at generating ideas for an essay. Reflection shows that presenting and organizing those ideas is still weak, and that the writer is not yet aware of the planning mistakes being made. Evaluation puts organization ahead of speed. The defined small step is to become more aware of current habits and mistakes, expected to take many hours over a week to find the main ones, then another week or two to try repairs. The trial uses a structure named PEEL, meaning point, evidence, explanation, and link. New mistakes appear, which is a sign that awareness of the skill is growing, and the loop starts again.

Tracking those steps starts farther out. Anchor a meaningful, difficult goal between nine months and three years away. Take the goal apart into the knowledge, attributes, skills, processes, or resources it needs, including mental health, focus, time management, and studying skill, and ask how sure you are about that list. For each factor, give the level you need as a score out of ten points and say in plain words what that score means. Then score where you are now, out of the same ten points, and justify it as plainly as you can. The next small step is whatever would move you about one percent closer to the target level. Gains usually run from knowledge, to action, to habits or to a stronger position, meaning more control over reaching the goal and more ability to reduce risks. On later reviews, the last two steps are often enough, because the goal and its parts stay similar.

When any of those answers is unknown, getting more information is itself the next small step. You cannot act before you know what action to take. Early on, the list does not have to be complete. The factors that clearly matter for the next step are enough, and the picture gets clearer after each one. If you already know enough to try something and make mistakes, what follows is typically to experiment and look at what happened. If you do not yet know enough to try, what follows is typically to study further so a first attempt becomes possible. Do not try to improve more than two or three skills or attributes at the same time. One part of the guidance says that more than two skills at once causes overload. A later worked example treats more than three skills at once as the point where overwhelm starts.
