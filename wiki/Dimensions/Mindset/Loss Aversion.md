# Loss Aversion

Loss aversion is preferring not to lose what you have over gaining something new. In choosing a study method, loss aversion works as a bias toward the method you already use. Calling that method good enough is a decision to keep it. That call is the good-enough verdict. Three questions test whether the verdict measured the method. Three hidden risks show what keeping the method costs.

People who settled on the good-enough verdict have later said they regret it, months or years on. Those regret reports come from practitioners. No follow-up study measured them.

## Core takeaways

- Calling the current study method good enough is a decision to keep the method. It is not a measurement of the method.
- The verdict is usually formed by comparing yourself to the people in view. A more reliable measure is a bar you write yourself.
- A method that depends on effort has nothing in reserve for a challenge harder than estimated. The target is a process that still delivers when effort drops.
- The hidden risk is the risk of not changing. Refusing a new method removes the chance that it works, and that loss is certain.
- The weeks of learning a new method are paid up front. The return arrives later. The bias counts the weeks and ignores the return.
- If the three questions come back clean twice, the verdict is an assessment. Keep the method and do the work.

## Comparing yourself to the people in view

The verdict is formed first against whoever is immediately visible. Outperforming the people in the room feels like enough. Beating the people in the room misleads when the real competition sits outside that room.

The comparison set also narrows by self-selection. Early on, the people around you want a spread of different things, so beating them means little. Later, everyone still in view is there because they wanted what you want. The local group comes to match the real competition at the point where the stakes rise.

The first question: am I comparing myself to others, or to my standards? Write the bar yourself, make it real, and score against that bar. Using other people as the measure of your own work is unreliable.

## What is good enough now and what is needed later

The comparison above was against other people. The next comparison is against time. What is good enough now may not be good enough later. The method does not fail gradually. It holds, and then one day the challenge is above it. At that point you have only what you already built. There is no interval in which to notice.

"Just try harder" fails under a ranked measure. The effort you add is also added by everyone else, and the gap between you and them does not close. Selective difficulty is calibrated so ordinary effort finds it hard. Ordinary effort applied harder lands where the difficulty was designed to put it. Against an absolute standard, more effort does work. More effort on the old method does not turn it into a different method. If the field moved to a new method, the answer is to take up the new method, and to be bad at it for a while.

Methods that depend heavily on effort and motivation leave no room for error or growth. The target is a process that would still deliver if effort dropped. Aiming at that target improves the process instead of adding effort. It also keeps effort in reserve for the challenge that turns out harder than estimated. [[Compounding vs Additive Gains]] describes what the old process keeps buying: hours added rather than capability built. [[How to shift your brain to be motivated (when you don't feel like it)|Motivation]] describes the handoff from effort to habit. An effort-dependent method never makes that handoff.

The second question: am I preparing for future challenges?

## What loss aversion means here

Loss aversion in the field sense covers any preference for keeping what you have over gaining something new. In the choice of a study method, the working bias is narrower: a bias toward "my current process is fine." The field names that protection of a process already in hand status-quo bias and the endowment effect. That protection is what loss aversion does in a study-method choice. It is not a special multiplier on losses. The size of loss aversion is contested. The bias toward the current process does not depend on that size.

The third question: am I avoiding tomorrow's gain out of fear of losing today?

## The risks you see and the risks you do not

The risks you perceive are rarely all the risks. The hidden risk is usually the risk of not changing. Refusing a method because it might not work removes the chance that it does work. That removed chance is the real loss in the decision. The removed chance is certain. The risk being avoided is only possible.

People give three statements for staying with the current method. Each statement hides a chance that staying removes.

"I have no hours to practise a new method" hides the chance that the current method is the reason you are behind.

"What I do already works" hides the chance of missing later openings, and of arriving at a harder challenge unequipped.

"I might learn the new skill badly" hides the chance of never learning it, because the experiment never happens.

Taking the short-term gain removes the chance to build the skill. That chance does not come back later. The marginal hour spent on the next assessment is the same hour the skill would have come from. There is no other supply of hours. So each round removes the capacity that would have made the next round cheaper.

## Counting the cost and the return

Weeks spent learning a method are a loss only if there is no return. An honest count prices both sides. The weeks are real and paid up front. The return is real and arrives later. The bias counts the weeks and ignores the return.

When the decision is live, your first feeling favours the option where nothing is lost. That feeling is not neutral information about which option is better. It systematically misses a gain that was never priced. A decision made from fear and avoidance is a decision that feeling made and reasoning did not. [[Confidence Calibration]] develops that split between feeling-made and reasoning-made decisions. [[Changing Decisions]] covers when changing a decision is the right move rather than a cost to avoid. Review the choice afterward by [[wiki/Decision Making/Judging a Decision by Its Process|judging a decision by its process]], and set aside whether the new method felt costly in week one.

## Putting the work off

Saying the work will happen later is the same decision to keep the current method, in a different form. The work set for later does not happen. After that, the reason given is that what you have is fine. Postponing the work is the decision to keep the current method, already made.

## When the verdict is right

The good-enough verdict can be tested with the three questions and the three hidden risks. The feeling of loss aversion does not go away. What you can do is be accurate about what is actually at risk. Sometimes the verdict is correct. A method that is adequate to a stable challenge does not need replacing. Switching costs are real and paid up front. The three questions come back clean when you score against your own standard, when you are prepared for the harder version, and when you are taking a gain instead of defending what you have. If they come back clean twice, the verdict is an assessment. Stop diagnosing and go do the work. [[Marginal Gains]] is the mindset that loss aversion blocks: small upgrades that compound.

## How to practice this

1. Write your own bar for the study method and score the method against it. Notice whether the score differs from how you rank against the people in view.
2. Ask whether the method is preparing you for a harder challenge than the current one. Notice whether the method would still deliver if your effort dropped.
3. Ask whether you are avoiding tomorrow's gain out of fear of losing today. Notice whether the option you prefer is the one where nothing is lost.
4. Write each reason you give for staying, then write the chance it hides. Notice that the hidden chance is lost for certain, and the risk avoided is only possible.
5. Write down the weeks a new method would cost and the return it would bring later. Notice whether your first estimate counted the weeks and left out the return.
6. Repeat the three questions later. If they come back clean twice, keep the method and do the work.
