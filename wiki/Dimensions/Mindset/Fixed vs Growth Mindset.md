# Fixed vs Growth Mindset

A fixed mindset treats ability and intelligence as things that cannot change. A growth mindset treats the present level as a starting point. The difference is not a slogan. It shows up in what a person will say about a hard task, in what they protect, and in which challenges they will take on.

The fixed version sounds like a closed door. The person talks as if they simply cannot do the work, as if their brain has only one way to learn, or as if they are simply not smart enough. The growth version talks about the present instead. They may not know how yet. They can try, they did not do well this time, they could put in more work, or they still lack the skill a better result would take.

That difference reaches identity. A fixed stance often lines a person up as better than someone else or worse than someone else, and the feeling that follows depends on that comparison. Worth gets tied to being right, to having the answers, and to a high or low result. A growth stance is less interested in that ranking. The person looks for questions, takes pride in trying, and treats the result as secondary to the skill and the growth that produced it.

The behavior follows. Someone protecting a fixed picture of themselves avoids challenges they are not already confident about. Being wrong feels like an attack on who they are, so they stay with easier work even when there is nothing left to learn there. The early result can look strong, because the work was still easy. Later, when the work gets hard, the missing skills show. That pattern is peaking early.

Someone working from a growth stance looks for the hard problem because the hard problem is where learning sits. Being wrong is useful information. They do not need to prove themselves to other people, or even to themselves. Worth is tied to trying, to how they answer a challenge, and to living as the kind of person who gets back up. Over time those choices build the skills the harder work will ask for.

One common source of the fixed stance is early praise. Children in one comparison took the same test, after which one group heard praise for the mark and the other heard praise for trying their best. The first group learned that a good result is what earns approval, and they later treated anything that might lower that result as a danger. After a poor score they were much more likely to lie about it. The second group learned that trying is what earns approval. Early schoolwork is often easy, so a child praised for the mark can build an identity around high results before the work has a chance to get hard.

Three moves help shift the stance. The first is to act. Waiting until you feel like acting is a mistaken requirement. Action can come first. The second is to look back at mistakes in a way that turns them into learning. The third is to look at the chain that produces a result, so effort goes where it is actually blocked.

That chain runs backward from the result. Outcomes come from skills and processes. Those include encoding and retrieval, managing time, looking back at what happened, holding attention, and regulating emotion. The skills themselves come from attributes and the behavior that goes with them, such as openness, a willingness to try things and to sit with discomfort, diligence, patience, humility, and self-compassion. Behind those attributes sit mindsets, beliefs, and identities. Fear of failure belongs there, along with ideas about intelligence and talent. So does the question of whether someone sees themselves as a learner, as someone who tries, or as someone who gets back up. Wanting a result does not produce it. Finding the weak link in that chain is what makes the next effort specific.

The pull described on the loss-aversion page sits underneath a great deal of this. Protecting what you already have, including a picture of yourself as already capable, can matter more than the chance to grow. The pattern is often hidden in school and early university, and it becomes costly later, once a life has already changed direction. Most people can change it once they can see it. It can also appear in some parts of life even when it does not run the whole of a person's thinking.

## Open questions

- How does treating a result as a verdict on ability change which challenges a person will take on?
- How does praising the mark, rather than the effort, change what a learner later tries to protect?
