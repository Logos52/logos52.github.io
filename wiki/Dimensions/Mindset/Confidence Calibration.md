# Confidence Calibration

Confidence calibration is how closely a person's confidence in their knowledge or skill matches how good they actually are. People are usually more confident than their results justify, most of all when they know a little about a subject. A learner who checks their confidence against evidence changes their methods sooner and avoids decisions built on knowledge they do not have.

## Core takeaways

- Most people rate themselves above average at most things.
- Confidence often peaks early, after only a little learning.
- High confidence without years of study is usually overconfidence.
- The feeling will not go away, and it need not steer behaviour.
- Harder tasks produce more overconfidence.
- Knowing that people overrate themselves helps reduce it.
- A wider claim can be held with more confidence.

## The pattern

In a 1999 study by Justin Kruger and David Dunning, people who scored in the bottom quarter on tests of logic and grammar placed themselves, on average, above the middle. Similar results turn up elsewhere: most drivers rate themselves as better than average, and in one survey about 94% of university teachers rated their own teaching above average. A common picture of this has confidence rising quickly with a little knowledge, falling as the learner discovers how much they do not know, and then climbing slowly over years of study.

```
confidence
  |  *
  | * *                         *
  |*   *                  *
  |     *           *
  |       *    *
  +----------------------------- knowledge
  beginner   aware of gaps     expert
```

- The early peak comes from fast gains from a start at zero.
- The dip comes once the size of the subject is visible.
- High confidence fits only the early peak or real expertise.

## How much of it is real

Researchers still argue about the effect. One line of work says much of the pattern is a statistical artefact that appears whenever scores are compared this way. A later re-analysis still found that low performers update their self-rating less when given evidence. The strength of the effect depends on the task, and overconfidence shows up more consistently for skills than for factual knowledge.

- Harder, more complex tasks produce more overconfidence.
- Very easy tasks can make skilled people underrate themselves.
- Expertise and general self-esteem both change how far off a self-rating is.
- Teaching people about the effect has reduced it in some studies.

## How to check it

Overconfidence cannot be felt from the inside, so it has to be checked with questions. The learner asks whether they feel very confident and whether they have spent years studying the subject closely. A yes to the first and a no to the second means the confidence is probably too high. The feeling will stay, and the useful skill is to let reasoning decide what to do next while it does.

- Ask whether the confidence feels very high.
- Ask whether years went into studying the subject closely.
- Yes then no: treat the confidence as likely too high.
- Beginners who did well with old study methods resist changing them most.
- Stay open to changing a method that feels proven.

## Scope and confidence

Confidence also depends on how broad the claim is. A precise claim about a complex situation is hard to be sure of, so it is hard to be sure a new sales page will turn exactly 5% of visitors into buyers, and much easier to be sure the figure will fall between 2% and 7%. Two mistakes come from ignoring this: a narrow answer given as if it were certain, and a search for a certainty the situation cannot give.

- A narrow answer ignores the factors that make it uncertain.
- More testing cannot supply a certainty the situation does not hold.
- Aim for an accurate claim, widened until it can be held.
- Plan for every outcome inside the range.
- Narrow the claim as more evidence comes in.
