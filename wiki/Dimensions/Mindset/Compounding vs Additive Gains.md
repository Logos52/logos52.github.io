# Compounding vs Additive Gains

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

A gain compounds when it changes the learner, so each later gain builds on a higher starting point. A gain that props up results without changing the learner only adds. The test is simple: if the support were removed, would the person be any different? Tutoring and extra study hours usually fail that test, because they lift performance only while they last. Learning skills pass it, and because a skill such as recalling details applies to every subject at once, a small skill gain spreads across all of them. A 1% daily gain compounded over a year comes to about 37 times the start, against about 4.65 times if the same 1% were simply added each day. A compounding strategy starts slowly and then pulls ahead by multiples.

## Core takeaways

- A gain compounds only if the learner's own state is different afterward.
- Ask whether removing a support would leave the learner where they started. If so, the gain was additive.
- Tutoring and more hours are additive and can backfire.
- Skill gains carry across every subject, while subject help stays in its subject.
- A technique that is known but not used daily has added nothing.

## The arithmetic with money

Compound interest shows the difference. An account at 1% grows from $100 to $101, then to $102.01, and the gap over simple growth widens at every step because each gain is calculated on the new balance. A 1% gain each day for a year compounds to roughly 37 times the starting amount, a gain of about 3,600%. Adding the same 1% in a straight line gives a gain of 365%.

## Support you buy

Some help raises results without raising the learner. Tutoring lends resources, guidance and teaching, so a student performs above their real level, like a level-one game character wearing level-six gear. Take the tutor away and performance drops back. As content gets harder the student leans on the tutor more, until the student is the bottleneck. Heavy tutoring, especially at a young age, has been linked to worse later performance, and top students receive less tutoring than others. Extra study hours work the same way up to a point, then turn harmful once stress climbs and the learner gives up sleep, friends and hobbies.

## In learning, the balance is you

In the money example the balance is the account. In learning the balance is the learner. A gain only compounds if the learner is more capable afterward, which is why the removal test asks whether the learner would be different without the support. A gain that leaves the learner unchanged has to be bought again every time it is needed.

## What a skill gain raises

A skill gain raises every subject at once. A grade comes from a set of skills. They include exam technique, precise recall, understanding of concepts, solving both routine and unexpected problems, and working fast under time pressure. Managing anxiety, time and procrastination belongs on the list too. A 1% improvement in recall or in conceptual understanding, with no extra study time, helps every subject, so across five subjects it works like a 5% gain. The technique is shared even when content differs, much as the same painting technique works with blue paint or yellow. Science tutoring does nothing for maths, but a better learning skill helps both.

Aiming at the skills also keeps the focus on process. Wanting a top grade changes nothing by itself, and the grade only reads out the skills underneath, the way a speedometer reads speed.

## Tools that do the work and tools that train you

Help comes in two kinds. Some help does the work for the learner. A tutor's explanations and extra hours of coverage are examples, and their effect ends when the help ends. Other help trains the learner, as practicing a learning skill does, and its effect stays. Only the second kind compounds. The first kind is still useful, but it makes the learner more reliant over time, not more capable.

## Techniques you have not practiced yet

Knowing about a technique is worth nothing until it is used. Familiarity gives a false sense of security, and every technique has nuances that only show up in practice, since people do not know what they do not know. A technique not applied daily should be treated as unknown.

Not every small gain compounds upward either. A 1% change can be positive, fluctuating or negative. Fluctuating gains come from applying improvements inconsistently, so nothing builds and the learner burns out. Negative gains compound in the wrong direction when the learner lacks reliable feedback on whether each change helps.

## How to practice this

1. List the subjects to improve and the skills each one needs.
2. Pick one skill and define a concrete 1% gain in it.
3. Apply the removal test to every tutor, tool or extra hour in use.
4. Aim to be 1% better each week than the week before, and check with real feedback that the change is helping.
