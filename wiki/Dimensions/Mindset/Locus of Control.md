# Locus of Control: The Golden Rule of Learning

An exam score, a place on a course, and a job offer cannot be chosen directly. Each is produced by months of process. The process has named parts: the techniques used, the effort spent, the focus trained, how much gets procrastinated, and how easily distraction wins. That cluster, the things that can be controlled to get the outcome, is the locus of control. The Golden Rule of Learning is to spend attention ruthlessly inside it.

The rule contradicts the simplest model of studying. That model says study more and you will do well, and want it harder and it will come. Wanting does not change a result's probability. The locus of control here is a set of acts. It is not a personality score. Psychology uses the same term for a trait measured on internal-and-external scales. That trait is a different object, and the two stay separate.

## Core takeaways

- Results such as an exam score, a place on a course, or a job offer cannot be chosen. They are produced by months of process.
- The locus of control is the cluster of acts that produce the result: techniques, effort, focus, procrastination, distraction. The Golden Rule of Learning is to spend attention inside that cluster and nowhere else.
- Wanting a result harder does not change its probability. Worrying about it does not either.
- Hours of study are a requirement, and they do not decide the grade. Once study quality and prior achievement are accounted for, study time is a weak predictor of grades.
- The rule raises the probability of the result. It does not deliver the result, and the probability stays below 100%.
- The cheapest move inside the locus is to stop a harmful habit. Stopping takes only a decision. Starting a new skill takes an ability the learner does not yet have, so the stop is the more controllable move.

## What the rule asks you to accept

The rule asks for an acceptance first. The acceptance costs the feeling of certainty about the things that matter most. The cost is felt before it is reasoned, and it sounds scary. It is true anyway, because certainty was never available. The test of this is cheap: if worry could produce results, the more you worried, the more you would win. The evidence is usually already on hand: something once wanted badly, worried over thoroughly, and not gotten anyway.

What the acceptance gives back is the attention itself. Steering at the result directly has a recognisable shape: replaying worst cases, watching the competition, refreshing a page that cannot change. While this runs, the goal stays as distant and as risky as it was. From outside it looks like busyness, anxiety, and random switching between methods while the goal stands still. Time goes on working out how to reach the goal, all sorts of different things get tried, and the time and effort are wasted. The same attention spent inside the cluster is more effective. It is also calmer, because the stress drops as the switching drops.

## Sorting inside from outside

The sort that runs the rule has two bins, and the rule only works if each item goes into the right bin. Outside sit the outcomes: grades, exam results, admission decisions, the dream role. Many factors feed each one, and several of those factors are beyond any action available. An outcome is a probability that can be influenced. It cannot be set directly. Inside sit the processes: which techniques get selected, how focus is built, what is done about distraction, when the studying starts, and how it gets reviewed. Each is a concrete behaviour that can be changed this week.

Effort is inside too, and it does not decide the result. Volume of work is a baseline requirement. Heavy study with weak techniques loses to heavy study with strong ones. Once quality of study and prior achievement are accounted for, sheer study time is a weak predictor of grades. The type of work decides the result. The hours are a requirement. At the same effort, attention moved to the controllable side produces progress out of proportion to the hours. The chain from mindsets through behaviours and processes to outcomes is drawn on [[Fixed vs Growth Mindset]]. That chain is the reason the process end is where change has the most effect. [[Reverse Goal Setting]] turns the same indirect-control logic into a planning method: it works backwards from an outcome that cannot be set directly.

## What the rule delivers and what it does not

Run properly, the rule delivers position. The position is the strongest available, and the outcome is markedly more probable than it was. The probability still stays below certainty. It probably will not reach 100%, and that is as good as it gets. The remainder is ambient uncertainty to live with. Worry spent on it gains nothing and costs focus. The falsifiable promise concerns position. What improves is the probability of the result. A version of this rule that promised the result itself would be false. The rule is the root of the [[wiki/Dimensions/Mindset|Mindset]] dimension, and the rest of that dimension works inside it.

## The test that runs the rule

The rule runs live through one test. Catch attention fixed on something: a result, a rival, a what-if. Then name the action the fixating has produced. If there is none, the thing is outside the locus. Drop it and return to process. The test costs an interruption every time it fires, and the interruption is the price of moving the attention. A related test looks backward: [[wiki/Decision Making/Judging a Decision by Its Process|judging a decision by its process]]. This rule allocates attention before the result. That test judges the choice after the result, and it does not let the outcome change the verdict.

## Stop a harmful habit before adding a technique

The usual self-improvement instinct is to add something. The cheapest move inside the locus is to remove something. Stopping a harmful habit takes a decision. Starting a new skill takes an ability that does not exist yet. So the stop is more controllable than the start, and that asymmetry is the whole argument. Ask a hundred people how they study and a hundred methods come back. Each carries years of accumulated habits, some helpful and some damaging without being noticed. A hundred answers does not mean a hundred equally effective methods. Cutting one damaging habit can improve results more than adding a new technique. This is a consistent observation. It has not been tested in a comparative trial. Cutting a habit requires learning nothing: find what current behaviour does harm, and stop that first. The tool for this pass is a [[Skills Audit]], which shows what current behaviour is already doing. Once the audit finds the habit, [[wiki/Concepts/How to Unlearn Old or Bad Habits Efficiently|How to Unlearn Old or Bad Habits Efficiently]] is how it gets stopped.

## Why the rule comes before every technique

The score, the place, and the offer cannot be changed by attention aimed at them directly. Everything that moves them is in the cluster of acts. That is why the rule is stated before any technique. Every technique is spent inside the locus. A technique aimed at the outcome reaches nothing. Spend the attention where the acts are. The results follow from the acts.

## How to practice this

1. Write down one result you want, such as an exam score. List the acts that produce it: techniques, effort, focus, procrastination, distraction. Notice that the result itself is not on the list of things you can change.
2. Sort each item you listed as an outcome or a process. Grades, exam results, and admission decisions are outcomes; technique choice, focus, and review are processes. Notice which outcomes you had been treating as things you could set directly.
3. Catch your attention fixed on a result, a rival, or a what-if. Name the action the fixating has produced; if there is none, drop the thing and return to process. Notice that each catch costs an interruption, and the interruption is the price of moving the attention.
4. Recall something you once wanted badly, worried over thoroughly, and did not get. Notice that the worrying did not raise the chance of getting it.
5. Before adding a new technique, list what your current study behaviour already does. Find one habit that does harm and stop it first. Notice that stopping it took a decision and no new ability.
