# Neuroticism

Neuroticism is often linked with a fixed mindset and with fear of failure. In one widely used personality framework, it is one of five core traits. Some of it is useful. It can keep you safe, set a high bar, and supply motivation, which is why it shows up often among people who achieve a great deal. Too much of it is associated with serious mental-health trouble, including anxiety and depression, and that pairing is offered as one reason those problems are especially common among high-performing people.

Five named stages are laid out for working with it. They are prevention, planning, execution, measurement, and management. The notes used for this page cover the last of those stages, plus a separate method called chain analysis. The earlier stages are named and not described here.

Managing neuroticism means training the brain not to fixate on certain thoughts and feelings. That training is meant to make attention steadier, reduce how easily it is pulled away, and make mood more stable. The work is less about forcing the mind to stay on one thing, and more about stopping thoughts and feelings from taking over. Focus, in this sense, is treated as a skill you can train.

The training itself is conceptually simple. Put yourself in a situation where attention drifts, which often happens when there are no outside distractions, including in the quiet before sleep. Then practice letting the stray thought go and bringing attention back. A session looks like this. Sit somewhere comfortable and away from distractions, and close your eyes. Breathe slowly. Rest attention on the feeling of each breath, including the air and how the body moves. After a few seconds other thoughts will arrive. Each time they do, notice the thought, let it pass without chasing it, and bring attention gently back to the breath. Repeat for 15 to 20 minutes. It is normal for attention to drift every three or four seconds. The point of the session is that wandering, and the practice of returning. Trying to stop the thoughts on purpose works against the method. Let them pass, the way weather passes overhead, without holding them or reaching for them.

Over time the gaps between wanderings get longer. Even after years of practice, no more than 10 seconds of quiet in the mind is described as normal, and that stretch is treated as about three times the starting ability. The dose generally said to produce a clear change is 20 minutes a day for 30 days. Effects have also been seen with 10 minutes a day. Consistency matters more than a heroic session. If you have a history of trauma, this kind of training, which is identified here as a form of mindfulness meditation, can bring traumatic memories back. In that case the recommendation is to do it with a therapist or psychologist, most of whom will already know how to guide it.

Chain analysis is a separate method for finding what caused a response or a feeling. One simple version used by psychologists has six parts. First, name the specific behaviour you want to change, such as delaying work, overeating, spending too long on a feed, or snapping at someone. Second, name the prompting event that started the chain, as specifically as you can. Third, name what made you vulnerable to that event on that day, including mood, earlier events, or habits. Fourth, list the links between the event and the behaviour. That includes what you did, how you felt at each point, and what you thought. Fifth, name the short-term and long-term consequences. Sixth, look for solutions in four directions. Exposure asks how to see less of the prompting event, or of the vulnerability factors if the event itself cannot be avoided. Skills asks what you can learn or use to change the event, the reaction, or the consequences. Cognitive modifications ask how to change the way you see the event so it lands with less force, and how you will remember that view next time. Contingency management asks what to do if the earlier steps fail, so the damage is smaller and the next attempt is clearer.

A change in how you see an event can reduce its impact even before you have every skill for handling it directly. Anxiety, for example, often gets worse when you watch yourself trying not to be anxious, which can spiral toward panic. One modification is to notice the anxiety, treat it as the brain trying to keep you safe, and remind it that there is no real danger in that moment. Whether to run a chain analysis is left to your own judgment. It is not assigned to everyone, because not everyone needs it.

## Open questions

- How does treating a wandering thought as something to notice and release, rather than as a failure of focus, change what a session of this training becomes?
- How does naming the links between a prompting event and a later behaviour change what someone is able to try next time?
