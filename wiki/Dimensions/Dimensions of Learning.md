# Dimensions of Learning

> Every learner relies on five trainable dimensions: mindset, self-management, self-regulation, deep processing, and retrieval. Each dimension answers a different question about how you study, and a weakness in any one caps what the others can deliver. Diagnose the limiting dimension first, train it with deliberate practice, and only then stack more techniques. Alongside that map sit three knowledge jobs (knowing what something is, running it automatically, and judging when to use it) and a practical rule that multimodal effort beats matching a mythical learning style.

## The five questions

Each dimension is defined by the question it answers, not by a bag of tips.

| Dimension | Question | Job |
|---|---|---|
| [[wiki/Dimensions/Mindset\|Mindset]] | Do my beliefs let me grow through difficulty? | Read challenge and mistakes as usable feedback |
| Self-Management | Do I have systems that make consistent action possible? | Time, tasks, attention, and environment |
| [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation\|Self-Regulation]] | Can I notice what is happening and adjust? | Monitor and steer learning as it runs |
| [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing\|Deep Processing]] | Am I extracting meaning from what I take in? | Connect, analyse, and critique new information |
| [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval\|Retrieval]] | Can I recall and use what I know under pressure? | Keep knowledge accurate and available |

Self-management sets the floor for the rest, because until time and attention are under some control, the other dimensions never get honest practice. Mindset decides whether mistakes become data or verdicts. Self-regulation is the driving skill on top of deep processing’s “engine”: you need both for steady performance when material or conditions change. Retrieval depends on encoding quality and, in turn, forces deeper processing when tests are hard enough.

Typical failures show up clearly in each dimension. A fixed mindset avoids challenge and over-demands certainty before trying, while weak self-management leaves you reactive and dependent on willpower. Weak self-regulation causes performance to collapse once teaching stops hand-holding. Shallow processing leaves isolated facts without a usable structure, and weak retrieval shows up as confident recognition that fails under pressure.

Several pairs are often confused. Self-management builds the systems around learning, whereas self-regulation steers the learning itself and rests on [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation/Metacognition - The Control Layer|metacognition]]. Deep processing sets how much meaning you can pull from a source, while self-regulation decides whether you actually do that work in the moment.

## What holds for everyone, what is individual

The principles apply broadly, but ceilings differ. Deep processing has a genetic and early-life component, though it can be trained. Someone with unusually strong processing may succeed with mediocre methods (**deep processing tanking**), which misleads others who copy the same surface habits. Even strong processors eventually hit goals where tanking fails and the other dimensions matter. Mindset also varies by domain: growth in one subject, fixed in another, is normal.

## Three knowledge jobs under any skill

Every skill hides three separable jobs. Having one does not give you the others.

**Declarative** work is what you can still explain step by step: facts, concepts, relationships, and any move you choose on purpose. **Procedural** work is what runs without conscious step choice (fluency, muscle memory, automated execution). Conditional judgment (knowing when and why to use a move) behaves as declarative knowledge that thickens through varied practice.

This three-way split diagnoses where performance breaks down. A messy problem exposes either the wrong method or order (declarative gap) or the right method done clumsily (procedural gap). Learners often understand an idea before they can execute it, because mastery grows through attempts rather than explanations. Theory first, then an early **fail-fast, fail-safe** attempt: you make roughly five to seven characteristic mistakes out of hundreds of possibilities, and those are what practice should hunt.

Suspect declarative before adding more drills of the same shape. Approach, strategy, and judgment stay expensive even as pure execution gets cheap to automate. After each attempt, run a short loop (what happened, how it felt, what rule follows, and what changes next time) along the lines of [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management/Kolbs Experiential Cycle|Kolb’s cycle]].

## Multimodal study, not learning styles

Fixed learning styles (visual, auditory, kinesthetic labels as identity) do not hold up in research. Preferences remain, but they narrow study when treated as limits. What tracks results more closely is whether the brain is engaged (work that is hard to do while switched off). The practical move is to **mix modes**: read, write, draw, speak, and practise, instead of matching a single channel. Study notes offer a simple place to add an underused mode (sketches, diagrams, or shorthand) without turning study into decoration. Left-brain and right-brain stories fail to rescue the old advice, since multimodal effort helps regardless of the mechanism.

## How techniques map to dimensions

Techniques are repeatable actions that train one or more dimensions once you know which dimension limits your progress.

Encoding techniques (the Bear Hunter System for selective intake, importance-based chunking for grouping by weight, and inquiry-driven reading) build structure as material arrives. Retrieval techniques (spaced interleaved retrieval, revision passes, and whole-part-whole teaching) test memory after encoding, while habit and scheduling methods protect the conditions both need.

Foundational bundles stack in order: question-first intake, nonlinear maps, importance-based grouping, then spaced retrieval layered on top. The skill target is **conscious competence** moving toward cheap success before adding the next method ([[wiki/Concepts/Four Stages of Competence|four stages of competence]]). Piling several high-effort methods at once overloads working memory and feels like stagnation.

## Skills audit

Growth stalls when technique mistakes accumulate unnoticed (after rushing, skipping a method, a long break, or a stage jump). A **skills audit** compares what you actually do against the guidelines for each method, rates honesty from poor to excellent, corrects one or two aspects at a time, and practises with feedback. The stance required is growth mindset: a low score is information, not a verdict. A lighter screen checks recent checkpoints, while a full audit walks every lesson when the screen misses the gap. Repeat every few months, or faster while new methods are landing.

## Using the model

Work on the dimension that currently blocks the others. For many learners the first payoff is self-management plus foundational retrieval habits, whereas retraining deep processing habits takes weeks to months and pays later. Rushing past foundations usually means relearning them under exam pressure.

A simple loop:

1. Ask each dimension’s question and answer honestly.
2. Pick the weakest link that blocks practice, often self-management if time and attention are chaotic.
3. Choose one practice from that dimension and run it for several weeks (30-day challenges mirror this).
4. Reflect, then move to the next limit.
