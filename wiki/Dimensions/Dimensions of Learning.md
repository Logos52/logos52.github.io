# Dimensions of Learning

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Learning ability breaks down into five dimensions: mindset, self-management, self-regulation, deep processing and retrieval. Mindset shapes how a person reacts to challenge and mistakes. Self-management builds the habits and systems that make consistent effort possible. Self-regulation monitors and steers learning as it happens. Deep processing turns information into meaningful knowledge, and retrieval keeps that knowledge accurate and usable. A weakness in any one limits the others, so the useful question is which dimension sets the current limit. Deep processing ability varies between people, which is why copying a top learner's technique often fails. All five can be trained through targeted practice.

## Core takeaways

- The five dimensions are mindset, self-management, self-regulation, deep processing and retrieval.
- Self-management comes first in practice, because without control of time and attention nothing else gets practiced.
- Deep processing is the engine and self-regulation is the driving skill, and consistent performance needs both.
- Some people succeed with mediocre technique because strong deep processing compensates, which does not carry over when someone else copies them.
- Each dimension is trained by building habits through practice, not by instruction alone.

## The five dimensions

Each dimension answers a different question about a learner.

| Dimension | The question | The job |
|---|---|---|
| [[wiki/Dimensions/Mindset\|Mindset]] | Do my beliefs let me grow through difficulty? | Shapes how challenges and mistakes are read |
| [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management\|Self-Management]] | Do I have systems that make consistent action possible? | Habits, routines and environments for time, tasks and attention |
| [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation\|Self-Regulation]] | Can I notice what is happening and adjust? | Monitoring, guiding and refining the learning process |
| [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing\|Deep Processing]] | Am I extracting meaning from what I take in? | Connecting, analyzing and critiquing new information |
| [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval\|Retrieval]] | Can I recall and use what I know? | Keeping knowledge accurate and usable under pressure |

## What each dimension does, and what happens when it is weak

Each dimension has a typical failure when it is weak.

Mindset is the belief that abilities, intelligence and even identity can grow. A growth mindset reads challenges as chances to learn and mistakes as feedback. A fixed mindset defines a person by past results or current limits, avoids challenge and demands far more certainty than the stakes warrant before trying something new, so improvement is slow.

Self-management covers focus, time, energy, attention, procrastination and priorities. Weak self-management leaves a person trapped by a stream of urgent tasks and running on willpower. Two common objections miss the point. Done well, self-management does not turn a person into a robot or feed toxic productivity, since its purpose is to spend time and attention the way the person intends.

Self-regulation is the ability to monitor, guide and refine one's own learning. Learners with weak self-regulation depend on raw processing ability, and their performance drops when instruction is less guided or conditions are hard.

Deep processing runs from shallow to deep. Deep processing analyzes, connects and critiques, giving strong memory and flexible use. Shallow processing repeats and memorizes without context, so a learner can grasp single concepts and still miss the bigger picture.

Retrieval is recalling and using knowledge, whether stating a fact, explaining, solving a problem or discussing an idea. Since knowledge always decays, weak retrieval means errors and poor performance under pressure.

## Key Distinctions

Several pairs of dimensions are easy to confuse. Deep processing and self-regulation differ the way an engine differs from driving skill. Deep processing sets the engine's power, and self-regulation is how well it is driven, so consistent performance across changing conditions needs both.

Self-management and self-regulation also differ. Self-management builds the systems, habits and environments around learning, while self-regulation steers the learning itself as it happens. Self-regulation rests on metacognition, meaning awareness of one's own thinking, which supplies the awareness that adjustment acts on. Self-regulation is also not about avoiding mistakes. It is the skill of noticing what is happening, such as catching oneself memorizing details instead of understanding concepts, and changing course.

## How the five work together

The dimensions depend on each other. Self-management gates the rest, because until a person controls their time and attention they cannot put real effort into the other dimensions. Learning techniques are useless without the habits to practice, reflect and come back. Mindset governs how mistakes are used, which drives growth in every dimension. Self-regulation steers deep processing and retrieval as they run.

## What holds for everyone, and what is one person's solution

The principles hold for everyone, but natural starting points differ. Deep processing depends heavily on genetics and early childhood, though it can be trained. A person with exceptional deep processing can do well with mediocre methods, which is called deep processing tanking. Learners without that ability who copy the same strategies do not get the same results. Even strong processors eventually meet goals hard enough that tanking fails, so they too need the other dimensions. Mindset also varies by area, since most people hold a growth mindset in some subjects and a fixed one in others.

## Which dimension each technique serves

Each dimension is trained by its own set of practices.

- Mindset: building awareness of limiting beliefs, making many small and cheap mistakes, reflecting on what to change, then repeating until mistakes feel like progress.
- Self-management: realistic schedules with time-blocking, task screening by time, importance and urgency, focus routines and breaking and making habits.
- Self-regulation: reflection that builds metacognition, accepting desirable difficulty and learning the theory of how learning works so adjustments are well aimed.
- Deep processing: scaffolded practice that builds cognitive habits one skill at a time until they become automatic, as with drills in tennis before full matches.
- Retrieval: matching the method to declarative, procedural or conditional knowledge, spacing and interleaving practice and aiming retrieval at gaps.

## Using the model

The model is a diagnostic tool. A learner looks for the dimension that sets the current limit and works there first. For most people the biggest early gains come from the foundational lessons, which deal with the usual barriers in learning and self-management, while retraining deep processing habits takes weeks to months and pays off later. Rushing past the foundations usually means coming back to relearn them.

## How to practice this

1. For each dimension, ask its question and rate the honest answer.
2. Pick the weakest dimension that blocks the others, usually self-management if time and attention are not under control.
3. Choose one practice for that dimension and run it for a few weeks.
4. Reflect on what changed and pick the next limit.

## Declarative, Procedural, and Conditional Knowledge

Under any skill there are three separate jobs. Having one of them does not give you the other two. The first job is knowing what the thing is and what it accomplishes. The second job is running it without spending attention on the running. That is a different achievement, and it comes later. The third job is judging, in a situation you have not met before, that this is the move to use. The psychology terms for the three are declarative, procedural, and conditional.

Keeping the three apart is what makes the labels useful. When a technique you understand completely still fails in performance, the missing job tells you which practice to do next.

## Core takeaways

- Every skill has three separate jobs: knowing what it is, running it automatically, and judging when to use it. Having one does not give you the others.
- This vault counts any step that is still chosen on purpose as declarative. Procedure begins only where conscious control ends.
- This vault treats conditional knowledge, the when and why, as a subset of declarative. It grows as a by-product of practice in varied contexts.
- When an understood technique fails in performance, suspect the declarative side first. The usual block is deciding which method to use and in what order.
- The test is a messy problem. A wrong method or order is a declarative gap. The right method done clumsily is a procedural gap.
- The first job comes from being told. The other two are built by doing. Start attempts early, because each person makes about five to seven of the hundreds of possible mistakes.

## Where each type begins and ends

**Declarative knowledge** is the foundation. It covers facts, concepts, relationships, and anything that can be explained. In this vault it also covers any step still chosen on purpose. Every other type builds on it. Content-heavy subjects are almost entirely declarative. History, biology, and theory of any kind are declarative. Medicine is mixed: the facts and the diagnostic plan are declarative; the practised motor part of a procedure is not.

**Procedural knowledge** means automatic. Driving, native-sounding pronunciation, and fluent code are examples. The execution is so practised that it runs without thinking about the steps. The everyday name for this is muscle memory. It is not "knowing how" in the everyday sense. Taking disease facts, picking a diagnosis, and writing a plan is still choosing, reasoning, and ordering steps on purpose. That whole stretch is declarative. Procedure begins only where conscious control ends. This is where this vault draws the line. The research field does not draw it in the same place.

The automatic kind develops slowly, and only through doing. Once it is in place, the declarative scaffolding underneath it can fade. The usual example is a fluent speaker who cannot state the adjective-order rule. The production runs, and the verbal access is gone. [[wiki/Concepts/Global Workspace and J-space|Global Workspace and J-space]] explains why: the skill has left the deliberate workspace. [[Knowledge Mastery - From Recognition to Usable Knowledge]] grades the declarative side of the same split. The levels stay on that page.

**Conditional knowledge** is knowing when and why to use what is already held. This vault treats it as a subset of declarative, because the when and why can be explained on demand. Scholars do not agree that it is a separate type. Strategy-instruction research treats the when-layer as something to teach explicitly. This vault's stance is different and narrower. Building declarative and procedural knowledge across varied contexts, challenge types, and time pressures produces the when-layer as a by-product. It grows when context is changed on purpose. It does not grow by waiting.

## Theory first, then the attempt

The theory is engaged first, through notes, analogies, connections, and critique. The skill is attempted as soon as there is enough theory to find the first mistakes. The order is working understanding, then the attempt. [[Rapid Skill Acquisition]] sets this pacing. Certainty before the first attempt is impossible. Asking more clarifying questions to reach certainty delays the attempt, and the attempt is what teaches.

**Fail fast, fail safe** means finding the cheap early mistake before the cost is concentrated. A messy session half a year before the deadline is cheap information. Finding the same hole in the last week puts the whole cost into that one week. Small early errors prevent the large late ones.

A skill may offer hundreds of possible mistakes. Any one person makes about five to seven of them. Those five to seven are the ones to look for. Trying to prevent the whole catalogue with what-if questions overwhelms working memory and stalls the start. The start is what reveals the five to seven.

Some tendencies come from another domain and cannot be seen from inside. A habit trained in a different skill carries over and keeps happening. An observer sees it. The person doing it does not.

The loop after an attempt is try, reflect against the declarative model, refine, retry. With a strong model of the skill, a failed attempt gives a specific question to ask. [[Kolbs Experiential Cycle]] is that loop: what happened, how it felt and why, what rule that suggests, what changes next time.

## Understanding without being able to perform

A person can understand a skill completely and still fail to perform it. This gap is the reason the three-way split exists. Understanding is not performance. Knowing what to do is a small fraction of mastery. Most of the work is built through practice. Each job has a different best teacher: explanation for the first, the attempt for the other two. The fraction is a teaching rule, and no study has measured knowing-what as a tenth of mastery.

## Finding which type is missing

Suspect declarative first. Even in subjects that look procedural, the usual block is deciding which method to use, in what order, for this situation. That decision is declarative. A great deal can be solved without procedure if each step can be paused and decided. Pure execution is becoming cheap to automate. The layer of approach, strategy, and judgment stays expensive. This is a reason to check the first job first. It says nothing about the future of labour.

Someone who can finish the usual practice set and then cannot solve the same problem inside a real case is missing a concept. More repetitions of the same set will not supply the concept. [[Interleaving for Complex Problem Solving]] applies this diagnosis. The test is a messy challenge. A wrong method, a wrong order, or a broken chain of reasoning is a declarative gap. The right method done clumsily is a procedural gap: a slipped calculation, a syntax bug, or stiff prose.

Match practice to that diagnosis. Retrieval methods split along the same line. [[Interleaving Table]] holds that split, and the knowledge type diagnosed above is the variable it splits on. [[Spaced Interleaved Retrieval]] is the retrieval system that takes that variable. Use the three types before the next hour of practice or the next test. Reciting them after the practice has no use.

## Where this vault differs from the research

This vault's cut is a useful diagnostic. It is not a consensus definition. A reader who treats the three names as three real, separate things will call application procedure and then add more drills of the same shape. The research field often treats application as a mix of types, and treats the when and why as teachable. Both disagreements are real. The cut is still the tool: if the steps are still being chosen, the hour is declarative.

The split that holds is explanation versus attempt. It is not person versus experience in every domain. A coach can shape procedure through feedback, demonstration, and constraint. Mental practice can move procedure a little. The direction stays the same: the first job arrives from being told, and the other two are built in the doing.

The 10/90 split and the five-to-seven-of-hundreds are this vault's teaching numbers. They are not research findings. Varied practice is the technique for the when-layer. "No dedicated techniques" and "develops for free" are too strong as absolutes. If the next hour is still chosen step by step, and the practice is more drills of the same shape, the diagnosis was skipped. The check is the next messy problem. It exposes either a wrong approach or a right approach done clumsily.

## Sorting the next hour of practice

The three types are used before the next hour of practice. An hour that can still be explained step by step is declarative, even when it looks like doing. An hour that runs without the steps being chosen is procedural. An hour that changes context on purpose is how the when-layer grows. The three names are a tool for sorting practice. Memorizing them as a list is not their use.

## How to practice this

1. Before the next hour of practice, ask whether each step is still chosen on purpose. If it is, count the hour as declarative, even when it looks like doing.
2. Start attempting the skill as soon as you understand enough to find the first mistakes. Notice which mistakes you make. Expect about five to seven of the hundreds possible.
3. Give yourself a messy problem instead of the usual practice set. Notice whether the method or order was wrong, which is a declarative gap. Notice whether the right method was done clumsily, which is a procedural gap.
4. After each attempt, ask what happened, how it felt and why, what rule that suggests, and what changes next time. Notice the failed attempt turning into one specific question.
5. Have an observer watch you. Notice any habit carried in from another skill that you could not see yourself.
6. Change the context, the challenge type, or the time pressure on purpose. Notice whether you judge better when and why to use each move.

## Learning Styles Myth and Multimodal Learning

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Learning styles, the idea that each person is a visual, auditory, reading and writing or hands-on learner, do not exist. Large, long-running studies tested the idea and found it false. People do have learning preferences, meaning they prefer certain kinds of information delivered in certain ways, but almost everything is learned more efficiently through several modes than through one. The factor that tracks learning most closely is how switched on the brain is, whatever the mode. Believing in a fixed style does harm, because it treats the brain as a fixed object. The practical rule is to mix modes on purpose, and especially to add visual work to notes, since schoolwork already leans hard on reading and writing.

## Core takeaways

- There is no such thing as a fixed individual learning style that a person must match.
- What remains after the myth is preference. A preference is not a limit, since every learner can learn in multiple modes.
- Mode matters less than attention. A technique works when it is hard to do with the brain switched off.
- Each added mode makes learning more effective, and notes are the easiest place to add a less used mode such as pictures, symbols and diagrams.
- A popular left-brain, right-brain story does not explain why drawing helps. Mixing modes still helps, whatever the reason.

## What the studies found about matching

The old model was VARK, for visual, auditory, reading and writing, and kinesthetic, the last meaning learning through experience and practice. Many people still describe themselves with one of those labels. When researchers tested the labels, most students came out as reading, writing and visual learners. That pattern reflects schooling, which leans heavily on reading, writing and visuals, and not a fixed trait. Auditory learning looks weak for a different reason. It is easy to zone out while listening and harder to zone out while reading or watching.

## How a preference comes to feel like an identity

What remains after the myth is preference. Some material a person likes to have explained aloud, some they like to read and some they like to see as a video, flowchart or diagram. A preference is not a limit, since every learner can learn in multiple modes.

Belief in a single style undercuts that. It treats the brain as fixed, when the brain can learn well across a very wide range of situations. Having preferences is fine, but the useful move is to use as many modes as possible.

## Attention matters more than the channel

Mode matters less than attention. Reading without paying attention is not effective, and neither is watching a video, drawing a picture, listening or running a chemistry experiment without attention. A technique works when it is hard to do with the brain switched off.

## Two rules to use instead of matching

Use as many modes as possible, rather than matching work to a single label. Prefer techniques that are hard to do with the brain switched off, because attention matters more than the channel.

## Drawing and other ways to re-represent the material

Each added mode makes learning more effective. Reading something and then writing about it teaches a little. Reading it and drawing a picture teaches more, and the picture works better still when it is a bit abstract instead of accurate. Teaching the idea aloud, hearing it explained and practicing it in real life where possible each add more.

Notes are where this is easiest to apply. Reading and writing already fill most study, so notes made only of words use the brain in the way it is already being used. Cutting words and adding pictures, symbols, doodles, shorthand and diagrams brings in a less used mode. Visuals also act as landmarks in memory and make notes more personal. Turning a paragraph into a doodle means reading it critically, working out a different way to represent it and then drawing that, which is far more work than copying it.

The product matters less than the process. Other people may not understand a set of doodles, but their maker has a strong connection to them because of the thinking that went into imagining and drawing them.

## The left-brain, right-brain story and why it was dropped

A popular explanation for why doodles help puts critical thinking in one hemisphere and abstract, artistic thought in the other, so drawing would recruit both halves. Current evidence does not back that account. Different brain regions do have different functions, yet personality has no tie to either hemisphere. A person may favor one side for movement and the senses, but nobody seems to rely on one side for processing information.

## What remains

The evidence supports multimodal teaching without showing that the benefit comes from using both hemispheres. For a learner the mechanism does not change the advice, because mixing modes helps whatever the reason.

## How to practice this

- After reading a paragraph, represent it in a second mode, such as a slightly abstract drawing, a diagram or a short spoken explanation.
- In notes, cut some words and add pictures, symbols, doodles or shorthand, so the work is hard to do with the brain switched off.
- Do not treat a preference for one mode as a limit on how you study.

## Technique Training & Fundamentals

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Technique training and fundamentals are the base study methods that later, harder work is built on. Four bundles make up that base. Inquiry-based learning has you create questions and answer them, so curiosity steers what you read. Mind mapping turns linear material into a picture of relationships that breaks the false sense of fluency ordinary notes give. Importance-based chunking sorts information according to why it matters. The retrieval methods then bring knowledge back at widening intervals and in layered passes, so gaps show up while there is still time to fill them. The target is a level of skill where you use each method correctly without coaching yourself through it, and a new method waits until the current ones reach that level.

Asking before reading is the first bundle. [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing|Inquiry-Based Learning]] means creating problems and then answering them. Each answer raises more questions, and that cycle builds a richer and more usable understanding with stronger retention.

Asking questions only helps if the answers land somewhere, which is where the second bundle comes in. [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing|Mindmaps]] turn linear material into a nonlinear picture of relationships, and an early map should look messy. New knowledge only settles once it joins a schema, a web of related knowledge.

How you group what you map decides how efficient it becomes. [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Importance-Based Chunking|Importance-Based Chunking]] judges each group by why it matters, looking past the first obvious reason to a broader root reason.

Encoding still leaves some decay, so the fourth bundle covers retrieval. [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval/Spaced Interleaved Retrieval|Spaced Interleaved Retrieval]] brings answers back from memory at intervals that keep widening, approaching them from a new angle each time. The [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval|Multipass System]] covers a large topic in layered sweeps, starting with logic and concepts before details. [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval|Revision]] goes back to studied material to locate gaps and close them through true recall, meaning you can use the knowledge with no cue present.

All four bundles serve the five capabilities set out on [[wiki/Dimensions/Dimensions of Learning|Dimensions of Learning]], and the skill target for each one comes from [[wiki/Concepts/Four Stages of Competence|Four Stages of Competence]]. At the stage called conscious competence you can use a method correctly if you apply effort. The goal is to move each method toward the top of that stage, where success is common and cheap, before adding the next one. ## Techniques - Learning Craft

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

A technique here is a concrete method you use to train one of five dimensions of learning, which are self-management, self-regulation, deep processing, mindset and retrieval. The dimensions say what has to get stronger, and the techniques are the repeatable actions that make it stronger. Encoding methods, among them the Bear Hunter System, importance-based chunking and inquiry-based learning, build understanding as you take material in. Retrieval methods such as revision and the multipass system then keep that understanding available, while habit methods such as PEER make the work happen without waiting on motivation. Because a technique still costs effort until it becomes automatic, a new one should wait until the ones you already use are close to that point.

## The Five Dimensions

Each technique trains one or more of the five dimensions of learning:

- [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management|Self-Management]]: setting up habits, a daily rhythm and a working environment that support steady action, so less energy goes into just getting through the day.
- [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation|Self-Regulation]]: monitoring and adjusting your own learning as it happens, so performance holds up when teaching gives less guidance or the going gets hard.
- [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing|Deep Processing]]: how well the brain turns information into meaningful learning, through analysing, linking and critiquing what you take in.
- [[wiki/Dimensions/Mindset|Mindset]]: the filter on how you read experience, where a growth mindset treats abilities as improvable and a fixed mindset treats them as static.
- [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval|Retrieval]]: recalling and using knowledge from memory, practised so that knowledge fades more slowly and holds up under pressure.

- Encoding methods organise new information while you take it in. The [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Bear Hunter System|Bear Hunter System]] has three steps, Aim, Shoot and Skin, which ask questions, answer them to build a map and then refine the grouping. [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Importance-Based Chunking|Importance-Based Chunking]] groups information by why it matters, and [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing|Inquiry-Based Learning]] lets questions you create direct what you read and how you think.
- Retrieval methods slow the decay of what you have already learned. [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval|Revision]] goes back over the material, spotting gaps and closing them with true recall, and the [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval|Multipass System]] works through a big topic in layered passes that put logic and concepts ahead of details.
- Attention and scheduling methods protect the conditions the other techniques need. [[wiki/Self Management/Time Management, Attention & Scheduling|Time Management, Attention & Scheduling]] gathers the pages on deciding which work gets time and keeping attention on it.
- Habit methods make the work happen by default. [[wiki/Self Management/Habits, Productive Routines & PEER|Habits, Productive Routines & PEER]] sets out a five-part setup of Prep, Easy, Exit, Reward and Peer, so motivation is kept for the days that need a push.

## Getting Started Material

A new learning project starts better when new information has somewhere to land. [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing|Prestudy]] is work done ahead of a main learning event, for example a class or lecture, and it builds a rough outline that makes later detail easier to retain.

The [[wiki/Dimensions/30-Day Challenges|30-Day Challenges]] then turn the dimensions into practice, with one four-week track for each trainable part of learning, and each week asks a little more than the last.

## Philosophy

Competence with a technique grows in stages, from doing it correctly only with effort to doing it almost automatically. Several high-effort techniques at once add up to overload and drain motivation, because results that come only now and then feel like a lack of progress. A new technique therefore waits until the ones already in use are approaching unconscious competence, the top of the [[wiki/Concepts/Four Stages of Competence|Four Stages of Competence]].

# Skills Audit

A skills audit is a structured review of your techniques against stated guidelines, so gaps can be found and improved on purpose. Doing that review regularly is how a learning system stays flexible and useful. The audit is first named at the briefing stage, then again at later checkpoints. The same steps can be used before those stages, without waiting to feel ready.

The review is recommended after several kinds of strain. Learning a skill too fast, without enough practice, makes technique mistakes and weak systems more likely. So does skipping a technique because it did not seem relevant. Those mistakes can be hard to trace even for an experienced coach. A long break can also dull a skill through lack of practice, which is called technique decline. Moving into a new stage that builds on earlier skills is another reason to check, as is the feeling of being stuck without knowing why.

The stance that makes the review work is a growth mindset. That means a willingness to notice being wrong rather than assume you are already correct. Come in intending to understand and improve, even in areas that have already taken a lot of time. Rate yourself honestly. A low rating is not a verdict on past effort, it is a sign you want a clear picture. Take the time the review needs, which can run to hours later on, and practice a skill during the review if that is the only way to see it clearly. Repeat the audit at least every two to three months, or more often when new techniques are arriving quickly.

The steps themselves are grouped as LEEP. First, map the flow of how you currently learn, using the checklist from the briefing stage, and check that the flow includes techniques from every stage you have finished. Second, evaluate each technique against the stated guidelines. Those include checkpoint videos, milestone lists, later checklists, the aims at the start of a stage, and end-of-stage quizzes. Look for gaps that came from rushing or from skipped parts. Rate each technique as a score from 1 point, poor, to 5 points, excellent, as plainly as you can. Third, correct the errors by adjusting the technique toward those targets. Practice only one or two aspects at a time so the work does not overload you, and ask for feedback from your own review, from peers, or from scheduled help. Fourth, practice with care, using small-step tracking and a reflection cycle to experiment, because fixing a technique often takes more than one try, especially after rushing or after technique decline.

The same review can be done two ways. A skill screen is faster. It looks at earlier checkpoint videos and the main lessons that introduced new techniques. It rates competence, accuracy, and consistency lesson by lesson as a score from 1 point to 5 points, and then focuses on scores below 4 points. A comprehensive audit is slower. It reviews each lesson, each checkpoint, and each quiz at the end of a stage, from the start. It looks for gaps between what you understand and what you can actually do, and then focuses on scores below 4 points, or below 5 points for foundational techniques. The faster screen is for regular use. The longer audit is for when the screen has not found the gap, or when the problem has been around for a long time.
