# Problem-First Learning

Problem-first learning flips the usual order. The usual order is to learn the material and then try to solve a problem with it. The more useful order is to name the problem you are trying to solve, become curious about it, and only then read the material through that lens. The brain holds what helps it solve a problem, or what fits a pattern. Isolated facts, even facts you intended to remember, are easy to forget. In a subject such as math, that order can produce a clear sense of how the ideas will be used before you have learned the equations themselves.

The brain does not automatically create relationships just because you want more of them. New information has to meet a check before those connections form readily. Holding information costs energy, and the brain is good at forgetting. It sweeps through memories and drops what is not related to anything else. A fact you need for an exam, such as a piece of trigonometry, still gets dropped if even you cannot say why it matters. Showing that the fact connects to other things you know, then using it to answer problems, is what gets it kept and what raises retention later.

There are two checks. The first is that the information helps solve a problem. The second is that it fits a pattern, or that you can find one. Meeting the problem check raises retention sharply.

Not every problem counts the same. The brain gives more weight to problems that are emotionally charged, personally relevant, or intensely curious, and less weight to dry, technical, surface-level ones. An essay whose literary techniques you do not care about, and still have to answer on, sits lower on that scale. You can raise the priority by finding a way to become more curious, such as getting interested in the author's life, or treating the work as if someone you care about had made it. Creating useful problems is itself a skill that improves with practice.

Look at each new piece as a tool for the problems you already have in mind. Treat it the way you would turn a jigsaw piece around to see where it fits in a picture you already know. Examining it that carefully tends to leave you able to remember the piece without trying to memorize it. The opposite is picking up pieces, putting them down, and only later noticing a few accidental joins, then discovering a week later that the picture is not the one you thought. Until you deliberately use the information for a purpose, the brain stays in a passive, low-care state, and the next day you are starting over.

Frame the work as wanting to solve a problem, then take in the information, not as learning first and solving later. Put yourself in a detective mode. For each new piece, ask which of your questions it might answer, even if the answer is that it does not fit this one and might fit a later one. Hitting the same fact from several angles raises the chance that a first connection forms, and that connection makes the next ones easier.

The pattern check is the companion. The brain uses patterns to guess that something will help solve a problem even when the problem is not yet obvious. An unseen berry that shares the features of known poisonous berries is treated as a risk. A new stretch of ground that matches known hunting country is treated as worth the time. In a novel, a recurring way a character behaves, once you look for it, can make a theme easier to see. It can also make an essay flow because the actions belong together, and help you remember a later action as the same pattern or a contrast. Shared features across several literary techniques work the same way. Clusters of patterns then become a shortcut in an exam. You no longer have to solve every problem from scratch, because you can bring out a whole network at once.

Without a problem in mind, study can feel like opening the fridge with no idea why you walked into the kitchen. With a problem you are already chewing on, even something heard later on the radio can connect and help. When information is framed as solving a problem or as fitting a pattern, retention rises. That is having won about 80% of the way.

Practice questions done before you study a topic, and prestudy that builds a big-picture view, prime the brain for relationships of both kinds. Relationships to what you already know are analogous, such as an accurate analogy, a link to prior learning, or renaming a term in simpler language. Relationships among the new material itself are in-context, such as comparing two new ideas, grouping them by what they share, or drawing how the groups affect each other. Applying a fact to solve a problem right away can be either kind, depending on the problem. Try to build both when you can. The point is not to label each link perfectly. If you cannot make any relationships at all, the material is probably too detailed for your current level, and a stronger surface-level foundation through prestudy comes first.

Later techniques only work if you keep this purpose in mind. Running them as extra repetition means you are using them for the wrong end, and you will not be able to tell whether you are doing them right.

A math example shows what the flipped order looks like in practice. Keep a list of the topic's questions or keywords in view and return to it every 3 to 5 minutes. For each new piece, ask how it relates to what you already have, and whether it is the same as or different from those items. Map the relationships onto the page as simply as you can, starting with a basic logical frame and adding detail as you learn more. In a calculus pass that began from almost scratch, the starting knowledge was small. It included little more than that integration and differentiation are nearly opposites, that a derivative comes from differentiation, and what a function is. The frame built from there included functions and inverse functions, and differentiation producing a derivative. It also included uses such as tangents and normals, anti-differentiation, and integration leading to quantities such as area, rates, and averages.

After about one and a half hours, the equations and formulae were still not all known. What was known was how each equation would be used once it was learned. Those uses included finding minima and maxima, judging the smoothness of a function, locating stationary points, and working with signed areas. That prepares you for applied questions, which are often the hardest, before you can run the calculations. Students who struggle with applied math are usually approaching the topic in a way that is too heavy on memorization. They have not lined up the material they study with the reason they are studying it. Only after that logical picture is in place do you move on to the equations, the calculations, and practice. Spending the time up front can flip a roughly 1-to-1 return into a roughly 10-to-1 return. Each later practice question then helps you understand about 10 more question types, because you already know the conceptual ways the idea can be applied and therefore the ways it can be tested. The later procedural work of applying the math then becomes faster, more intuitive, and more purposeful. About one and a half hours to reach that kind of confidence, without yet knowing the equations, is presented as time well spent.

Good recall and a deep grasp of the material show up after those knowledge structures are built. They do not come from intending to remember or understand something.
