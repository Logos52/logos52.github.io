# Aim

Aiming is about steering your brain toward the right direction. By asking useful questions, you nudge it toward particular patterns and particular relationships. Two questions that reliably build strong networks in any subject are "Why does this matter?" and "How does this connect to something else?"

The purpose of the step is to make new information relevant and form networks immediately, and to switch on higher-order learning as soon as you can. It is also to create groups, also called chunks, and relationships that are strong, versatile, and suited to what you will later need to retrieve.

Those two questions can sound similar, and they are the pair arrived at after years of trial and watching what works. "Why does this matter?" turns attention to the big picture. It creates focus, purpose, and alignment among ideas, and it helps you reach the evaluate level of thinking, where you make judgments and prioritise networks. Asking it critically helps groups become easier to remember and more intuitive. "How does this connect to another concept?" looks for further relationships that may not be obvious. When you dig into why a point matters, it is hard to step outside existing biases and preconceptions. Two concepts may seem unrelated at first, and only when you are forced to explore their relationship do you find new perspectives and potential groups. Sometimes that leads you to re-evaluate the entire grouping and the reasons for importance.

"What" questions are usually too simple and too isolated to produce high retention. Questions that start with "Why" and "How" join information to other pieces and are therefore much more meaningful. Questions that feel more emotional or personal, and that stir curiosity, also bind more strongly. Since the aim is to enter a problem-solving mindset, skip questions about problems whose answers you already have. If the topic itself makes you curious, you will be able to produce more knowledge. Asking the right questions may be challenging at the beginning. Generating more curiosity also makes studying more enjoyable, which makes it easier to learn and retain information.

Aim completely replaces the red-light phase of the traffic-light system, where you create questions before you consume. Red light forms some early connections and relationships. Aim is a tighter, more deliberate use of questions, meant to form only those relationships that are likely to pay off.

Prestudy, meaning study done before a main learning event, and Aim often overlap, and they are not the same thing. The aim of that earlier study is to ready the brain to form relationships ahead of an intensive main learning event. The Bear Hunter System is a method of learning, and prestudy is a chronological step in the journey. Aim should be one of the first steps in that journey, if not the very first. If that happens before an intensive main event, it counts as prestudy. If prestudy does not happen, Aim should still come first. This kind of thinking is very hard to manage in an intensive lecture or class, so Aim may have to happen afterward during a self-study session, which is not optimal. You could also do a very short prestudy of just 5 minutes or 10 minutes, in which a full Aim may not be feasible. In that case you would do a very light Aim and develop only the largest groups and the main relationships. A more comprehensive prestudy can include a full Aim and even more detail, and later the next step, Shoot, could be done during prestudy as well. In short, prestudy is about when learning begins. The Bear Hunter System is the method you learn with.

In earlier stages the work is to ask a wide range of questions, so you learn how to call up curiosity and see how questions change the way you process information. Here the work shifts toward fewer questions, centred on judging importance, so the groups you form also reflect those judgments. That method of grouping is called importance-based chunking. The steps start with asking why a keyword or concept is important, then looking for the possible answers, since there will usually be more than one. You then move on to the next keyword in any order that feels useful and repeat. Similarities in why things are important become potential groups. You judge which relationships are worth keeping, and which similarities are worth turning into groups. You continue until you have covered the concepts. Work through and settle each new keyword as you go, and use grouping and mapping to reduce cognitive load, meaning the amount of mental work in play, before you introduce new ideas.

A keyword list used for this should not already have headings or groupings, so you are not biased toward someone else's groups and can form your own by choosing the order. As relationships multiply, the map can show spiderwebbing, meaning many chaotic and messy links that easily overwhelm you if you leave them there. When that happens, look at the relationships you have found and hypothesise a different grouping. Important relationships should show through better grouping, not through more lines and arrows. You use grouping and non-linear notes throughout to manage cognitive load and keep it at an optimum. Higher-order work raises that load on purpose. Grouping and notes bring it back down so you do not overflow. The optimum range can still feel uncomfortable if your habits have been mostly lower-order.

Once you have groups based on importance, you can ask how those groups, or the groups inside them, relate to and affect each other. Those links are inter-chunk relationships. When they are clean, they create a simple backbone to the learning, and the process becomes much faster. At this stage you also want to drop "what" questions, meaning fact or recall questions, because they lead only to lower-order learning. Questions that force you to compare concepts, such as why one thing is important or how it relates to another, produce higher-order learning as well. When you do higher-order learning, the brain also completes lower-order learning, so "why" and "how it relates" questions can replace "what" questions.

Understanding why something is important gives you a sense of how the information is structured, which means you know how to think about the subject. When the brain does not know how to think about a subject, it tries to solve many problems at the same time. It asks what something means, why you need to know it, how to use it, and whether it is relevant. It also asks whether you need to memorise the details, how to solve problems with it, and how it fits with everything else. That scatter makes you less focused, costs more time and energy, and yields less learning. When information is poorly organised, retention stays low. Logical frameworks and patterns also repeat across topics, and you will discover many of them by asking why a concept is important. Common patterns exist in different subjects. The instruction is still to start by asking why it is important, because relying only on a list of common patterns will cause problems later.

The work can feel the same as the traffic-light system. It can also feel overwhelming, because answering "Why is it important?" reveals a large number of relationships, which rapidly creates a network you can later refine. It can feel confusing, because many non-linear layouts are possible for the main concepts, and choosing the layout that makes the most sense takes real effort. That effort adds a strong amount of prioritisation and evaluation, described as one of the highest orders of learning. It can feel time-consuming, because each keyword or concept needs such deep examination. That examination consolidates your learning. The feeling fades as speed and efficiency increase, and the brain gets better at building networks, ranking them, and choosing the strongest hypothetical structure.

A common objection is that the process takes too much time. Encoding the first time can take a good deal longer when you are first learning the skill. It becomes faster and more efficient as the skill develops. When efficiency is measured across the whole process, time lost in the first encoding is more than made up for even in the early stages of competence. Another objection is that it is too hard, too effortful, and too tiring. Effective learning always takes effort, even though many people have been taught otherwise. Steering around difficulty and discomfort only works for easy material at fairly low volume. With practice you learn to associate higher cognitive load with effective learning, so the difficulty that helps learning starts to feel desirable as well as difficult. As skill improves, the load itself does not reduce. You become better at managing it and are no longer bothered by it. Many people then become bothered by passive learning, because they can see how little it achieves.

A third objection is that the questions you think of are not the same as how you will be assessed. When encoding information, you should prioritise what your brain finds relevant, which is described as the shortest path to expertise. Once you have developed expertise, you can practise retrieving it in the way assessments require. That is more efficient than limiting encoding, and therefore forgetting more, through trying to match assessments during the first encoding. A comparison used here is laying down a road network in a city. Once those roads exist, you can use them however you need to get where you need to go. You may also worry that your questions sit outside the scope of an assessment. It is more efficient to step a little past a curriculum's boundary to improve depth and retention than to remain inside that boundary and suffer reduced retention or depth. You can use your own judgment, together with learning objectives if they are available, to avoid wandering too far from the topic.

## Open questions

- How does asking why something is important, before you ask what it is, change the groups you are able to form?
- How does measuring time across the whole process, not only the first encoding session, change whether Aim feels worth the effort?
