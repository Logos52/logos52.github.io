# Non-Linear Note-Making

Non-linear note-making keeps the work of deciding how pieces relate, which a verbatim copy of the speaker's order throws away. The form is a wait until a relation can be named, then a mark that says how. A first page that still follows the contents list is a form error rather than a verdict on the method.

## Process, then the mark

The strongest habit is the delay. After a section, the relation gets said in one sentence or one arrow before any notes exist. If that sentence will not come, the next move is another pass at the source, not a page of marks. These mechanics serve [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Shoot|Shoot]], the encoding step that works a source against questions into a rough map.

Continuous note-taking feels safe. It also buys a later revision bill: the deciding was skipped, so the same material has to be processed again, and the total time goes up. The full page that felt secure is paid for after the session.

Sitting with the material before a mark hits the page is uncomfortable. That discomfort is the work happening, not a score of how well it is happening. Writing to end the feeling chooses the cheaper-feeling path that defers the work.

The default mark is an arrow, not a line. A line says two things are connected; an arrow says how — one-way, two-way, bracketed, or deliberately not drawn. Choosing the type is the higher-order work a bare line lets a page skip. [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Mindmaps|Mindmaps]] owns the image-craft and the default-to-arrows rule at length. Concepts go spatial; specific facts often stay verbal. Forcing every fact into a drawing is not this technique. A mark on the page reinforces a relation already decided. It is not a substitute for grouping, which happens first.

Chunks form first, then the map. Groups come from relations and importance, not from the source's hierarchy. The way a source hands over its headings already decides the first groups, and those given groups already look logical, which is why "there is no other way" feels true. The headings are a starting list, not the chunks.

## Failures, citations, limits

Two named failures show up as signals.

Spiderwebbing is grouping the way the book is structured, then adding lateral arrows afterward. The arrows cross. The map gets slow, frustrating, and hard to keep. The repair is to flatten the source hierarchy to a list first, then build chunks from relations that are actually there. Waterfalling lives on [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Mindmaps|Mindmaps]] with the rest of that family.

[[wiki/Concepts/Reverse Causality|Reverse Causality]] is the other signal: calling A important only because it produces B, so both now have to be memorised. The repair is to anchor importance in a wider network, so each piece is inferable rather than rote. Intuitive chunking tends to prevent the loop; importance-checklisting and simple Q&A can worsen it. [[Importance-Based Chunking]] is what makes a chunk a chunk; [[Survive and Thrive]] is why relations are the keep-signal.

Non-linear encoding makes citation awkward, so two layers run in parallel. The map is for understanding. Every two or three sources — not after every page, and not at the end of the topic — a short linear, cited synthesis is for consolidation and later retrieval of sources. The cited-as-you-go synthesis is the method for a single piece of writing that has to ship. A stored reference layer, in a reference app or a citation manager, is the method for sources reused across projects. When both jobs exist, the two combine; neither replaces the other.

Both methods add administrative time on top of encoding. The delay is also slower in the first ten minutes. That is the price in the same breath as the benefit: the map holds relations instead of a transcript, and the citations still exist when a paper is due. The stored layer is worth a trial on a handful of sources before a whole field is committed to a template; a bad structure is expensive to unwind. Being able to cite the right source later is not a filing problem. It rides on already having a relational grasp of the topic, plus using those sources often enough that retrieval is interleaved with the writing. A well-tagged library on a thin map still will not hand over the right paper.

Verbatim capture is the weak form. Typing is a risk factor for that drift, not a finding that the keyboard or the line is the enemy. Constructing a relation structure — nodes plus labelled links — is what the outside record actually supports.

Verbatim linear notes are the wrong tool for understanding, not for a timestamped record. Minutes, a quote log, a legal pad — those jobs keep the linear form. A first map that copies the table of contents is also not this page, and it is the usual reason a first try gets abandoned.

If after a section one relation still cannot be named without looking back, drawing stops and the source comes back — the map is not the bottleneck. If the arrows on a finished page all run the same direction as the chapter headings, the grouping never left the source: flatten to a list and start again.

A page that lasted is a page that decided a relation before the mark. After one delayed pass the arrows can be defended — what kind of relation, which way. If the page is neater and still in the source's sequence, the delay did not happen.
