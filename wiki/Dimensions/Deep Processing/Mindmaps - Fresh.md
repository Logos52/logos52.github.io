# Mindmaps - Fresh

A mindmap is a page of keywords spread out in space and joined by lines and arrows that show how the ideas relate. It is a way of taking notes that makes you decide how a topic fits together, and that deciding is the work that turns new facts into memory you can use. The first maps feel slow and confusing. That feeling is the method working, and knowing it in advance is what keeps you going past the point where most people stop.

## Core takeaways

- The map is a by-product. The deciding is what stores the knowledge.
- Confusion and slowness are signs it is working.
- A map that feels like normal notes is a list with lines added.
- Decide the relations before you write the words.
- Draw only the links that matter, and give each a direction.
- Expect a tangled first draft. Redraw it.
- Test yourself from the map. A miss means a redraw.

## Why it works

Almost everything you learn from arrives in a line. A lecture, a book and a video each deliver one word after another, and normal notes keep that line and shorten it. The brain rewards the shortening with a feeling of understanding and progress, and that feeling is a poor guide to what you will be able to recall or use a week later. A map breaks the line. Every idea needs a place on the page, its links to other ideas, the direction of each link and a label of one or two words, and each of those decisions makes you recall what came earlier and compare it with what came later. That recall and comparison is what attaches a new fact to the things you already know, and an attached fact is one that lasts.

- New facts fade within days unless they attach to known ones.
- Placing and linking a fact on paper is how it attaches.
- Each attached fact is an anchor for the next, so later facts in the same field land faster.
- Rearranging the page stores position and shape, an extra cue at recall.

```
source:  A B C D   (one after another)

notes:   a b c d          map:   A --> B
         same order,             |     |
         shorter                 v     v
                                 C <-- D
feels fast and clear      feels slow and confusing
```

## How to do it

Work out how the ideas relate before you write any of them down. Arrows added afterwards cross through words and run everywhere, and that is the surest sign the relations were an afterthought. Build in layers, with the main chain of cause and effect first, then the concepts that hang off it, then only the details that make a concept concrete. Take the source in small pieces, and draw a little of the map after each piece, because collecting everything first and mapping at the end is slower and harder.

- For every idea, ask why it matters and how it relates to the rest.
- Group by why things matter. The first grouping you find is rarely the best.
- Few words, your own shorthand, simple symbols.
- A small odd drawing beside an idea is a landmark you find again later.
- Do not copy a diagram from the source. Drawing it yourself is what stores it.
- Make the main path visibly bigger or thicker than the rest.
- Facts that connect to nothing go to flashcards.
- Unlined paper, a page big enough to keep the links visible, by hand until fluent.

## What a finished map has

A finished map can be checked against six qualities. Each one forces a decision, and the decision is where the learning happens, so a map that passes all six was hard to make. Getting the first three right usually takes weeks of practice, so early maps will fail the check.

- Grouped: related ideas sit together, in groups you chose.
- Relational: the links that matter are drawn, and only those.
- Interconnected: the groups link to each other, so the topic is one structure.
- Nonverbal: few words, symbols and small drawings.
- Directional: arrows show which idea acts on which.
- Emphasised: the most important groups and links are visibly larger.

Choosing what to emphasise often shows that a different grouping would be better, and the map gets redone. Redoing is part of the method.

## Where it fails

Two kinds of people decide that mapping does not work. The first feel the confusion and the slowness and stop, because nobody told them the confusion is the method working. The second feel nothing different from their usual notes. They have drifted back to what feels fluent: ideas in the source's order, a few obvious links, and no time spent on the messier alternatives. The fix for the second group is to ask how else the ideas could be arranged and which real relations are still undrawn.

- Hub and spokes: every line runs from the centre, none run sideways between groups.
- Islands: tight groups with weak links between them. You know each part and cannot use them together.
- Reverse causality: an item explained by what it produces. It sits isolated and has to be memorised.
- Too many arrows: every relation drawn, so none can be read.
- A copied map: someone else's map, or a pasted diagram, skips the decisions that store the knowledge.
- A model doing the grouping: asking an AI to group your keywords does the deciding for you. Asking it to check groups you made is fine.

A confusing map shows what is confused in your head. Normal notes hide the same confusion until an exam or a real problem exposes it.

## Evidence

- A meta-analysis of 55 studies: learner-built concept maps beat conventional study, with a moderate effect, larger when learners build the map themselves.
- Experts sort problems by underlying principle, novices by surface features.
- Handwriting did not beat typing for notes once the study was repeated. Notes that copy the source word for word retain less, and a map cannot be word for word.
- Studies of the drawing style with one centre and branches fanning out are small and mixed. The evidence supports building the relation structure yourself and does not favour one drawing style.
