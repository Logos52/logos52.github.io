# Deep Processing Tanking

Deep processing tanking is one failure mode of [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing|Deep Processing]]. It is the stretch of years in which a learner evaluates importance, compares, analogizes, and forms schema by instinct, and clears curricula pitched at the average without ever needing a method. It holds until the material grows past what instinct can carry. Copying a top learner's visible strategy does not transfer the result, because the strategy was never what produced the result.

The type is a history of automatic work. It is not a measured trait. People who have been evaluating importance, comparing, analogizing, and forming schema without naming any of it as a method clear ordinary environments so cleanly that no deliberate system is ever demanded of them.

## Core takeaways

- Deep processing is evaluating importance, comparing, analogizing, simplifying accurately, and building schema. When it runs by instinct it carries easier environments, and that success hides the missing method.
- The break usually arrives at a transition: school to university, university to work, a familiar domain to an unfamiliar one, individual work to higher responsibility, low volume to high.
- Harder work should cost more hours. Tanking is a disproportionate jump in hours for the same or worse result.
- Understanding is not enough. Material can be followed while no retrievable, usable structure exists.
- More effort only helps when it creates better organization. Effort spent fighting confusion, distraction, or unsupported detail is bad load.
- The six repairs give the existing processing handles: thinking on paper, schema mapping, comparison, importance ranking, micro-retrieval, early application. The readiness filter asks whether enough is already known to make the material simpler, and if not, the work steps down a layer.

## What deep processing does

Deep processing is the work of making meaning from material instead of repeating its surface: evaluating how important a piece is, comparing it with what is already held, finding an analogy, simplifying it accurately, and placing it in a structure that can be retrieved later. Evaluation, stated as an act, is a value judgment: how important this piece is, whether it is more or less important than that one, and what order they go in if they are ranked. Most learners finish a page asking only whether they understood it.

A strong processor applies a higher bar to the same test of whether the material makes sense. The material does not count as making sense until it has been simplified, connected to what is already known, given an analogy, and ranked against its neighbours. The work continues until it is clear what matters, why it matters, how it compares, where it fits, what it changes, how to simplify it, and how to use it. That work produces schema rather than isolated facts. Surface detail can then be forgotten and the idea rebuilt from the structure.

The same work can feel effortless. While it stays implicit it is never noticed as a set of skills, and it is never built into tools that still run when the day is bad and the volume is high.

## Why the method never gets built

Three levers move results: time and effort, strategy, and natural processing. When challenge rises, most learners add time and effort first. That lever is finite. Exhaustion or weak returns end it, and then those learners are pushed onto strategy. Strategy is what carries them upward afterwards. The average learner's strategy improved continuously across those same years because improving it was the only lever available.

More hours on one method raise results until the work reaches tasks that the method cannot do at all. More hours do not convert into those tasks. This system's own teaching model gives the same fact in numbers: a learner whose methods are limited moves from thirty hours to sixty and lifts total mastery from about 66 percent to about 76 percent. Doubling the hours gains ten points. Time and effort is finite, and more of it only helps when it creates better organization.

**Some methods are incompatible with the higher levels of mastery unless the processing is already high.**

School curricula, university examinations, and grading standards are pitched at the average, so almost any method clears them for a strong processor. The case is the same as a person who can lift a hundred kilos one-handed: that person does not need clean technique to lift five or ten. The strategy lever is never pulled because it is never needed. The discovery that no method exists arrives when the material is already past what instinct carries and no method has been practised.

Natural processing is powerful and unreliable while it stays implicit. The first response to the jump is usually more hours. Grinding the hours, or having someone else break the topic down, clears the instance and leaves the cause in place, so the same problem returns. Being a hard worker is a real capability. Needing maximum effort for everything is not a capability.

## Ability did not drop

The learner has not become less capable. The limit that was reached is the limit of the strategies already in use. The test is not whether the work got harder. Harder work should cost more. Tanking is the disproportionate jump: the same or worse result for far more hours. In this system's teaching model the ratio measures mastery and retention held per hour. It does not measure content covered per session. Coverage does not measure what is still there a week later, or whether the knowledge can be used rather than only recognised.

Between the stretch when things were easy and the point where they stopped being easy, the difficulty moved roughly from a three to a seven. The question to ask is whether the strategy moved from a three to a seven, or only from a three to a four.

The sequence runs as follows. Strong processing produces early success on ordinary methods. Early success removes the pressure to build strategy. A transition arrives. Hours go up. Returns decline. The learner comes to believe that ability dropped.

## Where it shows up and what it looks like

Tanking appears at transitions: school to university, university to work, a familiar domain to an unfamiliar one, individual contributor to higher responsibility, low volume to high. A programme where everyone arrived as the top of their own school, taught at a level pitched for exactly that, removes the margin the strong processor had been living on. When complexity or volume rises past what instinct carries, performance drops or effort spikes.

The signs are concrete. Hours rise without proportional gains. The material makes sense but is not usable. Notes multiply, and the learner still cannot command the material. The work feels slower than the learner's picture of their own ability. Rereading and rewatching stop solving it. Performance exposes gaps that passive understanding hid.

Tanking is not the main explanation for attention-span problems, low motivation, or misuse of generated answers. Those are adjacent mechanisms. Tanking is specifically the strategy gap when natural depth stops scaling. If a few weeks of externalizing and testing early has not moved the effort-to-result ratio, strategy was not the bottleneck. Volume, sleep, prerequisite knowledge, and attention each produce the same surface and each has its own repair.

## Understanding is not organization

Understanding is not enough. The material can be followed while no retrievable, applicable, modifiable structure exists.

| Understanding | Organization |
|---|---|
| Makes sense while looking at it | What matters, where it fits, how to use it |
| Recognition, familiarity, short-term clarity | Schema, retrieval, transfer, application |

Deep processors are especially exposed because they understand quickly, and quick understanding hides weak organization until performance. The replacement question is "How can I organize this?" [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Knowledge Mastery - From Recognition to Usable Knowledge|Knowledge Mastery]] owns the levels between recognizing a thing and being able to use it. [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Schema|Schema Construction, Assimilation, and Reorganization]] owns how an organized structure is built, extended, and rebuilt.

## Good load and bad load

The repair is not to make learning easier. Easy learning often removes the effort that creates durable encoding. The move changes the kind of load. It does not reduce the load.

More effort only helps when it creates better organization. Load spent decoding, copying, or holding detail that has nothing to attach to occupies working memory, and the structure has to be built with what is left. Resources that interleave fine detail with occasional logic keep relevance fluctuating, so no model can form to hold what arrives, and difficulty stays high the whole way through.

```
weak: copying notes without judgment; decoding technical language too early; memorizing unsupported detail; fighting distraction; accepting another person's structure wholesale; pushing into material with no foundation

strong: comparing distant concepts; judging importance; simplifying accurately; explaining from memory; asking non-obvious relationship questions; building a schema map; testing a hypothesis; applying immediately
```

[[wiki/Concepts/Cognitive Load & What Mental Effort Is Trying to Cue|Cognitive Load]] is the page that owns this split: effort that builds the model against effort that occupies working memory without building it.

## The readiness question and the four layers

The readiness filter is one question: do I know enough about this to make it simpler? If not, the work steps down a layer. A layer, in this system's teaching model, is one of four. Logic is the backbone: the largest chunks and the relationships that hold them. Concepts are what a thing is and how it happens. Important details are the ones that make a concept concrete. Arbitrary details are carried only because they are assessed. Roughly a tenth of the material is conceptual, a fifth is important detail, and the remaining seventy per cent is arbitrary. The arbitrary share shrinks as the first two layers get better, because relevance is what converts an arbitrary detail into a placed one. The proportion is like ten thousand leaves on five or six branches: a small number of backbone relationships carry the large number of details. The operative move is to improve the backbone. Memorising harder does not do that work.

If a piece cannot be simplified, the next move is a simpler explanation, the missing terms, the prerequisites, the detail marked for later, or a rough schema first. Deep processors tolerate high effort and can read it as progress. The readiness question keeps productive depth from becoming unsupported strain. [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Layers of Learning|Layers of Learning]] owns this structure and the volume estimate behind it.

## Six handles

The six repairs do not replace deep processing. They give it handles.

- Thinking on paper makes the evaluation visible, so it can be inspected and revised.
- Schema mapping places each piece against the others.
- Importance ranking gives each relationship an explicit significance rating against the wider topic, with a short bounded lookup when the rating is uncertain. Gaps stop hiding, and relationships that felt settled turn uncertain.
- Non-obvious questions force comparisons the source never made.
- Micro-retrieval reconstructs a piece immediately, before checking.
- Early application ships an imperfect structure on purpose and tests it.

[[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Thinking on Paper - Mindmaps and Other Techniques|Thinking on Paper]] is the externalizing move and the likely first repair. [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Best-attempt Encoding|Best-attempt Encoding]] is what testing the structure early means in practice.

## Practising the handles

Owning a skill takes more than understanding it, remembering it, and applying it correctly. It also has to run consistently and easily when the learner is tired, stressed, or having a bad day. That fourth requirement is the one that fails, and it fails exactly where the skill was most needed. Implicit depth only runs on a good day. The handles are what change that.

Practice time is fixed. Spreading it across five new skills grows each skill at a fifth of the rate, and a payoff that would have arrived after ten focused hours arrives after fifty. Work on one or two handles at a time, until each is correct and consistent.

For the average learner the eventual limiter is the processing habits themselves, and retraining those runs in months to years. A learner whose processing is already strong is limited by strategy instead, and strategy moves on a much shorter clock. That is why the repair is often fast. It is a reason the repair can be fast. It is not a guarantee that it will be.

The material feeling easier is not the check, because easy learning often removes the effort that creates durable encoding. The check is the same result arriving in fewer hours, and the topic being rebuildable from memory with the notes closed.

## What the research supports

Deep processing tanking is a practitioner category. It is not a measured syndrome. The nearest public research establishes that instructional support has to change as skill grows. That finding supports the general shape, that what helped before stops helping. It does not establish a named condition. The reframe is only earned once the ordinary causes of the same surface are ruled out.

## The whole loop

The operating model is a short loop: diagnose the transition, identify the hidden strategy gap, externalize, move effort into good load, apply the readiness filter, test early, make depth repeatable. The work is turning deep processing from an unconscious strength into a conscious skill. The repair adds no processing power. It gives the processing handles. Depth counts as a capability when it still runs while the learner is tired and the volume is high. Until then it is a tendency.

## How to practice this

1. Compare the hours you spend now with the hours you spent before the transition. Set the two against the results. Notice whether the same or a worse result now costs far more hours. That disproportionate jump is the sign, and harder work costing more is normal.
2. Rate the difficulty of the material then and now on one scale. Notice whether it moved roughly from a three to a seven. Rate your strategy on the same scale. Notice whether it moved to a seven or only to a four.
3. Check volume, sleep, prerequisite knowledge, and attention before naming a strategy gap. Each of these produces the same surface. If a few weeks of externalizing and early testing has not moved the ratio, strategy was not the bottleneck.
4. Before pushing into a piece of material, ask whether you know enough to make it simpler. If the answer is no, step down a layer: a simpler explanation, the missing terms, or the prerequisites. Notice whether the effort you tolerate is building a structure or only producing strain.
5. Pick one or two handles and work them until each runs correctly and consistently. Thinking on paper is the likely first: write the evaluation down so it can be inspected and revised. Rank each relationship's importance against the wider topic. Notice which relationships that felt settled turn uncertain, and which gaps stop hiding.
6. After a piece, reconstruct it from memory before checking. Then apply the structure while it is still imperfect, and test it. Notice whether the same result arrives in fewer hours. Notice whether the topic can be rebuilt with the notes closed, on a tired day with the volume high.
