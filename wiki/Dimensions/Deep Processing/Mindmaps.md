# Mindmaps

Mind mapping asks you to turn linear material, which almost everything you read or hear is, into a nonlinear picture of relationships. Ordinary notes produce feelings of fluency, meaning the sense that you understand the page, that you are making progress, that the material is becoming familiar, and that you are moving faster. Mapping breaks those feelings. Those feelings are a poor guide to whether you can actually use the knowledge. [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Thinking on Paper - Mindmaps and Other Techniques|Thinking on Paper - Mindmaps and Other Techniques]] covers why working on paper helps, why linear notes fall short, why mapping feels slow, and why an early map should look messy. This page picks up what that account leaves open. It covers how new knowledge has to join a schema, meaning a network of related knowledge, before it can settle, so it does not fade on its own. It also covers the two ways mapping fails, and what to do when the page gets messy.

High-performing knowledge has to be retained long enough to use. It also has to be mastered deeply enough that you can bring concepts together, not only use each one in isolation, and used efficiently, meaning in a short amount of time. Isolated repetition, such as flashcards that you revisit every day, every few days, or every couple of weeks, can hold items in place for a while. It is not time-efficient. If you forget quickly, you soon carry a debt of hundreds or thousands of items to keep repeating, and that pattern usually also means you cannot use the information at a high level of mastery.

That repeating debt appears when new facts stay isolated. A fact needs a place to belong, connected to other things you already know. Sitting as a loose piece you try to hold onto by itself is what fades and has to be repeated. When those connections are clear, the information can settle. As the schema grows, it offers more anchor points for the next fact. That is why an expert can pick up a new fact in their field quickly, while a beginner finds the same fact obscure. Once a piece fits, you hold it more securely, you can use it together with other ideas on varied problems, and you spend less time relearning what you would otherwise forget each day.

Schema formation feels recursive, slow, effortful, and confusing, because you are looking for the parts that confuse you and asking how they connect so you can sort them out. You meet more confusion because you are moving toward it, the way a cleaner meets dirt by going to where it is dirty. That is how effective learning feels. Ordinary note-taking sends the opposite signals, which is why mapping can feel like a waste of time until you know what those signals mean. A serve that started to feel more comfortable was, to a coach, worse technique, because comfort had been mistaken for good form. Some skills have to feel uncomfortable at first if they are going to work.

There are two groups of people who find mind mapping ineffective. The first group tries it, feels more confused, finds the work recursive and slow, and spends a lot of effort just arranging ideas on the page. Because they do not realize that this difficulty is what drives schema formation, they decide mapping is not for them and they stop. For that group, the move is to try again and push through. Retention and the ability to use the knowledge rise on the other side of that stretch.

The second group is more of a problem. They feel that mapping is not helping, and they also feel no extra confusion or extra recursion. Mapping feels about the same as their usual method, except that the notes are now drawn in different places on the page. That almost always means they have adjusted back toward fluency. Arranging, relating, and spacing ideas creates so many options that the page quickly becomes messy and hard to follow, and a map that crowded stops helping. If you treat smoothness and speed as a good sign, you then pick the first obvious links, add a few points inside those groups, and stop, because thinking through the other possibilities is overwhelming. The change that group needs is to ask, at that stopping point, how else the ideas could be arranged, and which real relationships are present that have not yet been drawn.

When you ask that, the page will look messier. That is a good sign in the early stages. The messy sheet is the raw material of schema building. The next step is to clean it up. Organize it more intuitively and more simply, and give the ideas room on the page. Spacing the map that way also uses visual and spatial memory as an extra cue for retention. As the paper becomes cleaner, the thinking becomes cleaner with it. Only when you look at the mess, accept that it needs cleaning, and work through it are you doing the mapping itself. If that picture of your mind looks confused, the knowledge is confused. You have put the disorganization where you can see it, which is why the map can be called a mind mirror. With ordinary learning, that same disorganization is still there. The first time you may see it is in an exam, or when someone tests you, or when you have to perform. You would not leave for an important event without looking in a mirror. Do not try to perform with your knowledge without first seeing how organized the thoughts really are.

## Open questions

- How does connecting an isolated fact into a schema change how much later repetition that fact still needs?
- How does cleaning a messy first map change what you can see about your own confusion before an exam?
