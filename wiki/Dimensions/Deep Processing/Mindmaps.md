# Mindmaps

A mindmap is a page of short keywords placed in space and joined by lines that show how the ideas cause, need or contain each other. Making one is slower and more confusing than writing normal notes, and that slowness is the part that builds lasting memory. A reader who expects the confusion can keep going where most people quit.

## Core takeaways

- The map is a by-product of the thinking that stores the knowledge.
- Feeling fluent while taking notes is a poor guide to recall.
- Mapping feels slow because every idea needs decisions.
- Cleaning up a messy map is where the structure forms.
- A map that feels like normal notes is a list with lines added.
- Build in layers: main logic, then concepts, then details.
- Draw only the links that matter, each a real cause and effect.

## How it works

Input arrives in a line. A book, a lecture and a video each deliver one word after another, and linear notes keep that order and shorten it, which the brain rewards with a feeling of fluency. A map breaks the order. Each idea needs a place on the page, its links, their direction and a short label, and those extra decisions force you to retrieve what came before and compare it with what came after. The decisions are what store the knowledge.

- New facts fade unless they attach to known ones.
- Linking on paper is how a fact attaches.
- Each attached fact is an anchor for the next.
- Arranging the page stores position and shape, an extra cue.

```
source:  A B C D   (one after another)

notes:   a b c d          map:   A --> B
         same order,             |     |
         shorter                 v     v
                                 C <-- D
feels fast and clear      feels slow and confusing
```

## How to do it

Decide the relations before writing content. Arrows added afterwards, crossing through words, are the sign that relations were an afterthought. Hold two questions while reading: why does this matter, and how does it relate to the rest. Group by the answers, since the source's headings are rarely the best grouping. Expect the first draft to tangle, and redraw it with the crossing lines pulled apart, the ideas regrouped and given room.

- Layer 1: the core points and the main flow.
- Layer 2: the concepts that hang off them.
- Layer 3: details that make a concept concrete.
- Facts with no connection go to flashcards.
- A tangle that will not untangle: regroup around a new centre.
- Few words, your own shorthand, simple symbols.
- Draw every diagram yourself instead of copying it.
- Make the main path visibly bigger or thicker.
- Test yourself from the map, and redraw after any error.
- Unlined paper, a large page, by hand until fluent.

## Where it fails

Most people who drop mapping do so at the confusion. The confusion is the map showing what is not yet organised, and normal notes hide the same disorder until a test exposes it. The other common way out is the map that never felt different from normal notes. It kept the source's order with a few links, and the messy alternatives were never tried, so the fix is to ask how else the ideas could be arranged. One structural mistake is worth knowing by name: an item explained by what it produces has no cause to hang on, so it has to be memorised on its own.

- Hub and spokes: every line runs from the centre, none sideways.
- Reverse causality: an item explained by what it produces.
- Too many arrows: every relation drawn, so none can be read.
- The list with lines added: source order kept, few links.
- Quitting at the confusion.

## Evidence

Three findings from the research support the method. None of them favour one drawing style over another. What they test is whether building a structure of relations yourself beats reading or copying one.

- A pooled analysis of 55 studies: self-built maps beat ordinary study.
- Experts sort problems by underlying principle, novices by surface features.
- Handwriting did not beat typing once the study was repeated.
- Word-for-word notes retain less, and a map cannot be word for word.

- Nesbit, J. C., & Adesope, O. O. (2006). Learning with concept and knowledge maps: A meta-analysis. *Review of Educational Research, 76*(3), 413–448. 55 studies. Learner-constructed concept mapping against conventional study, *d* ≈ 0.46; the effect is larger when learners construct rather than study a provided map. Construction of a relation structure versus conventional study; supports the operating claim from the outside.
- Chi, M. T. H., Feltovich, P. J., & Glaser, R. (1981). Categorization and representation of physics problems by experts and novices. *Cognitive Science, 5*(2), 121–152. Novices group by surface features, experts by principle: the supported basis for chunking by importance rather than by resemblance.
- Farrand, P., Hussain, F., & Hennessy, E. (2002). The efficacy of the 'mind map' study technique. *Medical Education, 36*(5), 426–431. Small and mixed. The weaker literature around the radial-diagram brand, cited as the bound rather than as support.
- Paivio, A. (1991). Dual coding theory: Retrospect and current status. *Canadian Journal of Psychology, 45*(3), 255–287. A mark alongside words has a trace of its own. The theory does not license "images encode more than text."
- Mueller, P. A., & Oppenheimer, D. M. (2014). The pen is mightier than the keyboard: Advantages of longhand over laptop note taking. *Psychological Science*, 25(6), 1159–1168. Mechanism: laptop notes were more verbatim; conceptual questions suffered. Notes that drift toward verbatim retain less. The mechanism survives; the tool claim does not, and the format finding did not hold in later replications.
- Morehead, K., Dunlosky, J., & Rawson, K. A. (2019). How much mightier is the pen than the keyboard for note-taking? A replication and extension. *Educational Psychology Review*, 31, 753–780. Failed to reproduce the conceptual longhand advantage; the longhand-beats-laptop tool claim failed to reproduce.
- Urry, H. L., et al. (2021). Don't ditch the laptop just yet: A direct replication of Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014). *Psychological Science*, 32(8), 1192–1206. Direct replication; no significant longhand advantage.
- Ausubel, D. P. (1960). The use of advance organizers in the learning and retention of meaningful verbal material. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 51(5), 267–272. Arriving with a more abstract frame makes later detail land. Mayer's pre-training principle is the later statement of the same move.
- Craik, F. I. M., & Lockhart, R. S. (1972). Levels of processing: A framework for memory research. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 11(6), 671–684. Encoding quality over repeated exposure.
- Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 14(1), 4–58. Elaborative interrogation and self-explanation.
- Miller, G. A. (1956). The magical number seven, plus or minus two. *Psychological Review*, 63(2), 81–97. Grouping as load reduction. Cowan (2001) is the later working-memory bound.
