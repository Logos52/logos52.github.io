# Aim - Training I

## Reasoning

### Abstract

The Aim step is the list of questions written before the material is opened, and it is the first pass of the Bear Hunter System. Two questions carry it. Why is this important, and how does it relate to that other concept. The pass collects a topic's keywords fast and out of order, asks the two questions of each, and sketches a structure it refuses to commit to. What comes out is a backbone of the main chunks with no details on it. It also leaves a brain primed, so that everything read afterwards has somewhere to land. Before the first Aim, thirteen skills have to be in place, and three of them are drills the step rests on. A session takes about 25 minutes for a topic of 15 to 30 concepts. It is meant to feel chaotic, and it goes on Sunday for the week's material. The bar is the ability to turn a concept list into questions that find chunks and the relationships between them. Holding those chunks to a standard is the second half of Aim, on Aim - Training II.

### Core takeaways

- Write the questions before you open the material. Two questions carry the pass: why is this important, and how does it relate to that other concept. Never what it is.
- Thirteen things have to be in place first, and three of them are drills the step rests on: the traffic light drill, order control, and biggest chunks first. A missing one means more practice, not a smaller Aim.
- Collect the keywords fast and out of order, then question each one and sketch a structure you refuse to commit to. The pass ends with a backbone and no details.
- It is meant to feel chaotic, confusing, and slow, and each of those has a meaning. Ease is the one feeling to distrust.
- Aim goes before the class when it can, on Sunday for the week. A few minutes buys a superficial Aim and still pays.
- The bar is one sentence: a concept list turns into questions that find chunks and the relationships between them. The quality of those chunks is graded a stage later.

### Essay

Start with what a question does to reading. A page read with no question in mind pours past. The same page read to answer a question arrives into a frame that is already comparing and placing what it finds. That is the whole reason Aim exists. It is why the questions are written before the material is opened and not after. The brain keeps what it can connect and drops what stands alone. A question that forces a connection is a question that makes the answer stick.

Two questions do that work, and they are not interchangeable. Why is this important pushes toward the big picture. It asks what the concept is for and what would break without it, and answering it means judging one thing against another. How does this relate to that other concept exists because the first question cannot escape what you already believe. Importance gets answered through your current view of the topic. Forcing a relationship between two things that seem unrelated is how the view changes. Neither question asks what something is. Comparing two things teaches you what they are, with the purpose attached. A definition learned on its own is gone within minutes.

The step sits on top of skills that were trained separately first. That is why the readiness list is long. Inquiry was trained as a drill, with a red light for questions and a green light for answers. Order control was trained until learning out of the resource's order felt normal. Non-linear notes were trained until a map could hold what the questions found. Aim folds those into one pass and raises the standard on the questions. Without the drills in place the pass overloads, and the honest response is more practice rather than a smaller Aim.

A session has a shape. Keywords first, collected fast across every resource and out of order on purpose, so the headings of the resource do not become the chunks of the map. Then the two questions over the list, top down at first, then wherever curiosity goes. Then a sketch of the structure, held loosely, because effort spent on a structure makes it feel too expensive to break. The pass ends with a backbone of the main chunks and no details on it. Ten minutes before a class buys a superficial version of that, and it still pays.

The feelings are the audit. Chaos, confusion, and slowness are what the thinking feels like from inside. Each maps to a process the pass is meant to run. The feeling to distrust is ease, because an easy pass is usually one where the questions were copied from headings. The bar at the end of the stage is about the step, not the standard. Turning a concept list into questions that find chunks and relationships is the step. Whether those chunks are the right chunks is graded a stage later, and that is the second page.

### Conclusion

So the Aim step is a small procedure with a long prerequisite list. Thirteen skills first, three of them drills. Then keywords fast, two questions each, a structure held loosely, a backbone with no details. It is meant to feel chaotic and slow, and it goes on Sunday for the week or in the minutes before a class. The bar is that the questions come out well every time. The standard those questions are then held to is the next page.

### Before you start

- Run the traffic light drill until the questions come without it.
- Take one chapter and learn it in your own order, jumping where the questions lead.
- Give a whole topic 20 to 30 minutes and stop with three to six chunks and how they relate.

Thirteen things should already be in place before the first Aim. You have felt that the quality of learning depends on the kind of thinking used. You have compared and contrasted instead of learning things one at a time. You have made non-linear notes, and you have felt how the way notes are written helps or blocks that thinking. You have used the traffic light drill, writing questions before reading and answering them after, and you have noticed that different questions open different relationships. You have formed backbones and seen a topic in a different order from the one it was taught in. You have run spaced interleaved retrieval. You have run Kolb's cycle on your own attempts and felt how the depth of a reflection decides how useful the next experiment is. You count progress as marginal gains. You keep your time and tasks in order. If any of these is missing, the rule is to practise the current skills more before moving on. Moderate to high conscious competence in the earlier techniques is what prevents technique errors and overload.

Three of the thirteen are the drills the step rests on. The traffic light drill trains inquiry. Red light means write questions before you read. Green light means answer them. It is a drill and not a system, and once the questioning runs on its own the drill is dropped. Order control trains you to learn in your own order. The order a resource uses is a stranger's guess about what you already know, and you almost always rearrange it, a little or a lot. Biggest chunks first trains the first half hour of a session. In that half hour the only goal is the three to six main chunks of the whole topic and how they relate. Hundreds of pages can be scoped that way in 15 to 30 minutes. Aim replaces the red light of the drill and replaces prestudy basics. The green light stays until Shoot replaces it.

### The session

- Skim every resource for keywords, out of order, about 25 minutes, about 80 percent of them.
- Ask the two questions of each keyword. Never ask what it is.
- Sketch a structure and hold it loosely. Stop with a backbone and no details.

Collect the keywords first, across every resource at once. Video, book, slides, course guide, textbook, and a search engine or a model for what the resources lack. Collect out of order on purpose, so the resource's headings do not become your chunks. Give your own name to a concept or a diagram the resource never names. For a term you cannot even guess at, take a one-paragraph gloss and move on. Keep it fast. A topic the size of a couple of chapters or a few weeks of class has 15 to 30 major concepts. About 80 percent of them in about 25 minutes beats all of them in two hours. When collection runs long, one of three things is wrong. Too many words are being collected, the volume is too high for the current skill, or detail is pulling you in. The collection itself is the easy part and can be handed to a transcription tool or a model. The thinking that follows cannot.

Then the two questions go over the list. Why is this important. How does this relate to that other concept. Top of the list down at first, because the first keywords have nothing to relate to yet. By the third or fourth there is enough on the table, and from then on curiosity chooses the order. No question asks what something is. Comparing two things forces you to learn what they are, with the purpose attached, and a definition learned first and related later is mostly gone within minutes. As relationships appear, sketch possible groupings and hold every one of them loosely. Concept A seems important through B. C is made by A and B and feeds back to B. E leads to D. Later, maybe A and C belong together and lead to B. That sprawl of half-held ideas is what a real Aim looks like. Committing early is the sunk cost trap, because effort spent on a structure makes it feel too expensive to break. The pass ends with a rough backbone of the main chunks and no details on it. Cast the net shallow and wide. A wide net with holes catches many fish. A small tight net catches almost nothing. As skill grows the net covers a whole topic in one pass.

### How it feels

- Chaotic, confusing, overwhelmed, curious, insecure, slow: all expected.
- Easy: the alarm. Check whether the questions were copied from headings.
- Run Kolb's cycle after the session: what happened, what rule, what changes next time.

The pass is meant to feel bad in specific ways, and each feeling has a meaning. Chaotic, from going back and forth between the questions and the material. That is integrative thinking, building the network. Confusing, while an answer is being worked out. That is deep processing. Overwhelmed, while a non-linear structure is being built. That is prioritising and evaluating, the highest order of the work. Curious and effortful. More of the brain is in use. Insecure, while details are skipped and the order is your own. That is the brain forming schemas in its own order and ignoring information until it becomes relevant.

Aim adds three feelings of its own. Overwhelmed by the number of relationships the why question raises. That is a network of possibilities waiting to be refined. Confused because many non-linear structures are possible and choosing takes effort. That choosing is the evaluation the pass exists for. Slow, because each keyword gets a deep look. The depth is what consolidates the learning, and the slowness fades as speed grows. The feeling to distrust is ease. A pass that feels easy is usually a pass where the questions were copied from headings.

### When to aim

- Sunday, for the coming week's material.
- Ten minutes before a class if that is all there is. The largest chunks only.
- After the class if there was no time before, with the class notes as one more resource.

Prestudy is when learning starts. The Bear Hunter System is how it happens. Aim is the first step of the journey. When it happens before the class that covers the topic, it is prestudy. How long the prestudy runs is a decision. Five or ten minutes buys a superficial Aim, the largest chunks and the major relationships only, and even that pays. More time buys a full Aim and part of Shoot. When the class is mandatory, studying everything beforehand wastes the hours you have to sit there anyway. Arrive primed so the class counts, and finish the Aim after it.

Aim after the class, with no prestudy, still works. It is the messiest order and not the one to plan for. The notes made in class become one more resource for the Aim, and they already carry some grouping, so the pass runs a little shorter. Doing the Aim inside the class itself is hard, because that kind of thinking does not fit an intensive lecture. In the standard week, Aim goes on Sunday for the coming week's material.

### The bar

- A concept list turns into high-quality questions that find chunks, sub-chunks, and relationships, every time.
- Focus, the green light, retrieval, and non-linear notes still running underneath.
- Chunk quality is graded next stage. Move on when the step is consistent, not when the chunks are perfect.

The bar for Aim is one sentence. You can consistently use concept lists to create high-quality questions that identify chunks, sub-chunks, and the relationships between them. Four other things are graded at the same point, because they have to keep running while Aim is learned. Focus is consistent and easy to set up in different environments. The green light of the drill still answers the Aim questions, in processed notes that show chunks and relationships. Spaced interleaved retrieval is near unconscious competence, with several interleaving methods and consistent spacing. Non-linear notes are becoming substantially more comfortable.

The bar is about the step and not the standard, on purpose. Whether the chunks are importance-based, whether the reasons are root reasons, whether a map built from these questions has single-node chains or reverse causality, all of that is graded one stage later, when Shoot maps are read. That is the second half of Aim, on the page Aim - Training II.
