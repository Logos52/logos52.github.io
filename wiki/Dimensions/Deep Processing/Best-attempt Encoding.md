# Best-attempt Encoding

Best-attempt encoding is the first pass at organizing a topic. You spend enough time to build the most interconnected, organized picture your current skill can reach, without waiting until you can produce a perfect map. A useful attempt is simple and intuitive, and the grouping should help you remember the material, so it does not become a second list you have to memorize. The groups should be specific enough that this topic would not be confused with another, and intuitive enough that remembering one part of the structure brings back the rest. What counts as intuitive depends on how much of the subject you already know.

You cannot jump from a low skill level to a perfect structure in one sitting. Encoding skill moves slowly, and aiming at a gold-standard organization before you can produce it in a realistic time costs coverage. The practical target is the next level of organization above where you are now. A small rise in how much you retain, such as moving from 40% to 50% of what you studied, reduces later repetition, and that saving compounds across weeks and months.

When encoding skill is still low, retrieval practice does more of the work than encoding, because you cannot yet organize the material well on the first pass. A retrieval prompt such as explaining the topic to a 10-year-old forces simplifying and judging even if the explanation fails, and the failure shows the gap. Later, stronger encoding reduces how much retrieval and repetition you need.

Each isolated detail also needs a width decision. Learning a fact one-to-one protects you for one kind of question about that fact, if retention holds. Learning it a little wider takes longer and protects you for that question plus related questions on the same topic. That wider coverage is described as something like a ratio of 1 question to 1.2 questions, rather than 1 question to 1 question. Two checks guide the choice. One is whether the detail extends something you already know matters, called conceptual relevance. The other is whether testing keeps calling on it, called repeated testing trends. Screening takes about 20 seconds. Testing soon after gives a wider margin of error on that choice. An item filed as isolated will show itself if it keeps being needed, and an item encoded more widely will show a gap if the extra work did not hold.

After encoding, test early at a high-volume level that asks you to generate and connect a large share of the topic. Do this within about one week, so a large structural error is found before it is built into a long map. When a test goes badly, diagnose the layer of the gap and fix that layer. A gap at the higher order, meaning how ideas connect, is different from a lower-order gap about specific facts, or a procedural gap about the skill of executing, such as fluent writing. The organization you drew is only your current interpretation, so a gap is a reason to rearrange it. In a high-volume exam you will not feel you know everything. Confidence should be solid on the core higher-order ideas, fairly strong on the next layers of related concepts and important examples, and allowed to stay mixed on the finest, least connected details.

## Why encoding quality matters in high-volume study

High-volume exams combine a large body of fixed material with material that keeps updating, and only about 1% to 2% of what you cover will be tested, without knowing which part that will be. Past papers show trends in what examiners have focused on, and they still miss questions from unexpected areas. Examiners can also change the pattern, so a plan that uses practice questions without depending on them alone is needed.

In a single-semester exam, better methods can cut study time a great deal. In an ultra high-volume exam, study time falls much less, because there is always more content, more testing, or more depth waiting. What should change is the return on that time. Measure progress by coverage, by gaps found, and by depth, not by how many hours disappeared.

A common research-paper ballpark is about 50% retention one week after learning. Isolated repetition, including flashcards, can raise retention on lower-order facts. In a short course that can be enough, because the total volume is limited. The exam often arrives around the 40th day of the course, and sometimes as late as the 60th day, so the material only has to be held for a couple of months. In an ultra high-volume exam both conditions fail. The volume is high enough that forgetting half of what you cover leaves you unable to finish the set. You can reach a point where all of your time goes to relearning, with none left for new material, and even that is not enough to catch every forgotten item. The exam may also sit around the 300th day, or a year or more after you first learned a topic, and preparation can stretch across one to five years. If retention is low and the hold has to last that long, more hours do not solve the equation. The methods have to produce a retention level and a retention duration that leave time for quantity and depth as well.

You will still do a large volume of practice questions, self-testing, memorization, and flashcards. That work has to sit on a foundation that lets you move through content and keep it, rather than repeating without progress.

## How wide to encode a detail

Hit rate here means the chance that what you are learning will help on the exam. One part of that chance is whether the topic will be tested. The other is how wide you make the box around a single detail, because high volume often comes from an endless set of separate facts.

If you learn a detail one-to-one, and retention and depth are adequate, you are protected when the test asks about that detail in the way you understood it. One fact covers one kind of question. If you learn the same detail a little more broadly, you are protected for that question and for nearby questions on the same topic. That wider protection is pictured as a ratio of about 1 question to 1.2 questions. The wider way takes longer. If both ways took the same time, the wider way would always be better. Because it costs extra time, you choose when a detail is worth the extra width and when a discrete, one-to-one encoding is enough.

Two checks guide that choice, and they fit updating material such as a new regulation or a news item whose test value and required depth are unknown.

Conceptual relevance asks whether the detail extends something you already treat as important. A small legislative change tied to smoking and lung cancer is an isolated fact on its own, and it still belongs with a larger healthcare theme you already know matters. That makes it worth encoding more deeply. Ask how it might be used, including as an example in a short answer or essay, and how it joins what you have already learned. A change to speed limits that does not appear to join anything important has lower conceptual relevance.

Repeated testing trends ask whether the detail has shown up often on earlier tests, or whether, when you test yourself on a topic, you keep needing that fact more often than you expected. The speed-limit item can start as a one-to-one card and later prove useful once city planning and infrastructure testing keeps returning to road incidents. The testing has then shown a conceptual relevance you did not see at first.

When both checks are low, the straightforward move is to put the isolated fact on a flashcard and manage the deck so it does not get lost. When a detail is worth more width, work with it at a slightly higher order. Use it inside short-answer testing, add it onto a map you already have as a worked instance, and look for other connections. A flashcard can still be added as a reminder if you are worried about forgetting it. If the wider work holds, you get the card right more often, so it repeats less often and is less likely to pile into a daily backlog.

Screening and prioritizing a new item as isolated or conceptually relevant takes about 20 seconds. You can batch that filtering on one day of the week or do a little each day. Neither schedule is better. Frequent testing widens the margin of error on the encoding choice. If you filed something as isolated, later testing will show whether that was right. If you tried to integrate it, later testing will show whether you remember it or whether a gap remains.

If flashcards take more than about one and a half hours a day, and you are falling behind, look at how many cards you are making and whether more integration would raise retention. Even about one hour a day is a point to become cautious. For a high-volume exam, needing more than about one and a half hours a day usually means content is accumulating faster than retention can space the cards out. That happens when too many cards are made at once, or when retention is so low that yesterday's cards never leave the pile.

## Matching the structure to your current skill

Encoding skill here means your ability to do higher-order learning. At a high skill level, retention, depth, and detail are high, and efficiency is high. Your current skill sits somewhere on that range, and that position creates a cost-benefit choice.

There is a gold-standard organization, with knowledge so connected and so thoroughly thought through that it would be the best form of the material. If your skill is not there yet, reaching that standard for each piece of knowledge takes too long for the volume you have to cover. It can then be more practical to learn to a lower quality and use the leftover time for more repetition, while skill rises slowly in the background.

A move from a skill of 2 out of 10 to 10 out of 10 can take about 10 months of diligent, consistent effort, or it can take years. You cannot wait until that later skill arrives before you cover content. You still have to move material through, and fill the gap with less efficient methods such as repetition and testing, at the skill you have now.

If you are new to encoding, look for the next level of organization above your current one and push toward that. The retention gain can be modest. Moving from 40% retention to 50% retention is still a 10 percentage point improvement, which is 10% less volume spent on repetition. If that repetition would have been needed many times across the next few years, taking a working figure of about 10 later repetitions, the saving is not 10% once. It is 10% each day and each week, which can add up to dozens of hours a month. Once that next level feels comfortable, push to the one after it. Over a stretch such as two years before an exam, the method can become efficient enough that the same content would later take about six months, while you were still covering material along the way.

The main risk to avoid is spending too long chasing perfect encoding that your present skill cannot produce in a realistic time. Until skill rises, repetition and testing make up the difference. That mix is not ideal, and it is still the practical best available.

How you balance skill-building against exam strategy depends on the time you have. Someone with three years, and more than enough of that time, could spend the first year getting learning skill to a comfortable level before leaning harder into exam strategy. Most people have limited time, so a balanced approach keeps making small gains in skill while also making acceptable progress through the material. Compare yourself with your current level and try to be slightly better than that. Across one to two years, that starts to pay off.

## When retrieval does more than encoding

Higher encoding reduces how much repetition you later need. Early on, skill with learning, encoding, and higher-order thinking is still low. Retrieval strategies then matter more than encoding strategies, because you cannot yet reach the encoding quality you need. You have to accept the problems that come with weak first-pass organization and compensate with enough retrieval.

A high-quality retrieval method is easier to use well than spontaneous high-quality encoding. Being asked to explain something to a 10-year-old forces you to simplify, synthesize, generate, and evaluate together, without already having the skill to think at that level on your own. You may fail to explain it well, and that failure is the point, because it shows the knowledge gap. That is a time-effective way to reach higher mastery before encoding skill has caught up. Once you can organize material in a simplified, structured way without needing a test to force it, encoding matters more, because it sets how much retrieval and repetition you still need. When encoding is high, needed retrieval is low.

The time this shift takes depends on your starting point. A complete beginner, with no real grasp of their learning processes and no conscious methods, can take years to reach a point where learning is no longer a challenge. Most people are not starting from zero and do not need to reach a skill rating of 10 points out of 10 points. A move from about 3 out of 10 to about 7 out of 10 can be enough to change how learning feels, and that stretch takes diligent, consistent months.

## What a best-attempt structure looks like

People often rush the first encoding of a topic and do not hold a high enough standard after they find a gap, so the same problems return. The recommendation is to spend enough time, at your present skill, to encode the information in as interconnected and organized a way as you can. Aim for something simple to follow, something that feels intuitive, something organized, and something that cuts how much memorization the topic requires.

That last check is easy to miss. After you group the material, ask whether the only way to remember the grouping is to memorize the organizational structure itself. If the answer is yes, the structure will be harder to hold, and that is a retention risk. A structure can look simple, intuitive, logical, and organized, and still force that extra memorization. That happens in two ways. Either the grouping is too specific, especially when it leans on terminology, or it is too generic.

A too-specific grouping uses group names that do not call each other to mind. Remembering one name does not bring back the others, so each group label has to be learned on its own. A cluster of names such as inflammatory cytokines shows the problem, because holding one label does not cue the neighboring labels.

A too-generic grouping is so broad, and so often reused, that it is not unique to the topic. In a home-design subject, splitting first into interior and exterior, then furniture against art on the inside and materials against color on the outside, can look intuitive. If the next topic accepts the same split, and the one after that does too, the structure no longer tells you which topic you are in. Memory stops getting help from it. Medicine and history often teach a repeated template, such as taking a history, then examining, then investigating, then forming a management plan, and applying that same sequence to every disease. The template is intuitive, and because it is shared it does not mark out one topic from another.

The useful middle is specific enough to represent this topic uniquely, so you would not confuse it with another, and intuitive enough that you can recall the groups without memorizing each label. In domains you already know well, a fairly specific split can still be intuitive. Someone fluent in learning science can use intrinsic, germane, and extrinsic cognitive load, or procedural, declarative, and conditional knowledge, and remembering one member brings back the others. The same splits would not be intuitive to someone new to those subjects. What is intuitive depends on your knowledge of the field. You want the structure itself to support memory.

A clear illustration of an intuitive structure is the set before, during, and after. Similar sets include up, middle, and down, or left and right. Remembering one member brings back the others, so you only need to hold one small part of the structure for the rest to come to mind.

When you make your best attempt, arrange and group the topic and then ask those two questions of the groups. Would I recall this intuitively, and does this structure uniquely represent this topic? How well you can do that depends on skill, practice, and how well you handle cognitive overload. Those capacities develop over time, so do not try to make the first attempt perfect. Do your best to reach this standard.

## Test early, then diagnose the gap

Frequent self-testing is the process that sits beside this encoding. Start with high-volume methods that sit at a higher order, or that mix orders, and move later toward targeted lower-order testing. Higher-order testing finds big-picture gaps, such as a missing section you thought you understood, or a whole-topic misunderstanding whose connections are superficial or inaccurate. Those errors grow as you add more to the topic, and they are costly to fix late. Spending 20 hours building a mind map, then discovering a major concept that forces you to restructure the whole map, puts those 20 hours at risk. Meeting that concept in the first 30 minutes might have stopped you structuring it that way at all. Filling higher-order gaps early also makes later material easier to place, because relevance is clearer.

High volume here means a method that asks for a large amount of generation and retrieval. A practice exam of 20 questions is fairly targeted, because it probes 20 specific points. A brain dump of everything you know about the topic, in as much detail as possible, is high volume and not targeted. High-volume methods work best early, when larger gaps are likely. After three months of other testing, you might write for 20 minutes before noticing a gap, which is slow. When you use a retrieval method you want to find gaps every couple of minutes. After about three hours of study, teaching the topic from memory, as simply and as logically as you can, to a complete beginner, can surface a gap within the first few sentences. That is useful.

Do this kind of high-volume, higher-order retrieval within the first week, and about every week. In high-volume study you cover a lot in seven days, and waiting longer can turn the session into something like 15 hours. Weekly sessions keep the amount manageable. Later, move toward targeted methods. Those include test questions, practice questions, and quizzes, including quizzes run by AI. They also include focused brain dumps or explanations on concepts you struggle with, and flashcards, which probe a specific point by nature. Flashcards work best in parallel, as a small amount of time every day, such as 30 minutes once a day or 30 minutes twice a day, rather than a large weekly block.

Because new content keeps arriving, you will mix both kinds of testing in the same month. Material from six months ago can be in targeted lower-order testing while last week's material is still in high-volume, higher-order testing. Tracking what you are testing at what level, even in a simple notepad or sheet, keeps that mix visible.

Practice papers sit between high-volume higher-order testing and later targeted lower-order testing, around the second or third retrieval session, at about the three-to-four-week mark or the one-month mark. You can keep using questions after that, and once there is no new material they become a constant way to look for gaps and go deeper, while you also keep up with updating content. Overall trends on past papers can inform the relevance filter, telling you what to learn more deeply and what to learn more shallowly. Do not let those trends be the only guide, and do not build a narrow curriculum from them. It is more efficient to learn things to a decent foundation first, then use the trends to decide what to take even deeper.

Diagnosing a gap starts from the function of each kind of knowledge. Higher-order knowledge connects and integrates ideas. Lower-order knowledge holds specific facts and details. Procedural knowledge lets you carry out a skill, most of the time without thinking through each step. If an essay has a logical flow and relevant examples, yet it does not read fluently and the transitions and wording do not sound academic, the gap is likely procedural. Reviewing the topic again will not fix it. More practice of the writing skill will. If the essay reads fluently and uses relevant examples, yet the overall logical structure and the narrative of ideas feel off, the gap is most likely higher-order. Rereading notes, collecting more examples, or writing more essays on the same topic may not repair a weak grasp of how the ideas connect. The better move is to return to the mind map and challenge the chunk structures, meaning the groups and how they connect, along with the connections and the flow. If the problem is the examples, the concreteness, or the factual detail, the gap is lower-order.

A common story is that you know the topic and are simply bad at writing the answer. If the needed knowledge is already there and the procedural skill is in place, producing the answer should be possible. Failure means a gap exists somewhere. Finding that cause, then matching the fix to it, reduces how much practice you need and shows you where your encoding process leaves holes. If every gap that shows up sits at the higher order, the way you are doing higher-order learning at the start may need work. It may simply be your current skill, which you can accept and improve. The problem is not knowing that, and assuming the issue sits in one layer when the test results point to another.

## Rearranging the map after a gap

Redoing chunk structures and regenerating mind maps after a higher-order gap can feel frightening, as if the first map's failure means you are inadequate. What a mind map holds is just how you currently read that knowledge. It is not itself the source of truth. The organization is always an attempt. You try to make it correct, and you should still assume it is wrong. Finding out that it is wrong is a reason to be glad. Each time you find a fault, refresh the map, and work out a new arrangement, you pay cognitive effort for better knowledge. That exchange is something to repeat often. Paying a small amount of effort once, then carrying a low-quality product forward, leaves you worse off than exchanging effort for clearer knowledge again and again. Once that confusion resolves, the understanding you reach is deeper and clearer.

This is a perspective issue more than a technique issue. Even a very efficient learner finds gaps and rearranges chunk structures. The skilled learner expects those gaps, finds them, and re-evaluates them quickly. A newer learner delays finding them, puts off rearranging to avoid the extra effort, and then takes shortcuts when they finally re-evaluate, trying to finish quickly and skipping the chance to look again. That perspective has to change before more skilled learning opens up.

You are unlikely to reach a point where you feel you know everything, and trying to know everything is not a sensible aim. Think in layers of importance. The core is higher-order knowledge with many applications. You should feel very solid when someone asks about a higher-order principle, about applying a concept, or about a major connection. The next layer is extended concepts, meaning more specific ideas, often conceptual examples, that someone who knows the topic well would be expected to know and relate. You should feel fairly confident there too. Above that sit the first level of details and examples, which are more specific and usually build on the more important concepts. You should feel reasonably sure about most of this layer. The last layer is very fine detail, as specific as the layer below and more random, less obviously connected to anything else. It can include updating items such as news, though news is sometimes closely tied to a concept. Mixed confidence is acceptable here.

In testing, that order is also the order of importance. The core layer is about 30% of the mark, the extended layer another 20%, the first details another 20%, and the remainder the finest layer. The volume runs the other way. The core is less than 5% of the content, the extended layer about 15%, the first details about 30%, and the finest layer the remaining 50%. The hit rate on that top layer is low. There might be a thousand fine details to memorize, and you may only be tested on 10 details, so learning every one to a high standard is not possible. A workable picture is to feel confident enough with perhaps 300 details out of those thousand details that seem a little more important. Among about 500 details that really matter, feel confident on about 450 details. That is how you walk into the exam with the best chance you can reasonably build. Full confidence at every layer is probably not an option. Choosing to chase confidence on the finest details, when time is limited, means choosing less confidence on the layers underneath, which is riskier.

The feeling that you have missed something does not go away. Accept it and plan through it. People often spend their time on fine details because they are afraid of missing them, before the core is solid. They then enter the exam still unsure about those details, and with holes at the base. Prioritize where the largest value sits. You will miss something either way, so choose on purpose which gaps you will allow.
