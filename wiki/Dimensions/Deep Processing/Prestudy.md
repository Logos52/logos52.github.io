# Prestudy

Prestudy is any study you do before a main learning event, such as a class session, lecture, or long dedicated session. It is often brief, though it does not have to be. It gives new information a place to fit, so later detail is less overwhelming and easier to keep.

That early outline is useful. Random information is often harder to hold than information that already feels relevant and meaningful. The brain recognises that importance and is more willing and able to store what it hears, which raises both how much you keep and how quickly you can bring it back. A picture used for this is a jigsaw without a reference image, compared with one that already has the picture and a gap waiting to be filled. Prestudy builds that outline so later pieces have somewhere to go.

A main learning event can arrive as too much, too fast, and sometimes too complicated. Without an outline already in place, that density pushes the brain into overload, meaning more than it can process at once. Information then becomes easier to forget, and knowledge takes longer to build. When events like that happen every day or every week, recovery is hard. People often take shortcuts or give up some of the performance they wanted, and each class can feel more overwhelming than the last.

Prestudy spreads that confusion and load across more than one session. You first build a big-picture sense of why the material matters, and only later take on greater detail. Over time you become less confused. New information is easier to understand because the foundation is stronger, and knowledge is faster and easier to build.

A few principles keep the work pointed at that outline. Group information by how it is related. Stay with the big picture and leave details for later. Spend more time considering why a point matters or belongs than trying only to remember or understand it. Try to create comparisons with what you already know. Study the entire topic rather than single classes or lectures, so you can see the full range of relationships. Aim to be shallow and broad, not deep and narrow.

The process of gathering a large amount of information and sorting it into groups is called chunking. Large groups are chunks, and groups inside groups are sub-chunks.

Prestudy has been done correctly when you hold a clear big-picture grasp of the topic. You have gone across the whole topic or sub-topic, not only one class or lecture. You need not have details or facts memorised yet, because you stayed with the big picture. You should also see how the major ideas relate and could be grouped. You should hold some hypotheses about how the concepts might relate, and feel ready to learn more detail.

Correct prestudy frames the learning and creates organisational structure, and that framing should be one of the first steps in learning. Even if prestudy is so complete that classes become unnecessary, the first stage of a study session should still open as a framing and organising step. If there is not enough time for comprehensive prestudy, doing only this step is still beneficial for efficiency. Correct framing means skipping the smaller details, spotting the main ideas or clusters of ideas, and spotting the key relationships among them, and gaining a wide-view understanding of the topic.

Many people feel insecure about not learning smaller details during prestudy. Committing to details this early turns on isolated, lower-order learning processes that consume time and make the material harder to understand. The main ideas and relationships from this step should be written down if that is viable. If it is not, the mental work of framing is still beneficial. When notes are written, relational and non-linear notes such as mind maps are best, because they can show complex relationships at a level of accuracy that linear notes almost cannot reach.

If you are in a formal curriculum, it is best to do as much prestudy as you can ahead of classes or lectures. Assessments are timed to the end of those classes, so studying ahead leaves more time for later review. Covering content alone is not enough, because you are unlikely to hold the retention, the nuance, and the mastery you need, and many people cannot yet study comprehensively faster than class pace. For high school students whose techniques are moderately efficient, it should be possible to prestudy at least 4 weeks to 6 weeks of material in a single weekend, which would move you beyond class pace. If you have no time, even a 10-minute skim before class is beneficial, as long as the same principles are followed. A common pattern is to prestudy the coming week on the prior weekend, or the coming day that morning. You can prestudy at any point before the main event, depending on the time you have.

Prestudy is easy to confuse with inquiry-based learning, meaning a method that builds knowledge by asking questions and working through answers. Both the early stages of inquiry methods and prestudy try to find basic relationships, create simple networks, and reach a big-picture understanding quickly. They are not the same thing. Prestudy is about when you learn, as a chronological step of study done before an intensive main event. Using the wrong methods in that slot can waste time. Inquiry methods are about how you learn. They are cognitive steps that should begin in prestudy and continue in later sessions until the topic is adequately encoded, meaning stored in a usable structure. A simple inquiry method named the traffic-light system is one version of this, later expanded in a system called the Bear Hunter System. A later aiming step is named alongside it. Those methods are not unpacked here.

If there is no time for prestudy, the inquiry methods have to be used during the main event, which is very challenging, or after it. After the event you try to keep up and form basic structures during it, then consolidate and start priming and encoding properly afterward. That path is usually more viable than doing the inquiry during the event, and still less optimal than a separate prestudy step.

If earlier material was learned with poor technique, the same inquiry approaches should still be used during review or exam preparation. You may have studied the material before without building an appropriate knowledge schema, meaning a mental structure that organises and stores the knowledge. If that stays unchanged, retention stays poor and applying the knowledge, especially to complex problems, stays difficult. In that case you learn the material again, almost as if for the first time. A sign the approach is working is that it is difficult, which indicates a lack of existing schemas, and that knowledge gaps appear regularly, which indicates memory gaps that come from not having a schema.
