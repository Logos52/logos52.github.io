# Order Control

Order control means the learner decides the order in which to learn a topic, in place of the order set by the textbook, the course or the teacher. A person who reorders the material around their own questions understands more on the first pass and spends less time rereading. Many learners keep to the given order anyway, because leaving it feels like skipping work.

## Core takeaways

- The best order depends on what the learner already knows.
- An author cannot know that, so the given order rarely fits.
- Read next whatever answers the question most worth answering.
- Learn the overall shape of a topic before its details.
- Delay a detail until there is something for it to attach to.
- Going back and forth covers the material several times.
- The right order often feels too easy to count as studying.

## How it works

A person understands new information through what they already know, so two people with different backgrounds make sense of the same topic in different orders. An author or teacher lays the material out in the order they expect to suit a typical student. An expert knows the subject as a network of connected ideas with no fixed order. A learner who reads the material from top to bottom tends to memorise its order in place of rebuilding the network, and order control asks the learner to rebuild it, starting wherever their questions point.

- Start at the point of most curiosity or most relevance.
- Ask what each item is, why it matters and how it is used.
- Answer those questions, then link the answer to what came before.
- Let the next question decide where to go next.
  - That can be three pages ahead, or back to page one.
  - If the book does not answer a question, look it up elsewhere.
- Draw analogies from other fields the learner knows.

A physics guide might open with the heading "waves" and then a section on diffraction, and a reader who does not yet know what a wave is gains little from that section. The first move is to find what a wave is and why waves matter, even on a later page, and diffraction then makes sense on the way back.

## Big picture first

The same accurate details are hard to follow in one order and easy in another. A cup described part by part, starting with the width of the opening in its lid, is hard to picture. The same cup described first as something to drink from, and then part by part, is easy. Study material usually starts with the details, so the learner has to find the overall shape of the topic and put it first.

```
given order:   detail, detail, detail ... topic?
own order:     topic -> main parts -> details
```

- Find what the topic is for before learning its parts.
- Learn the main parts before the facts inside them.
- In a complex topic, learn the parts before how they interact.

## The fear of missing something

Most people who try order control worry that skipping around will leave gaps. The questions that link new material to old keep sending the learner back to earlier pages, so each part is met several times from different angles. Each pass misses a little, and the later passes catch most of what the earlier ones missed. Reading every line in order does not prevent gaps either, because a line read without understanding is not kept.

- Several imperfect passes catch more than one careful pass.
- A real gap is something read and not kept.
- Time saved on the first pass pays for filling the gaps.

## Limits

Curiosity is a useful guide, and school subjects do not always raise questions on their own, so the learner has to write questions on purpose. Order control changes the order inside the material a course gives, and the material itself stays. That keeps it apart from open-ended discovery, where the learner also chooses the material, which research finds works poorly for beginners.

- Write questions even when curiosity is low.
- Reorder inside the course material and stay inside it.
- A questioning drill builds the skill until it runs without effort.
