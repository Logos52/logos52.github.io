# Schema

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

A schema is a network of connected ideas in memory, and building one is the main job of learning. Information only becomes lasting knowledge when the brain connects it to what is already there, and connected knowledge is remembered longer and used more flexibly than isolated facts. The brain's processing capacity is limited, so piling on more material or more review does not fix weak learning. A schema forms in three moves. First comes a fast, rough draft built from keywords grouped by feel. New material is then added and linked to the draft, and the map is regularly reorganized so it stays clean. The draft is a hypothesis to revise, not an answer, and the work happens while learning, not in review afterward.

## Core takeaways

- Knowledge is non-linear, and the learner's job is to rebuild the connected structure that text, slides and video flatten into a line.
- Relevance, from the brain's point of view, means connection, and connected information is retained longer and more easily.
- More reading, rewriting and flashcards do not repair weak processing. They only slow the leak.
- Build the big picture first and memorize isolated details last.
- Draft fast and shallow, add and connect as you learn, and reorganize the map as better structure appears.

## What a schema is

A schema is the multi-relational, integrated structure an expert holds for a topic. Knowledge in this form is non-linear, but it reaches a learner in linear form, as a sequence of sentences, slides or video. The learner's task is to undo that flattening and rebuild the network the material came from. Even when a test asks for linear answers, the structure is best encoded in its connected form first and practiced in linear form afterward.

## The conversion bottleneck

Turning information into a schema runs through a narrow channel. The brain can handle only a limited amount of material at a time. A single word gives too little to think about, while a hundred pages at once overwhelms. Lectures, slides, textbooks and videos all arriving together create a bottleneck, so intake has to be kept to relevant material in an amount the learner can actually think through.

## Why more volume does not fix it

When processing is weak, memory leaks and material is forgotten again and again. The usual response is more of the same, with more rereading, more rewritten notes, more flashcards and more review. That is like bailing a leaking boat without patching the hole. The largest drain on study time is relearning what was forgotten, so remembering correctly the first time frees a great deal of time. The fix is to repair processing at its root.

## What makes a piece stay

A piece of information stays when it is connected. To the brain, relevance means connection, and integrated knowledge is retained longer and more easily than isolated facts. Lower-order learning leaves facts separate, so a learner can recite them or explain one concept at a time. Higher-order learning ties concepts together, which makes it possible to solve complex problems that involve several ideas and to write with flow and logic.

## Construction: the first draft

The first draft is fast, shallow and wide. It starts with a list of keywords collected by skimming every resource, including slides, textbook, video and notes. Collecting about 80% of the major concepts in around 25 minutes beats chasing all of them for two hours, and most topics hold only 15 to 30 major concepts. Gathering keywords in a shuffled order, and inventing names for unlabeled ideas, keeps the draft from copying the order in which the material was taught.

The keywords are then grouped by feel, with similar words placed together. Each group is a hypothesis. Unknown words get only a quick check, enough to decide where they belong, not enough to explain them. Accuracy is not the goal at this stage. A rough backbone of the main chunks is enough, and holding it loosely prevents defending a structure just because effort went into it. A wide net with holes catches more than a small, tight one, because broad priming gives new information more points to connect to.

## Adding and connecting

With the draft in place, learning adds to it. Each group gets a simple label in everyday words, such as outcomes, problems or skills, so that thinking about relationships takes no extra effort. Two questions drive the linking. One asks why a concept is important, and the other asks how it relates to concepts already on the map. As the learner reads and watches in depth, new concepts go onto existing branches, wording gets simpler and visuals replace text that is wordy or easy to forget. A useful limit is two to four keywords per group, with subgroups formed once a group grows larger.

## The gatekeeper

Adding without cleaning leads to a mess. When relationship arrows start crossing from one side of the map to the other, the grouping is at fault, because related items were placed apart. Reorganizing is the check that keeps the schema usable. Getting the groups right pays off in clean relationships, and a messy map is a signal to regroup.

## Reorganization

Reorganization is ongoing, not a final step. During the draft and while learning, a learner moves items, splits groups and creates new ones as better structure appears. First guesses about why something matters get corrected as understanding grows. Cleaning up a messy map later, for example on a weekend, also works as a form of interleaving, since it forces the learner to revisit the material from a new angle.

## Formation is not review

The schema forms during processing, not in review sessions afterward. Once the brain is primed, new information lands with more meaning, so the learner keeps more and relearns less. If some later step is what makes material make sense, that step belongs first. Writing down everything a teacher says so as to sort it out later pushes the real work onto a future self and is a sign of network-poor learning.

## When nothing attaches

Sometimes new material feels irrelevant and nothing seems to stick. The fix is to ask what knowledge or link would make it relevant, and then learn that connection first. Building the missing link breaks the ceiling of ineffective learning.

## Signs and limits

A few signs show whether schema building is working:

- A draft that takes much longer than planned usually means the learner was pulled into detail too early. A rough guide is about 15 minutes of priming per hour of content.
- Answering why something matters with a single reason and moving on is a sign the linking was skipped, since most things matter for several reasons of different weight.
- Transcribing everything signals lower-order, network-poor learning.

The method also has limits. It does not shorten the time spent consuming material, since reading and watching still happen in depth, and the savings come from retaining more. Deep processing ability also varies. Some people already have strong habits from early life and succeed with mediocre technique, so copying their technique does not work for someone who lacks the ability.

## In the study system

Schema building sits at the center of a simple model of learning. Information comes in, the brain processes it, and processing produces two results, memory and mastery. The learner controls only the input and the processing, and processing is where the schema forms. The draft step is the Aim stage of the Bear Hunter System. The whole method works only alongside time management, a growth mindset about mistakes, regular practice and spaced, interleaved retrieval.

## How to practice this

1. Skim every resource for a topic and collect keywords out of order, aiming for most of the major concepts in about 25 minutes.
2. Group the keywords by feel and label each group in plain words.
3. For each concept, ask why it is important and how it relates to what is already on the map.
4. While learning in depth, add detail onto existing branches and keep each group to two to four keywords.
5. When arrows start crossing the map, regroup.
