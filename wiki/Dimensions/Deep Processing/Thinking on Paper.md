# Thinking on Paper

Thinking on paper is using the page as extra working memory so relations can be seen and moved, not as a record of what was read. The few things a head can hold at once stay free for those relations, because the intermediates sit on the page. Good notes reflect the thinking, not a transcript of the source.

## What it looks like, and what it is not

On a page that is doing this, concepts land as nodes, relationships get drawn, dependencies get put outside the head, and a messy structure gets rewritten into a cleaner one. Intermediate thoughts sit there so working memory — the few things that can be held at once — can stay on the relations. [[wiki/Concepts/Memory Handling|Memory Handling]] owns that window; this page only borrows it. Maps, analogies, or a compressed summary are all legal forms. A concept gets clearer by stating it, elaborating it, giving an example, and making an analogy when one helps.

The page is allowed to be ugly. A first pass that tries to come out finished is the pass that does not start.

What it is not:

- Writing everything down.
- Capturing a perfect transcript.
- Highlighting or copying without evaluation.
- Optimising for quantity of notes.

Those four are the cheap moves [[wiki/Concepts/The Shortcut Problem|The Shortcut Problem]] refuses.

## Inspectable

Once the thoughts are outside, they can be inspected. Structure becomes visible. Gaps show. Overload shows. Connecting versus collecting becomes a thing that can be seen rather than guessed. That inspectability is a control move, which is why it sits under [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation/Metacognition - The Control Layer|Metacognition: The Control Layer]], not under a preference for a note style. It should feel like making cognition inspectable. The page holds more relationships than working memory can manage alone.

Good signs are specific. Vague thoughts become objects that can be moved and tested. Contradictions become visible. The next question appears from the page. The writing changes the thought rather than merely recording it.

## Dumping, and when to quit

Dumping is the failure on the other side of those signs. The page accumulates words but does not improve the decision, the structure, or the question. A capture-only lecture is where this happens most often: the hour is stored, the thought is not. A phone-sized surface fights the workbench for a different reason, the one [[wiki/Concepts/The Screen Inferiority Effect|The Screen Inferiority Effect]] names — the surface is too small to hold relations where they can be moved. That is a fact about surface, not a ban on devices.

The page has to be used, not filed. The modern count on what a head can hold at once is about four chunks; that figure is why the offload is worth the paper, and it is a fact about working memory, not a finding about this move. The stuck or overload signal that says to offload lives on [[wiki/Concepts/Cognitive Load & What Mental Effort Is Trying to Cue|Cognitive Load & What Mental Effort Is Trying to Cue]].

The quit signal is the same as the diagnostic. Words accumulate, and the decision, the structure, or the question does not move. What should be checkable after a session: a contradiction or a next question visible on the page that was not visible in the head.

The session worked if the writing changed the thought. A page that stored the hour did not.
