# ReCOVer System

Writing fails at the flow of ideas — sentences spent inventing a plan still waiting to be retrieved, cut, and ordered. Perspectives arrive in random order, conclusions sit without a path, and points arrive without instances. The four verbs — retrieve, cut, order, then write — are the plan built before any sentence.

## The four verbs

Write-from-chunks, in that order. Around the question stem, a small map of the points that actually serve it. Those chunks arranged so each one hands the next. Each chunk expanded into its supports — a concept, an instance, a quote, a tension, a reading. That skeleton turned into bullets in writing order, then the bullets into sentences. The four verbs are this sequence named.

Recall drafts a flow of main points from connected knowledge. The question stem selects which structure is relevant. Difficulty here signals shallow [[Higher-Order Learning]] of the topic: if a structure cannot be retrieved, it is not there. The exam default is up to ten minutes on Recall, a working figure of this system, not a finding.

Chunk cuts the weaker or less relevant points and checks that the remaining flow is the one a reader can follow. The default is one-directional progression with supports under each chunk, and roughly three or four main chunks for a timed essay. One-directional is a readability default, not a law — some arguments have to turn — and three-to-four moves with the length table. This is [[Skin|Skinning]] the map. Up to five minutes.

Order linearises: introduction, then each main point unpacked with its supports. The introduction states the main ideas and how the essay will move between them, so the path is visible before any unpacking starts. Up to five minutes.

Once Order has set the path, Verbalise expands the skeleton into sentences. With a clean plan this is often the light step; if it is hard, the limiter may be language, not planning. When the skeleton is long, bullets in order come first, then sentences. Timed short essays usually skip that extra move — fewer points, and the remaining minutes go to sentences and a last read. If Verbalise is hard and the first three verbs were clean, the limiter is language.

Deep encoding already produced the fluency. This page linearises a retrieved map under a clock. [[Bear Hunter System]] is the map of ideas; [[WPW]] is the other linearisation, reteach aloud, whole then parts then whole. The same move as a logic layer before the detail layers — the sequence [[Layers of Learning]] names.

## Reading the plan

What separates a followable essay from a muddy one is the time spent making the logic easy to walk. That step is routinely under-spent. The writer then wastes minutes inventing structure mid-clause.

Chunk count drives length. The conversion is a planning heuristic, not a law. High school work sits around three to five chunks and roughly two thousand words; early university around five to seven and three thousand; late university around five to seven and ten thousand — already a paper, and the heuristic still orients. Thesis and publication run five to ten chunks and thirty thousand words and up.

A plan that cannot be followed on the page produces an essay that cannot be followed, and the writing itself goes slower and less accurately. Weak plans almost never yield strong prose. A messy outline is not something the sentences will repair later. Some writers do discover structure in prose — and they are slower, which is why this page is for timed work where that luxury is gone.

Essay failure splits in two. One half is not knowing what to say, in what order, or how the points connect. The other half is turning an already-ordered skeleton into sentences that read. Most stuck writing is the first half being misread as the second. The check for the first half: could these points be explained in conversation, source closed? If not, the gap is the map, not the wording.

Lacking evidence, or waffling, means Recall was thin, or the topic was learned too superficially. Recalled points that will not support themes means weak chunking, or too little relationship-based learning. Not knowing where the essay is going means inadequate ordering, often downstream of weak chunking. Sentences that will not flow despite a good plan mean language mastery.

Twenty minutes of a timed paper go to the plan before a sentence — ten, then five, then five — on a plan that looks too simple. The remaining minutes are spent writing, not inventing structure mid-clause.

The method is wrong when the bottleneck is language, not structure: more practice and interleaving, not another plan. It is wrong for untimed exploratory drafts whose point is to find the structure in the prose. It is useless if the topic was never encoded — Recall will fail, and the right repair is the map, not a longer outline. The four-letter name and the length table are house packaging.

If Recall will not produce a structure, planning stops and the topic comes back. If the plan is clean and the sentences still will not come, this page is finished. If two timed attempts produce a tidy plan and a wandering essay, the structure is still being invented in the sentences.

A usable plan is one a stranger could write from: three or four chunks, one direction, supports under each, an opening that states the path. If the plan cannot be followed without new decisions, it is not a plan yet.

A followable plan is the fix. The remaining minutes go to sentences rather than to inventing structure.
