# Layers of Learning

Layers of learning is a way to give incoming information a structure. You always begin at the logic layer and then work outward. The logic and concepts layers stay hypothetical, meaning they can be revised at any point as the picture improves. Most resources are not arranged this way. Detail usually arrives far too early. A related method, order control, is what lets you rearrange the sequence in which the brain meets and organizes information. The layers tell you what that rearranged sequence should be.

The first and innermost layer is logic. It is the backbone of the topic, made of the largest ideas, the main groups, and the most important relationships. Those relationships are built by asking why something matters and how it connects, until the logical structure is visible. Skipping this step is like trying to raise a house before the foundation is in.

The second layer is concepts. These are the core details and ideas that form most of the subject-specific understanding. They sit on the first layer and are built by asking how something happens and what it is. People who skip the logic layer often struggle here.

The third layer is important details. Details are particular facts that often have to be memorized. Some of them help you understand the concept they belong to, and those are important. They make an abstract idea more concrete. Others are arbitrary. The test is whether knowing the detail helps you understand the concept attached to it. If it does, it is important. If it does not, it is arbitrary.

The fourth and outermost layer is those arbitrary details. They can look random or unnecessary, and they are still in the curriculum or on the exam. They are learned because assessments require them, and they have to be memorized until, much later, they start to make sense on their own.

Of the total volume that has to be studied, only about 10 percent is conceptual and about 20 percent is important details. That leaves about 70 percent as arbitrary details. Studying from beginning to end therefore means trying to hold a great deal of information that has no meaning yet. Starting with the deep, foundational layers raises the relevance of what comes later, and that can turn details that looked arbitrary into details that now have a place.

The layers are not fixed. How relevant a detail feels depends on whether a structure already exists to give it relevance. Weak work on the logic and concepts layers produces more details that seem irrelevant. The way to shrink the fourth layer is to improve the first two layers. Even a tree with 10,000 leaves may have only five or six main branches, and time spent on the branches is what lets the leaves appear more naturally. As a rough rule it is worth investing extra time in clear first and second layers. That changes how long the third and fourth layers take, which is where a lot of time is otherwise lost.

Learning in layers also helps with cognitive load, meaning the mental effort of holding and working with new information. Large volumes become less overwhelming and more relevant, retention improves, and mastery goes deeper. Understanding why that happens is useful because it lets you diagnose your own process when it is not working, which is part of steering your own learning.

## Open questions

- How does asking whether a detail helps you understand its concept change what gets memorized first?
- How does leaving the logic layer unfinished change how large the pile of arbitrary details becomes?
