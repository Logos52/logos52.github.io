# Shoot

Shoot is the second step of the Bear Hunter System. It is a stronger version of the green-light phase of the traffic-light system, where you answer the questions you asked. At this point you can replace that earlier system entirely by using Aim and Shoot together.

The two steps do different work. During Aim you prime the brain to find meaningful relationships by asking questions about importance. During Shoot you satisfy that curiosity. You clear up how ideas connect, add detail, look for more links, decide on groups and links, and concentrate on building the map.

Not every question needs a perfect answer. At times you will pose questions so complex or advanced that a full answer is not realistic. The learning occurs when you attempt to answer, which helps reinforce the relationships.

If the Aim step was done well, Shoot tends to go smoothly. Shoot gets hard when leftover habits or lower-order, linear learning interfere. Those habits can impair order control, meaning the sequence you choose to meet material in. The non-linear notes used in Shoot help anchor your thoughts and keep them focused. Shoot is also described as a more effective form of note-taking, because it stacks several earlier skills into one step. It replaces both the green-light phase and an earlier notes-and-processing step.

A common objection is that Aim and Shoot take too much time. The time spent building the map creates more connections and then saves more time. The real waste described here is returning to memorising pages of text with no way out. Building maps also speeds up a great deal as your skill grows, which makes learning quicker over time.

Another objection is that Aim and Shoot are too difficult. People can become uneasy because certainty about what they know stays out of reach for a long stretch. The sense of certainty traditional methods provide is described as a false sense of learning that does not match the knowledge you are actually forming. That illusion leads to inconsistent performance and results, which then lead to frustration, demotivation, and performance anxiety. Making mistakes and noticing them is a central part of learning and getting better. If the work does not feel difficult, that is treated as a warning that the technique is probably not being applied to the correct standard.

A third worry is missing something. The claim here is that you will miss more by skipping a mind map and ignoring the layers. Mind maps organise learning to match how the brain learns. You also need not fear skipped details while you move around and apply order control, since running the whole Bear Hunter System together with spaced interleaved retrieval is what keeps those gaps from lasting.

- How does waiting a long time for certainty change whether the map you are building feels like real knowledge?
