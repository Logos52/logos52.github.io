# Shoot

Shoot is the step in a study session where the learner reads the material to answer questions they wrote beforehand, and records the answers as a map of connected ideas. It is the second of three steps in the Bear Hunter System, where Aim writes the questions, Shoot answers them and Skin cleans up the result. Done well, it replaces notes copied from a textbook with notes already organised around why each idea matters.

- Read to answer questions written in advance.
- Ask what each idea is, why it matters and how it relates.
- Record relationships, and treat facts as what follows from them.
- Write notes in the order the questions set.
- Think first, then write, in few words and pictures.
- Build the main structure before any detail.
- Effort and some confusion mean the step is working.

## How it works

The questions from the Aim step give the reading a direction, so the learner goes where the questions point instead of from the first page to the last. Each answer should be logical, meaning the learner sees why it has to be that way, and connected, meaning it is tied to other answers on the page. Facts learned this way follow from relationships and need little separate memorising. Not every question gets a full answer, and the attempt to answer still builds the connections.

- Read a little, then flip back and forth in the source.
- Write a short note and connect it to what is already there.
- Let the next question decide where to read next.
- Return to Aim when the reading loses direction.
- Repeat until the material is covered.

## How to take the notes

Opening a textbook tends to pull a learner back into reading line by line and copying. Notes built on a few rules keep the thinking on track, and each rule stops a different way that ordinary notes fail. The learning happens in the thinking before anything is written, since copying is fast and produces almost no learning.

- Processed: ask the three questions before writing anything.
- Relational: show how each new item links to earlier ones.
- Visual: cut words, use symbols, arrows and rough sketches.
  - An abstract sketch works better than an accurate drawing.
- Freehand: draw the map by hand, with room to move things.
- Own order: follow the questions wherever they lead in the source.
- Efficient: after each study block, check what can be recalled and used.

Notes that follow the textbook's order line for line are a sign that the step was skipped. A map that looks like every other map also loses its own shape, which is one more cue for memory.

## Build in layers

The most common mistake is going straight to the details. A map built in layers keeps each detail tied to something larger. Details that do not connect to anything go on flashcards and stay off the map.

```
layer 1   backbone: the main flow of ideas
layer 2   concepts that hang off the backbone
layer 3   details that pin concepts down
leftover  unconnected facts -> flashcards
```

- Set out the major groups and their links first.
- A chain of single items means the source's order was copied.
- The groups should make sense on reflection, with nothing memorised yet.

## Barriers

Most learners who drop Shoot say it takes too long, or that it is too hard, or that they fear missing something. Each of those has an answer. With practice, Shoot becomes quicker than copying notes.

- Fast reading that builds no links teaches nothing.
- Effort and confusion are part of learning.
- Study so easy that it causes drowsiness is not working.
- Gaps get picked up in later passes and in review.
- The method suits everyone, since no one has a fixed learning style.
