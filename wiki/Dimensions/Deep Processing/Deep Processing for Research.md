# Deep Processing for Research

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Research is learning with two extra demands. Nobody hands you the material, and what you learn has to come out in a set structure such as a literature review or a paper. The processing itself is the same higher-order thinking used for any subject, pointed at journal articles. Each source is a puzzle piece. The job is to see where it fits in the field, what it agrees or disagrees with, and what it leaves out. Reading this way floods you at first, and the overwhelm is a true picture of how tangled the subject is, so you organise it until the same mass feels simple. That organised picture is expertise, and it shows later as clear writing. Because every later step depends on that picture, the cost per paper falls fast once it exists, and most writing trouble traces back to a picture that was never built. Systematic reviews are the best starting point but still carry the blind spots of whoever wrote them. AI tools help with a first sketch and with spotting perspectives you missed, but they can't do the placing for you without stopping you from becoming an expert.

## What makes research different from study

Most professional learning is self-directed, and research is the extreme case. A blog post, a course, a textbook and a journal article all feed the same process of gaining knowledge. The one thing unique to research is that the knowledge has to come out in a fixed form. Writing and presenting are separate skills on top of that, each deep enough to need long practice and feedback of its own.

The thinking research needs sits high on the ladder of thinking levels. Memorising and understanding can carry a student through school and often through an undergraduate degree. Applying knowledge to simple problems is the middle level. Analysing and evaluating are where thinking becomes truly higher order, and they are only required when the work is competitive or the volume of information is very large. Research is both, so there is almost no point in it where lower-order thinking is enough.

## Reading every source as a puzzle piece

Higher-order thinking here means integration, the big picture, and a constant search for relationships between ideas. Each fact you take in, whether from an article, a supervisor, a video or a commentary, matters only for where it sits in the larger image. Understanding an abstract is only the first step. After that, you keep pressing on it with four questions.

1. What does this finding mean for the rest of the field?
2. Where does it fit with everything else you have read?
3. Does it support or contradict what you know, or what other authors say?
4. What is missing from the picture it paints?

The last question matters most in young fields. Learning science has large gaps, sometimes whole ways of thinking about a problem that nobody has explored, while medicine has a much larger settled body of work. Every article is one person's view, and that person can have gaps in understanding like anyone else.

## Overwhelm, then organising

The first serious attempt at this usually means a literature review with many tabs and PDFs open and a sense that nothing fits together. Don't run from that feeling, because it is an accurate picture of the subject. Giving up on fitting it together is the mistake. The task is to take the overwhelming mass and organise it until the same information feels simple and easy to think about freely, and reaching that point is what expertise is.

How you organise depends on how practised you are. Early on, the aim is to push past understanding toward connecting and comparing, using whatever level of nonlinear notes you can manage, even a few linked points. As the notes grow you hit a point with too many lines and arrows to hold, which is a good sign because your brain is starting to see the connections. From there you clean up and prioritise by judging how strong each connection is, and the thinking grows more organised and concise. At that stage you spend far more time organising than reading, sometimes 10 to 20 minutes of pure thinking before you read on, which feels slow at first and is normal.

## Why the cost per paper falls

The slow start pays off because each paper joins a growing network. If the first source is a big-picture review, it exposes you to most of the field's ideas. By around the 10th article each paper adds only one or two new details, and a paper that took four hours at the start can take 10 minutes by the 15th. Now and then a paper brings a truly new perspective, and you stop to absorb it and reorganise. Overwhelm and reading speed keep rising and falling in this way for the whole project.

The habit takes time to become automatic. Four to six months of committed practice is typical before it feels second nature, and some people take two years or more when life interrupts the practice. A literature review read and marked up for three to four months under a weak system could, with a good one, take one or two weeks and come out better.

## Writing is the same process in reverse

Reading turned linear text into a nonlinear network and then into an organised structure. Writing runs the same path backwards. You unpack the organised picture into a nonlinear plan, often a mind map. This time the map serves expression, since the learning is already done. Then you turn that plan into a bullet scaffold and elaborate it, reordering a few points as you write.

The early steps are the hardest and the most valuable, so a problem you feel at the writing stage almost always starts further up. If you are an experienced writer and still don't know what to write or in what order, the ideas lack a flow. Ideas with no flow are ideas that were never organised, and ideas you can't organise point to missing expertise. Missing expertise in turn means the reading never reached the higher-order network. Working back up this chain shows where to fix things, and once you clear the earliest limit, the later steps usually become easy. Expertise also shows when it is working. The citations stop feeling random, and dates and authors stick because each one fits the story.

## What a body of evidence can and can't tell you

Evidence comes in levels, with anecdotes and single case reports at the bottom. Observational studies sit above them. They sort people by what they already do, and researchers use them when an experiment would be unethical or impractical. Randomised controlled trials come next, and a double-blind trial, where neither participants nor investigators know who got the real treatment, is the gold standard for a single study. Meta-analyses pool many lower-quality studies, and systematic reviews survey the whole state of the research, so a systematic review is the best single place to start.

Even a systematic review only reflects the best evidence so far, seen through the lens of the people who wrote it. Spaced repetition is an example of the risk. From around 1970 to 2010 most studies tested random word lists minutes after learning. Few asked how much time the repetition took or which students could keep it up, so the method looked better supported than it was. Studies from the last 10 years have largely found it weaker than once believed. A researcher's job is to create knowledge that didn't exist before, which means treating every meta-analysis and review as having a gap. You only find the gap if you believe it is there and go looking for it.

## Using AI without losing your own expertise

Language models learn from what they can reach. Many journal articles sit behind paywalls the models can't get past, so their picture leans toward the most mainstream view. For a literature review the output is usually shallow or simply made up. They are useful for a first lay of the land in a new topic, as an advanced search, and for rewording or structuring a few points. The most useful job is spotting gaps. You describe how you currently think about a topic and ask which research perspectives you are missing. The answer gives you new terms to look up, which can save weeks of reading.

If a model does the placing better than you, you are not yet good at it, and handing it over keeps you that way, because the skill only grows by doing the processing yourself. Expertise has to live in your own head, and an expert gets far more out of the same tools.

## Choosing what to read first

A good order starts with a few big-picture systematic reviews, or with a quick AI summary when the field is brand new. Contrary perspectives come next, so the overview is broad. By then certain authors and studies keep recurring. In most fields only five to 10 people are really pushing the frontier, and many others run small follow-ups or replications of their work. Reading those few authors' publication histories therefore covers most of what matters. Researchers are usually happy to email you a PDF if you ask. Asking AI to summarise a paper's key ideas before you read it risks biasing you, because you want to reach your own conclusions.

## Open questions

- How does a reader decide which connection between two sources is the stronger one?
- How does a field's age change what kinds of gaps a reader should expect to find?
- How does the picture of a field change when a source brings a truly new perspective?
