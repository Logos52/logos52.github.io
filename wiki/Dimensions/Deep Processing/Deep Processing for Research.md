# Deep Processing for Research

Deep processing for research is a way of reading a field of published papers so that each paper is placed in a picture of the field while it is read: what it supports, what it leaves out, and which open question it answers. Read this way, the reader knows when to stop collecting papers, and the review written at the end makes an argument rather than listing summaries.

## Core takeaways

- Place every paper in the field while reading it.
- Organising papers already read beats reading new ones.
- Overload on first contact with a field is normal and passes.
- Ask what a field never measured, as well as what it found.
- The first paper takes hours and later ones take minutes.
- A language model can check a picture, but should not build it.

## How it works

Each paper goes through six moves in order, and the last move only works once a picture of the field exists, so the first few papers get only the first five. Papers are sorted into camps that agree with each other and compared directly, and the sort is redone when a new paper breaks it. Work runs in passes of ten to twenty minutes, since overload comes and goes. The first paper takes hours because there is nothing to place it against, and each later paper takes less.

- Understand the paper's claim.
- Locate it in the picture of the field so far.
- Compare it with papers already placed, for who agrees on what.
- Find what it does not see: population, time cost, transfer.
- Judge it by the kind of evidence it is.
- Build from a gap: an open question the field left.

The order of reading matters as much as the moves. In a new field, start with broad reviews, then the papers that disagree with them, then the methods, authors and gaps that keep coming up, and only then draw the picture and set a focused question. Within one topic, open sources from most support to least: diagrams, an encyclopedia article, a textbook or review, and the research papers last, once there is somewhere to put them. [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Prestudy|Prestudy]] does the same for one session, and the [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Bear Hunter System|Bear Hunter System]] does it over a whole field.

## What a field can see

Kinds of evidence differ in strength. A single case is the weakest, then studies that observe without changing anything, then trials that change something for one group and compare it with another, and the strongest are reviews that pool many trials. A randomised double-blind trial is one where neither the participants nor the people measuring know who got what. Pooling reaches many people, and it cannot add what no trial measured, so a thing never measured has not been shown to be absent.

- A thirty-person trial cannot speak for a whole population.
- A review summarises the field through the field's own framing.
- In a thin field, that framing shows.
- Name each gap: which population, what time cost, what transfer.

An example: the owner reads about forty years of spaced-repetition studies, ending around 2010, as tests of short word lists over short delays. They say little about time cost or about long retention of complex knowledge. Those studies agree strongly partly because they tested such a narrow range.

## Where it fails

Most failures come from writing or narrowing before the picture exists. A pile of separate summaries, notes that keep growing and a picture that stays vague are all signs of the same thing. A review with no narrative is usually weak organisation, and the fix is to reorganise what was already read. Sometimes the writing skill itself is the limit, which is a separate and slow fix.

- One summary per paper, with nothing placing them together.
- A generated summary reads clearer than the reader's own.
- A question narrowed early makes every later paper confusing.
- The skill takes four to six months of practice to build.
- It does not pay on a short one-off project.

## Working with a language model

A language model is useful at the edge of a picture the reader already holds. It can find what is missing, check a suspected gap, and suggest keywords, contrary positions and authors. It is limited to what it can find, so paywalled work is missing, mainstream work is over-represented, and it can invent citations that look real.

- Good: state your understanding and ask what is missing.
- Good: a short orientation in an unfamiliar domain.
- Poor: summarising the field or writing the review.
- Poor: reading its summary before the paper itself.
- Check every citation it gives.
- A citation-network mapper suggests papers and makes no claims.
- Authors usually send a paywalled paper on a polite request.

## Keeping references

Maps and other non-linear notes lose track of who said what, so references need a system of their own. Citing the right paper at the right moment comes from knowing how the papers relate and from using them often in writing. Three ways of keeping them are in use, and each suits a different kind of work.

| Way | What it is | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Write as you go | A referenced piece, extended every two or three papers | One piece of writing |
| Second brain | One tagged page per paper in a linked notes app | Reuse across projects |
| Sort by job | Core, applied, context, counterpoint, methods | Someone deep in one field |

## How to tell it is working

The clearest test is explaining the field to a colleague in eight minutes without notes. A new paper should slot into the picture on contact, or land in a gap that already has a name. Three smaller signs go with it.

- The camps are visible.
- The gaps are specific.
- Questions get sharper with each paper.
