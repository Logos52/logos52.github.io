# Shoot - Training I

## Reasoning

### Abstract

Shoot Training I is the second pass of the Bear Hunter System learned as a drill. You read the material to answer the questions Aim wrote, and every answer lands on a map. An answer counts when it is logical, so you can see why it has to be that way, and interconnected, so it joins what is already on the map. If the Aim was good, the shooting is easy. What makes it hard is the old habit that takes over the moment the book opens: reading front to back and writing sentences. Seven note rules exist to make that habit hard to fall into. Shoot replaces the green light of the traffic light drill and replaces the old notes, and from here the drill is dropped and Aim and Shoot run alone. The bar is that Aim and Shoot connect without a seam, with high chunking and accurate relationships, at an effort that has become moderately low. What the map is then held to, layers, root reasons, and the map-reading checks, is on Shoot - Training II.

### Core takeaways

- Read the material to answer the questions Aim wrote, and put every answer on a map. An answer counts when it is logical and joins what is already there.
- If the Aim was good, shooting is easy. What makes it hard is the old habit of reading front to back and writing sentences, and the seven note rules exist to block it.
- Add the rules one at a time: visual, processed, relational, freehand, reflective, efficient. A map that follows the textbook's order is the sign something went wrong.
- Effort and confusion are the brain working. Drowsiness is the alarm. Certainty comes late on purpose.
- Shoot before the class if there is time, during it in short lines, and after it to finish. Skin takes the map at the end of the week.
- The bar is one sentence: Aim and Shoot connect without a seam, with high chunking and accurate relationships, at moderately low effort. The map's quality is graded on the next page.

### Essay

Start with what the questions did. Aim left a list of questions and a rough backbone, and it left the brain wanting the answers. Shoot is where that want gets spent. Each thing the material offers gets one of a few treatments. It answers a question, it goes under the chunk it belongs to, it adds a relationship that changes the map, or it gets marked as unresolved. Nothing is copied. The order is the order of the questions, not the order of the pages, so the session flips back and forth and looks disorderly to anyone watching. From inside it is the least chaotic way to read, because each passage arrives with its job already known.

Two words decide what an answer is worth. Logical means its origin is visible, so it can be rebuilt rather than held. Interconnected means it arrived attached, and the number of attachments is what tells the brain to keep it. Both come from the same habit: the answer to why something is important is nearly always a link to something already on the map, and drawing that link is the work. A fact that arrives alone is the fact that goes first.

The note rules are there because the pass is fragile. The moment a book opens, the old way of reading and writing is waiting, and it feels productive. Fewer words and more drawing. Nothing written that skipped the three questions. Relationships instead of items. A pen instead of a keyboard. Your order instead of the book's. A check afterwards that what was written was kept. Each rule blocks one door the old habit uses. They are added one at a time, because a rigid habit does not change in a day.

The feelings are the audit here as they were in Aim. Effort and confusion are the brain working, and the thing to distrust is ease, especially the kind that makes you drowsy. The pass withholds certainty for a long time, and that is the honest version of learning. The certainty that rereading hands out early is the illusion behind inconsistent results and exam anxiety. A learner who finds the pass easy is usually not running it to standard.

The bar at the end of the stage asks for the two passes to connect without a seam, at an effort that has become moderately low. The habits underneath keep running. Kolb's cycle is run well, because from here on the reasons a result is off are fine-grained. The bar is about the pass. Whether the map has the right chunks, in the right layers, with no chains and no reversed causes, is the standard. The standard is the next page.

### Conclusion

So Shoot is the pass that spends what Aim built. The questions get answered onto a map, logically and connected, in your order, with seven rules keeping the old habit out. It is meant to feel effortful and uncertain, and it goes before, during, and after each class. The bar is that Aim and Shoot run as one motion at an effort that has dropped. Holding the map to a standard is the next page.

The Aim bar has to be in place. A concept list turns into high-quality questions that find chunks, sub-chunks, and the relationships between them, every time. Focus sets up easily in any environment. Spaced interleaved retrieval is near unconscious competence, with several interleaving methods and consistent spacing. Non-linear notes are getting comfortable. Those are what Shoot stacks on. It is one new technique that folds several earlier ones into one pass.

Two things get replaced and three keep running. The green light of the traffic light drill is replaced, so the drill is now gone entirely and the two passes run alone. The old notes, collecting on one side of the page and processing on the other, are replaced by the map. Aim continues as it was. Maintaining focus and BEDS-M start turning into habits. Scheduling basics and spaced interleaved retrieval should be second nature by now.

### The session

- Questions beside the source. Read, flip back, write a few lines, connect them, find the next question.
- Three questions in the head for each thing: what is it, why is it important, how does it relate.
- Add one note rule at a time until all seven hold. Redo the first messy map; redoing is part of the system.

Watched from across a desk, a good session looks like this. A stretch of reading, a stop, a flip back to something earlier, a few lines written, a pause to join them to what is already on the map, and the material re-entered for the question that just appeared. Back and forth, not front to back. It looks chaotic from outside. From inside it is the opposite. Following the order the material was written in is the real chaos. The brain has to work out what a passage means, where it belongs, and why it matters, all at once. That is where retention drops. The order you answer in will change as you go, almost guaranteed. The notes are made so that changing it costs nothing. Three questions run in the head for each thing the material offers. What is it, why is it important, how does it relate. The answer to the second is usually a link to something already on the map, and that link is what gets drawn. Not every question gets a clean answer. Some are too advanced for this pass. The learning is in the attempt, so an unanswered question is marked and left, not forced.

Seven rules hold the notes to the pass. They are added one at a time, because a rigid linear habit does not change in a day. Visual: fewer words, rarely a sentence, almost never a paragraph. Doodles, symbols, and shorthand do the carrying, because re-drawing a paragraph as a doodle is the processing. Processed: nothing goes on the page that did not pass through the three questions first. Copying is fast and produces no learning. Relational: relationships on the page, not items, since an isolated fact is the one the brain drops. Freehand: by hand on an infinite canvas with a stylus, because typing is single-minded and represents relationships badly. Reflective: in your order, the one Aim chose, and if the notes follow the textbook's order something went wrong. Efficient: after a block, ask whether you kept it and can use it. Forgetting more than 30 percent of what you wrote means one of the earlier rules failed. The map itself is a technical skill. Relationships are planned before content goes down, first maps are messy with crossing lines, and redoing them is part of the system. Emphasise the flow that matters so the eye knows where to go. Never paste the textbook's diagram, because pasting removes the processing.

Effort, confusion, and the struggle to work something out are the signs the brain is working. Difficulty tracks learning, and the effect holds across ages and subjects. Study that is easy enough to make you drowsy, when sleep is not the cause, is study where the processing has stopped. The pass also withholds certainty for a long time. The certainty that rereading and copying hand out early is an illusion. It is where inconsistent results, frustration, and exam anxiety come from.

A learner who does not find the pass difficult is the one to worry about. It usually means the technique is not being applied to the standard. Time spent building a backbone, working out concepts, arranging them in space, and expressing them without words is all learning. Time spent reading, writing, and memorising with no relationship in view is the time wasted. Once the pass is a habit it runs on its own and is faster than the old method was. The hardest part is believing that and committing to it.

Shoot happens before, during, and after the main learning event. If there is time after the Aim, shoot the most basic concepts and relationships before the class. Continue the aim-shoot cycle in the class and finish the shoot there. In a live class the pass runs compressed: short lines, the map growing as the session runs, detail hung on chunks that already exist, question marks on what is unresolved, and no transcript. A few minutes afterwards tidies the rough map.

If there was no time before, shoot after. The class becomes one more resource. In the standard week, Shoot runs around each class from Monday to Friday, and Skin takes the map at the end of the week.

### The bar

- Aim and Shoot connect without a seam, high chunking, accurate relationships, moderately low effort.
- Focus, scheduling, BEDS-M, revision as habits. Retrieval never dropped. Kolb's run well.
- About 80 percent competence to move on. The map's chunks are graded on the next page.

The bar for Shoot is one sentence. You connect the aim and shoot steps accurately and seamlessly, with high chunking and accurate relationships between chunks, and the effort this takes has become moderately low. Focus, scheduling, BEDS-M, and revision have become habits by this point, at unconscious competence. About 80 percent competence with everything taught so far is the level to move on.

The checkpoint looks at three things, and people slack on the first two. Kolb's cycle is run consistently and at a high level. From here on, diagnosing why results are off becomes fine-grained. A reflection that is brief, or a rule that is theoretical rather than observed, stalls progress. The fundamentals, spaced interleaved retrieval above all, are still running and were not dropped while the encoding was being learned. And the aim-and-shoot maps themselves. What the checkpoint reads in those maps, whether the chunks are importance-based, whether there are single-node chains, chunks appearing from nowhere, reverse causality, keyword lists indented the way the textbook was, is the standard, and it is on Shoot - Training II. Work on the fence can move on, slowly, while the chunking is refined to the edges.
