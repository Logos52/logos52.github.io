# Skin

Skin is the cleanup pass on a study map: the learner stops taking in new material and reorganises what is already on the page until the structure is simple and can be rebuilt from memory. It is the third step of the Bear Hunter System, after Aim writes the questions and Shoot answers them on a map. The regrouping and ranking in this pass turns a map that covers a topic into one that is remembered and can be used on unfamiliar questions.

## Core takeaways

- No new material comes in during the pass.
- Group items into chunks, and chunks into larger chunks.
- Keep each point to two to four branches.
- Rank the links, and make the main path stand out.
- Leftover facts that link to nothing go to flashcards.
- The map is done when it can be rebuilt without the source.
- Tidying how a map looks, without regrouping it, gains little.

## How it works

The map that comes out of Shoot is rough, with items in the order they were found, arrows crossing and some points hanging on their own. Every decision in Skin, whether to group two items, split a group or move a link, makes the learner judge which ideas matter most and how they relate. That judgement is the highest level of processing a study method asks for, and it is where most of the retention gain comes from. Organised material is also easier to retrieve than the same material left loose.

```
after Shoot            after Skin
  a  b  c  d  e          [ group 1 ] --> [ group 2 ]
  |\/|\/ |  |              a   b          c   d
  f  g  h  i  j            f   g          h   e
  (loose, crossing)      (few groups, ranked links)
```

- A good pass leaves only a few details to memorise.
- The first map is never clean, so cycle Aim and Shoot first.
- Recalling a chunk's name brings back the items inside it.

## How to do it

The pass follows a short checklist, and each item on it fixes a different way a map stays hard to remember. The limit of two to four branches is a working rule. Attention holds about four items at once, which is part of why a point with more branches is hard to keep in mind.

- Group: put related items into chunks, then chunks into larger ones.
- Split: when a point has more than four branches, make sub-groups.
- Merge: fold a chain of single items into its parent.
- Label: name each group by what it does.
- Layer: show the main flow of ideas, then concepts, then details.
- Link: add side links between groups only where one really affects another.
- Direct: arrange the map so it reads in one direction.
- Emphasise: make the main path bigger or thicker than the rest.
- Cut: remove what turned out to be irrelevant.

## Checks

A map can look finished and still fail the test that matters, which is whether it can be rebuilt without the material. The checks below catch the common faults. Doing the whole pass again a week later, from memory, is also an effective form of revision.

- A few top groups sum up the topic and expand on demand.
- Each item hangs on what causes it, never on what it produces.
- Some links run between groups, as well as out from the centre.
- The map can be redrawn from memory.
- The main groups become test prompts for later review.

## Sources

- Tulving & Pearlstone (1966), and the category-clustering research that followed it: organised material is more retrievable than the same material left unorganised. That is the case for cleaning structure before testing it.
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006): practising retrieval beats further encoding. That is why the pass ends in prompts, and why a later pass from recall counts as revision.
- Cowan (2001): the focus of attention holds roughly four items. Carried as a reason a node with five or more branches is hard to hold, and not as a validation of the 2–4 rule.
