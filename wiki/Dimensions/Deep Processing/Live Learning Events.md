# Live Learning Events

A live session is used by arriving holding the topic's few largest ideas and the flow between them, then writing only the sentence that restates each finished point. The session reinforces understanding instead of demanding it cold.

## Before the session

The pass covers the topic that will run for the next few weeks, not the next class. A few largest ideas and the flow between them is the output. Each incoming point then has somewhere to attach, and part of the confusion is spent before the session rather than during it.

[[Prestudy]] is the setup pass itself — how the topic-level pass is actually run, which this page only applies in the room.

[[wiki/Self Management/Study Scheduling|Study Scheduling]] is where that block goes on the calendar, so the pass exists before the session rather than after it.

With no time at all, ten minutes on headings, diagrams, bold terms, and stated objectives still pays, provided it stays on the big picture. No details memorised yet is the check that the pass stayed a pass. Under time pressure this pass beats reviewing past material: if the bucket leaks, plug the holes rather than keep refilling. Done right, the main ideas are already in view and questions turn up during the talk — a self-report.

## In the room

**The work is the compression. Verbatim capture is what fails, not the keyboard.**

The lever is whatever forces a choice about what to keep. Listen first. A point that has not been finished cannot be paraphrased, so writing while the speaker talks can only be transcription. The paraphrase has a spec: the simplest, shortest form that can be managed.

```
The speaker finishes a point. The pen does not move. The point gets said back in one sentence. That sentence is what gets written.
```

Word count is a proxy. More words copied means fewer decisions about what mattered. Learning happens in that judgment, not on the page.

A relationship that costs a sentence in prose costs one arrow on a page, and where a thing sits becomes its own cue. Maps of relationships beat copying. Concept maps are the supported form, not every spatial doodle.

[[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Non-Linear Note-Making|Non-Linear Note-Making]] is the note format these guidelines assume — relationships first, not the speaker's sentence order.

The effort of getting a point into shorter words of one's own is the signal. Anxiety that produces more copying is not. Discomfort is not a quality meter. Struggling to process before writing points to the processing, not a trait, and is the thing the delay repairs. Delay the pen as long as the point can still be held, then extend the window. Relating each point to the big picture and grouping as it arrives is what makes the delay possible. If delaying the pen produces panic and more copying rather than a sentence of one's own, shorten the window instead of extending it.

## What you are training

These are cheap habits that raise a session's floor. They do not replace studying the material afterwards, and they are not the strongest note-making methods available. What is being trained is the processing, with the room as the place it gets tested under time pressure. The checks are self-reports.

Ausubel, D. P. (1960). The use of advance organizers in the learning and retention of meaningful verbal material. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 51(5), 267–272. Arriving with a more abstract frame makes later detail land. Mayer's pre-training principle is the later statement of the same move.

Mueller, P. A., & Oppenheimer, D. M. (2014). The pen is mightier than the keyboard. *Psychological Science*, 25(6), 1159–1168. Notes that drift toward verbatim retain less. The mechanism survives; the tool claim does not.

Morehead, K., Dunlosky, J., & Rawson, K. A. (2019). How much mightier is the pen than the keyboard for note-taking? *Educational Psychology Review*, 31, 753–780. The longhand-beats-laptop tool claim failed to reproduce.

Urry, H. L., et al. (2021). Don't ditch the laptop just yet. *Psychological Science*, 32(8), 1192–1206. Direct replication: no significant longhand advantage.

Nesbit, J. C., & Adesope, O. O. (2006). Learning with concept and knowledge maps. *Review of Educational Research*, 76(3), 413–448. Concept maps against conventional study, d ≈ 0.46.

Craik, F. I. M., & Lockhart, R. S. (1972). Levels of processing: A framework for memory research. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 11(6), 671–684. Encoding quality over repeated exposure.

Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 14(1), 4–58. Elaborative interrogation and self-explanation.

Miller, G. A. (1956). The magical number seven, plus or minus two. *Psychological Review*, 63(2), 81–97. Grouping as load reduction. Cowan (2001) is the later working-memory bound.
