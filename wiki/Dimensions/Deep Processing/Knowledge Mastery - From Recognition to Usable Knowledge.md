# Knowledge Mastery - From Recognition to Usable Knowledge

> Part of [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing|Deep Processing]]

Knowledge mastery is a diagnostic. It names the level a subject sits at, so the next study method can match that level instead of treating every gap the same way. Mastery here means four things: the learner can retrieve the knowledge, relate it to other knowledge, judge which relations matter, and use it. The four fail separately. A learner can hold isolated facts with no relations under them. A learner can hold the relations and still fail to use them under pressure.

The four capacities develop in layers. Use across contexts is the target, not a guarantee. The ladder that sorts the layers is a composite of two established accounts of learning outcomes. One account is built on cognitive process. The other is built on structural complexity. The ladder is this system's own teaching model, not a published scale. A level gets set during encoding, which is the work described in [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing|Deep Processing]]. The model the ladder sits inside is [[wiki/Dimensions/Dimensions of Learning|Dimensions of Learning]].

## Core takeaways

- Mastery has four parts: retrieve the knowledge, relate it, judge which relations matter, and use it. Each part can fail on its own.
- The ladder has five levels. Each level has a can-do half and a stopping half. The stopping half is the diagnostic.
- A method aimed one level below the target produces a session that looks finished and shows nothing about the higher level. More hours with the same method do not reach that level.
- The step from seeing relations at level 3 to ranking them at level 4 produces the deepest encoding. The material is the same at both levels. The judgment added to it is the difference.
- Higher-order methods produce lower-order outcomes as well, because comparing ideas requires understanding them first. Start with higher-order methods and use lower-order methods as a supplement.
- A level belongs to a subject and a topic. It does not belong to the learner. Re-run the ladder for each topic.

## The five levels

Each level has a can-do half and a stopping half. The stopping half is the diagnostic. Recognising the terminology and knowing what a term means both sit inside level 1. The span from recognition to usable knowledge is the span of the ladder itself.

| Level | What can be done | Where it stops | How it gets tested |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Recall isolated facts, details, and labels. Recognise the terminology and know what it means. | Learning at this level uses repetition and memorisation only. No other way of holding the material is available. | Fact-recall questions. |
| 2 | Explain an individual concept or process, discuss it, solve a simple problem with it. | The learner cannot handle the same concept inside an extended idea or an atypical application. | Questions that ask for an explanation or a description of a process. |
| 3 | See how concepts relate. Analyse and compare them against each other. | The relations sit between individual concepts and are not connected to the topic's big picture. An in-depth discussion is possible. An expert-level discussion is not. The learner does know what to ask an expert. | Compare-and-contrast questions, and problems that draw on several concepts at once. |
| 4 | A heavily connected appreciation of the relations. Individual facts have a place in the big picture. A change to one concept can be followed out to the other concepts and to the whole topic. The understanding feels simpler and more intuitive, and very little is held by repetition alone. | This is the target level. | Nuanced discussion of how concepts influence each other. Complex problems spanning several concepts. Contextually abstract problems that apply the knowledge across multi-factor scenarios. |
| 5 | Create new information from the knowledge. Form hypotheses and conjectures. | Formal education typically sets no assessment at this level before doctoral study. That describes what schools test, not what a learner can reach. Undergraduate design projects, original lab work, and a large share of professional problem-solving already operate at this level, whether or not anything grades them there. | High-level narrative, commentary, or original research. |

Higher levels correlate with solving more complex problems and with retaining more. The order of the levels reflects that.

## Methods and the level they reach

A method aimed at a level below the one the subject needs stops at the lower level. More time with that method does not reach the higher level. The cost of the mismatch is not slowness. A method one level below its target produces a session that looks finished and gives no signal about the level above.

| Level | Methods that produce it |
|---|---|
| 1 | Flashcards on facts. Cover-copy-check. Isolated-detail questions. |
| 2 | Flashcards on explaining. Teaching a single concept. Process maps and flow diagrams. |
| 3 | Teaching how two or more concepts relate. Compare-and-contrast. Questions on relations. Relational mindmaps. |
| 4 | Chunkmaps that group and then rank which relations matter. Evaluative questions. [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval/WPW|WPW]] reteaching. Discussion of how context changes importance. |
| 5 | Novel research and thesis exploration. Hypothesising structures from prior knowledge. Treating new information as an experiment that confirms or rejects the hypothesis. |

A linear brain dump, typing out everything known, tests low-to-mid mastery. It cannot represent relations, so it cannot report a level-4 failure. It finds gaps only when the writer happens to run into them. A mapped brain dump of the same session gives the relations somewhere to sit. It reaches mid-to-higher levels and finds large gaps fast. The hour spent is the same. The limit on the level is gone.

The limit also affects notes made earlier. Notes first encoded at a low level are hard to chunkmap later. The material was never processed above that level, so there are no judgments in the notes to recover. That is why [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Importance-Based Chunking|Importance-Based Chunking]], grouping by why things belong together and then ranking the groups, is mainly a level-4 activity. It is also why the chunking cannot be added to a level-1 encoding afterwards.

Difficulty does not put a question at level 4. An evaluative question requires a value judgment about the relative importance of concepts. That requirement puts it at level 4. A harder question about relations, with no judgment required, stays at level 3.

[[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval/WPW|WPW]] tests every level at once and finds gaps fast. What it returns depends on how well the encoding underneath it already works. Before the encoding methods are solid, reteaching whole-part-whole is close to unusable.

Flashcards are the right tool when the gap is genuinely at level 1 or at a narrow level 2. They are a legitimate tool class for those gaps. The test should match the encoding. A retrieval form aimed at a different level from the one just built cannot show whether the build worked.

## What the levels measure

The ladder measures degree of integration. The step that produces the largest gain is the step from seeing relations at level 3 to ranking them at level 4.

Level 3 identifies relations, similarities, and differences. That is moderately helpful. It starts building connections and networks. Level 4 makes value judgments about the overall importance and relevance of those same relations. That is extremely helpful. It forces substantially deeper encoding. Both levels work on the same raw material. The difference is the judgment applied to it. The labels "moderately helpful" and "extremely helpful" are this model's own comparative labels, not measured effect sizes.

Ranking the same relations moves the knowledge from level 3 to level 4.

The cost of staying below that step grows over time. Higher-order methods make new content easier to learn as expertise accumulates, because each new piece has somewhere to attach. Lower-order methods leave new information isolated over the same stretch, and the load grows. Matching the method to the level is required. [[wiki/Concepts/Learning Efficiency|Learning Efficiency]] is retention at the level the subject requires. Hours logged do not measure it.

## Why connected knowledge holds better

Higher-order knowledge is integrated. Lower-order knowledge is isolated. Once the network is strong, lower-order details become easier to hold, because they have places to attach. [[wiki/Concepts/Memory Handling|Memory Handling]] describes the working-memory window that makes isolated detail expensive to keep.

The retention chain has three steps. Better big-picture understanding makes the information more meaningful. More meaningful information is retained and encoded better. Better retention reduces the need for frequent repetition. Strong, simple, highly refined organisational structures let the mind move through a topic quickly. A set of unconnected facts does not allow that.

This does not remove the need for lower-order retrieval. It changes the order of operations. Build meaning first, then use targeted repetition for the details that still need it. The brain still forgets. Retrieval practice is still required at every level that has to be performed. [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval|Retrieval]] is the dimension where a level, once built, gets tested.

Lower-order methods produce only lower-order outcomes. Higher-order methods produce both. Ideas cannot be compared without first being understood, so the comparison work also produces the understanding. Start with higher-order methods. Use lower-order methods as a supplement, or nearer to an assessment.

The strongest case against the ladder comes from the two accounts it is built on. Treating memory and understanding as the bottom levels of a hierarchy has been criticised. The more accurate account is that higher-order thinking produces memory and understanding. The ladder ranks outcomes. The processes that produce its top levels are the same processes that produce its bottom levels. That is why working at the level the subject needs gives more than climbing the levels in order.

## After a failed session

When a session produces no gain, the question is which level failed. The answer decides the repair.

1. Could the pieces not be recalled? Use targeted retrieval on those specific gaps.
2. Could one of the pieces not be explained? Use encoding or retrieval aimed at a single concept: flashcards on explaining, teaching that one piece, or a process map.
3. Could the pieces not be connected? Do relating work during encoding: compare-and-contrast, relational maps, or teaching how two or more concepts sit together.
4. Could you not say which connections mattered? Do ranking work: chunkmaps, evaluative questions, or a discussion of how context changes importance.
5. Could nothing new be done with the knowledge? Build something: a hypothesis, a novel case, or an experiment against the current structure.

[[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Bear Hunter System|Bear Hunter System]] is the four-pass encoding routine aimed at levels 3–4. [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval/Spaced Interleaved Retrieval|Spaced Interleaved Retrieval]] tests all levels over time. Those retrieval sessions are themselves aimed at a level. Aiming them below the level needed produces the same limit in the retrieval dimension. [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation|Self-Regulation]] decides whether a failed level is repaired by encoding, retrieval, practice, or self-management. The five questions themselves belong to [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation/Metacognition - The Control Layer|Metacognition: The Control Layer]].

At level 4 the topic starts to feel simpler, and less of it is held by repetition. If the material still reads as a list after a level-4 method, the method landed at level 3. If naming the level does not change what the next session does, the diagnosis is not what is holding progress. Study, and return to the ladder at the next plateau that cannot be named.

A level describes a subject and a topic. It does not describe the learner. The same person sits at level 4 in one topic and at level 1 in another. The ladder is therefore re-run for each topic. It is not climbed once.

## How to practice this

1. Pick one subject and one topic. Read the "Where it stops" column and find the row that describes your current work. Notice that the level belongs to that topic only, so repeat this for each topic.
2. Find your current study method in the table of methods by level. Notice whether it sits below the level the topic needs. If it does, the sessions will look finished and show nothing about the higher level.
3. Replace a linear brain dump with a mapped one for the same session. Notice whether the relations have somewhere to sit. Notice whether large gaps show up faster.
4. After a session with no gain, ask the five questions in order: recall, explain, connect, rank, create. Notice the first question you answer no to. That is the level to repair.
5. Repair that level with a method from its own row in the methods table, not from a lower row. Notice whether the next session works at the repaired level.
6. After a level-4 method, read the material back. If it still reads as a list, the method landed at level 3. If naming the level changes nothing about the next session, study and return to the ladder at the next plateau you cannot name.

- Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1): practice testing supports isolated recall (level 1).
- Craik & Lockhart (1972); Craik & Tulving (1975): elaborative, meaningful encoding is more durable.
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006): retrieval practice is still required after better encoding. Meaning-first does not replace testing.
- Morris, Bransford & Franks (1977): transfer-appropriate processing. The test should match the encoding.
- Barnett & Ceci (2002): far transfer is mixed. "Across contexts" is the target, not the guarantee.
