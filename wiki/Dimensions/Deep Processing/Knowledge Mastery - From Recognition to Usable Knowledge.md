# Knowledge Mastery - From Recognition to Usable Knowledge

How well someone is learning is hard to see directly. The levels below are a working scale for setting goals and watching progress. They are inspired by two older maps of learning outcomes, and they are used here as one combined scale. Higher levels mean deeper learning, which goes with harder problems and with holding more of what was learned.

At the lowest level, you can recall facts and isolated details. Learning leans on repetition and memorizing. You can recognize a term and know what it means. Common practice includes cards on facts and definitions, covering and checking, and questions on isolated details. Tests at this level ask for fact recall.

At the next level, you can explain a single concept, talk about it, and solve simple problems with it. Extended ideas or unusual uses of the concept are still hard. Practice includes cards and questions that ask you to explain how something works, teaching one concept, and process maps. Tests ask you to describe a process or a concept.

At the third level, you can see how concepts relate, and you can compare them. Those links are still between individual ideas, not yet tightly tied to the big picture of the whole topic. You can have a thorough conversation about the topic. Expert-level talk may still be out of reach. You may still know what questions to ask an expert. Practice includes teaching how two or more concepts relate, compare-and-contrast work, questions that test a relationship, and maps that show how concepts connect. Tests ask for comparison, or for problems that draw on more than one concept.

The fourth level is where the benefit jumps. Relationships among many concepts are strongly connected, and individual facts fit into the big picture. The understanding often feels simpler and more intuitive, with little held only by repetition. You should be able to talk with an expert in a way that respects context, move between concepts, and see how a change in one idea affects others and the topic as a whole. The step up from the third mastery level is not only more links. Level 3 identifies relationships, similarities, and differences, which helps moderately by starting to build connections. Level 4 asks for a judgment about how important those relationships are, which forces much deeper encoding, meaning a deeper first pass at making the knowledge usable. Practice includes maps that group concepts by similarity and mark which relationships matter more. It also includes questions that ask you to justify relative importance, a reteaching method named whole-part-whole, and talk about how context changes what matters. Tests include nuanced discussion, complex problems that draw on several concepts at once, and problems set in unfamiliar, many-factor situations.

At the fifth level you are creating new information. That includes hypotheses and conjectures, extensions of what is already known, and experiments that try to confirm or reject those ideas. Formal tests at this level are uncommon before doctoral work.

A shorter way to hold the scale is that higher-order learning, meaning work that is integrated rather than isolated, is a clearer sense of how information fits the big picture. Lower-order learning is the isolated kind. That kind of picture needs simple, well-worked organizational schemas, meaning arrangements that let you move through a topic quickly, the way a set of highways would. A better big picture makes information more meaningful, which helps it stick and reduces how often it must be repeated.

Both kinds of method belong in a full system. Lower-order methods produce lower-order results, while higher-order methods produce both. It is better to start with higher-order work and treat lower-order methods as extra support, or nearer to a test. Higher-order methods also make later material easier as expertise grows. Lower-order methods make new information more overwhelming, because it stays isolated rather than joining a network.

## Open questions

- How does judging which relationship matters more change what gets remembered later?
- How does starting with isolated facts, rather than with the big picture, change how new material arrives?
