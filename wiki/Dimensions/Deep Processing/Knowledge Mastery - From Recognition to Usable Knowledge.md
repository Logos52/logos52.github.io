# Knowledge Mastery - From Recognition to Usable Knowledge

Knowledge mastery is how well a person knows a subject, on a scale that runs from recognising its terms to making new ideas with it. The level a person reaches decides which questions they can answer and how much repetition they need to keep what they learned. A learner who knows their level also knows which study method to use next.

- There are five levels, from recalling facts to creating new knowledge.
- Higher levels link more of the facts to each other.
- Level 4 adds a judgement of which links matter most.
- The step from level 3 to level 4 gives the largest gain.
- Study methods aimed at a low level give only low-level results.
- Methods aimed at a high level give both, so start with them.
- Connected knowledge needs less repetition to keep.

## The five levels

The levels combine two published ways of grading understanding, Bloom's revised taxonomy and the SOLO taxonomy. Bloom's sorts thinking from remembering at the bottom to creating at the top. SOLO sorts answers by how many ideas they connect. Each level has study methods that build it and kinds of question that test it, so a learner can use the levels as a ladder, picking one as the goal for a topic and checking where they stand.

```
5  create     new hypotheses from what is known
4  evaluate   which links matter most, and why
3  relate     compare and connect concepts
2  explain    one concept at a time
1  recall     isolated facts and terms
```

- Level 1: recall facts and recognise terms, mostly by repetition.
  - Built with fact flashcards and cover-copy-check.
- Level 2: explain one concept and solve simple problems with it.
  - Built by teaching one concept or drawing a process diagram.
- Level 3: see how concepts relate and compare them.
  - Built with mindmaps and compare-and-contrast questions.
- Level 4: judge how much each link matters to the whole topic.
  - Built with grouped, ranked maps.
  - Also built by reteaching the whole, then the parts, then the whole.
- Level 5: form new hypotheses and test them.
  - Rarely examined in formal education before a doctorate.

## Why level 4 matters

At level 3 a learner can say that two concepts are linked and how they differ. At level 4 the learner also decides which links carry the topic and which are minor, and that decision forces a much deeper processing of the material. The topic often feels simpler at this level, because the details hang on a few main relationships instead of being held one by one. A person at level 4 can hold a detailed conversation with an expert and can say how a change in one concept would affect the rest of the topic.

- Level 3 finds relationships, similarities and differences.
- Level 4 ranks those relationships by their importance to the topic.
- Little is memorised by plain repetition at level 4.
- Complex problems that draw on several concepts test level 4.
- So do unfamiliar situations with many factors at play.

## How to use it

A study method aimed at a low level gives only low-level results, and a method aimed at a high level gives both. The practical order is to start a topic by relating and ranking its ideas, and to add flashcards and other repetition as a supplement or close to an exam. New material gets easier to learn over time when each fact joins a network of known ones, and harder when each fact stays on its own. Testing is still needed after good encoding, and the test should match the level the subject requires.

- Start a topic by relating its ideas, then add flashcards.
- Use repetition for the facts that do not connect to anything.
- Practice testing mainly trains level 1.
- Choose the test to match the level being aimed at.
- A stuck level can be an encoding, retrieval, practice or scheduling problem.

## Evidence

The ladder is a working guide built from two taxonomies. It has not been measured as a scale. Research on experts supports its main idea, that knowledge organised around principles is what separates the higher levels from the lower ones. The main support comes from studies of how experts and beginners sort problems, and the main limit comes from studies of how far knowledge carries to new settings.

- Experts group problems by principle, beginners by surface features.
- Meaningful, elaborated encoding lasts longer than shallow encoding.
- Transfer to very different settings is uneven across studies.
