# Thinking on Paper - Mindmaps and Other Techniques

When a problem or a new topic has too many moving parts to hold in your head, it helps to work it out on paper. Once the pieces and the connections between them are written down, you no longer have to keep them in mind, which leaves you free to think about what comes next.

Not all thinking on paper is equal, however. Some note-taking strategies, linear note-taking for example, lead to lower retention. Our top choice for most situations is the mind map, which uses nodes and connections to show how ideas relate. That supports working memory by giving information anchor points to land on, which eventually helps it settle into long-term memory.

<div class="callout">

<b>At a glance</b>

- The mind map is the top choice for most situations, because it uses nodes and connections to show how ideas relate.
- A new piece of information becomes part of your schema when it finds a connection, and fades and is forgotten when it finds none.
- Mapping feels slow and confusing because it takes away the feelings of fluency. That is how effective learning feels.
- Start from about 15 keywords, map connections quickly and roughly, then simplify. In an hour of study, about 45 minutes go to simplifying and 15 to getting connections down.
- A map should look messy early on. A confusing map shows confused knowledge, now on paper where you can fix it.

</div>

## Schemas and anchor points

The schema is a network of everything you already know, but new information doesn't arrive as part of it. It comes in as isolated dots, and your brain's job is to work out how each one connects to the network. A piece that finds a connection becomes part of your schema and an anchor point for later information, while a piece that finds none fades and is forgotten. That is why learning gets easier the more you know, since every connection you add gives the next piece somewhere to attach.

## Why linear notes fall short

Writing something down doesn't mean your brain has learned it, which is why linear note-taking is extremely limited. The better approach is non-linear notes built from mind maps, arrows, symbols and page layout. A map in particular makes you decide the order of the concepts, how they relate and where each one sits, which takes much more effort than a linear summary.

| | Linear summary | Mind map |
|---|---|---|
| What you decide | How to shorten the ideas, since their order stays the same | The order of the concepts, how they relate, where each one sits and how to keep it concise |
| Effort | Less | Much more |
| Retention | Lower | Higher |
| The confusion in your head | Still there, and the first time you see it might be in an exam | On the page, where you can see it and fix it |

## Why mind mapping feels slow

Making all the choices a map asks for is hard work, and it takes away the feelings that normally tell you a technique is working. Your brain looks for signs like understanding, progress, familiarity and speed, and those signs are what we call fluency. Linear notes produce them, but a map often leaves you more confused and slower, so mapping can seem like wasted time.

That feeling is mostly wrong, which is known as the illusion of fluency. Forming a schema naturally feels slow, effortful and confusing, because you're seeking out the parts that confuse you so that you can sort them out. That is how effective learning feels. If you stop at the confusion, push through, and if mapping feels no harder than your usual notes, you have drifted back toward fluency, so ask how else you could arrange the ideas.

| What you notice | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| You stop at the confusion | You are feeling what forming a schema is like | Push through |
| Mapping feels no harder than your usual notes, often because you settled on the first arrangement | You have drifted back toward fluency | Ask how else you could arrange the ideas |

## How to map

One way to begin, called blind mapping, starts from a list of about 15 keywords on a topic you're learning or a problem you're working on. Draw the keywords with lines and arrows between them, with no sentences and no bullet points. When the map gets messy and overwhelming, redraw it, group the keywords that belong together and give each arrow a direction, then keep adding connections and simplifying again. Speed matters more than accuracy, because a wrong schema is easier to build on than no schema at all, and the simplifying is where the learning happens. Do the grouping yourself rather than handing it to a chatbot, because it is your own effort that builds the knowledge.

1. Start from a list of about 15 keywords on a topic or problem.
2. Pick a keyword that seems important. Find the keywords that give it that importance, since a thing is only important in relation to other things, and add them if they are missing.
3. Draw the keywords with lines and arrows, with no sentences and no bullet points.
4. Bring in two or three more keywords that connect to what you have.
5. When the map is messy and the lines cross, redraw it on a fresh sheet so the lines cross less and show a flow.
6. Group keywords that belong together, so that one item with six arrows becomes one item with two groups. Give each arrow a direction, with thicker lines for the strongest relationships.

<details>
<summary>Structure first, details second</summary>

Prioritise the relationships you draw so the page doesn't fill up with arrows, and form the overall structure before the details. Once your anchor points are built, whatever still connects to nothing is a discrete detail that can go into flashcards.

</details>

| Dependency map | A hard decision or problem | Write down every factor that makes it difficult, connect them, and look for the biggest constraints. |

## When the map looks messy

A map should look messy early on, because that is when schema building really starts. If it looks confusing, your knowledge is confused, and the map has only put that disorganisation on paper where you can spot it and fix it, which is why it can be called a mind mirror.

<details>
<summary>Is the map working as it grows?</summary>

A map should grow denser while learning feels easier. If you feel more overwhelmed, you are taking in too much compared with how much you are digesting.

</details>
