# Thinking on Paper - Mindmaps and Other Techniques

Thinking on paper means working through a hard problem by writing and drawing it out, instead of trying to hold every part of it in the head. The mind can only handle a few things at once, so a problem with many parts overloads it and feels confusing. Putting the parts on a page leaves the mind free to work on one connection at a time.

- Working memory holds only a few items at once.
- Feeling stuck usually means it is full.
- Thinking harder while stuck makes the overload worse.
- Put the parts on paper and work link by link.
- The first page will be messy, and that is expected.
- Draw several possible arrangements and compare them.
- The paper is a thinking tool, so it can stay rough.

## How it works

Working memory is the part of the mind where active thinking happens, and it holds about four items at a time. When a decision or topic has more parts than that, the mind spends its effort keeping them in view and has little left for working out how they connect. Paper holds the parts, so working memory only needs to hold the one relationship being worked on. Each settled link goes back onto the page, and the mind takes the next pair.

```
in the head:   A B C D E F  -> all held at once -> stuck

on paper:      page holds A..F
               mind holds A -> B only
               settle it, write it, take the next pair
```

- Overload feels like confusion or being stuck.
- Going over the problem again in the head adds tiredness.
- Paper acts as an outside memory for the parts.
- The paper shows links that were not visible in the head.

## How to do it

The habit is to reach for paper as soon as a problem feels tricky, before the overload builds. The first attempt is a rough dump of every part, followed by trying out how they connect. Many people stop at the list, because there are too many ways to link the items and they cannot choose one. Drawing a few arrangements and looking at them side by side is faster than deciding in the head.

- Write down every factor, idea or concern involved.
- Pick two and work out how they relate.
- Draw that link, then bring in the next item.
- Try grouping the items, and redraw when the grouping changes.
- Clean up the page once the links are clear.
- Give hard problems a block of time with pen and paper.

## Other techniques

Mindmaps are the most common form of thinking on paper for study, and the same idea works for decisions and planning. Two related methods, separation of concerns and the dependency map, break a large tangle into parts that can be handled one at a time. Both depend on having the parts written down where they can be seen.

- Separation of concerns: split the tangle into parts and settle each alone.
- Dependency map: list the factors and draw which cause or block others.
  - Work first on the factor that blocks the most.
- Mindmap: arrange ideas in space and link them by how they relate.
