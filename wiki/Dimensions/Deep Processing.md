# Deep Processing

> Meaningful learning comes from the thinking information triggers. Deep processing analyses, links, and critiques so memory, understanding, and flexible use improve. Shallow work—rote repetition and context-free memorisation—feels productive but leaves brittle knowledge. Genetics and childhood experience set a baseline, yet habits can be retrained in phases; strong processors may “tank” with weak methods until difficulty rises, and retrieval quality always feeds back on encoding.

## Shallow and deep

The brain processes constantly. Depth is a matter of habit, not intent alone. Shallow processing stores fragments: you may recognise terms without a usable structure. Deep processing builds networks: similarities, differences, importance, and implications are weighed on purpose. Higher-order questions ask how ideas connect, what function they serve, and how they compare to what you already know. Lower-order habits ask only what a line means and whether to repeat it.

**Deep processing tanking** is when exceptional processing hides weak technique—you perform well until goals outgrow the shortcut. Copying a tanker’s surface habits without their processing depth produces frustration, not parity.

## Four phases of skill

Think of skill building like tennis. Replaying full matches before footwork and grip are stable wastes time. You isolate components, drill until automatic, then integrate under pressure. Cognition works the same way: you cannot consciously steer every process while learning; you automate essentials.

| Phase | Focus |
|---|---|
| Understanding and awareness | Notice shallow vs deep habits; see how schemas form |
| Aligning technique | Apply methods that force depth—grouping, questioning, layer order |
| Refining and deepening | Drop into deeper processing at will; judge connection strength |
| Integration and mastery | Run questioning, analysing, and grouping in one fluid sequence |

A training ladder also moves from memorise → understand → judge keyword importance → group by likeness → rate groups → show how groups act on one another. Calibrate at one level before adding the next.

## Best-attempt encoding

The first pass should be the **best interconnected structure your current skill can produce in realistic time**, not a fantasy “perfect map.” Groups must be intuitive enough that remembering one part cues the rest, and specific enough that this topic is not confused with another. Too-generic templates (interior/exterior splits reused everywhere) or too-specific labels that do not cue each other both fail.

Early on, **retrieval may carry more load than encoding**—for example explaining to a beginner forces synthesis you cannot yet generate spontaneously. As encoding improves, needed retrieval falls.

High-volume study changes the economics: forgetting half of a huge corpus is fatal when hold times run to months or years. Progress is measured by coverage, gaps found, and depth—not hours spent. Encode details with a width choice: one-to-one fact vs slightly wider cluster (guarded by conceptual relevance and whether tests keep calling on the item). Test early with high-volume, higher-order methods in the first week; diagnose whether gaps are declarative, procedural, or higher-order; **rearrange maps** when structure was wrong, because the map is an attempt, not truth.

## Schemas, layers, and maps

Knowledge lives in **schemas**—frameworks of linked ideas. Conversion fails when new pieces have nowhere to attach; more volume does not fix a missing gatekeeper. Construction is an active first draft, then connection, reorganisation, and review that is not the same as passive rereading.

**Layers** run logic → concepts → important details → arbitrary specifics. Higher layers carry mark weight; the finest layer is vast and low hit-rate—confidence should be layered, not uniform. **Mindmaps** are the primary nonlinear form: early mess is expected; the goal is relationships, not transcription. **Order control** means choosing the sequence you meet ideas; linear source order is often wrong for your map.

Inquiry before consumption—creating questions, then answering—replaces passive intake. **Prestudy** is timing (before a lecture or block); the method may still be question-first encoding. **Problem-first** learning sets a challenge before theory so transfer improves. **Importance-based chunking** groups by why something matters, not by headings the author supplied.

## Primary practice system

The [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Bear Hunter System|Bear Hunter System]] consolidates question-first intake (Aim), map building while answering (Shoot), and evaluative refinement (Skin / GRINDE checks). It supersedes the older traffic-light inquiry pattern once Aim and Shoot are stable. **ReCOVer** and other checklists name quality bars for processed notes. **Interleaving for complex problems** applies when familiar drills collapse in novel contexts—the bottleneck is usually declarative strategy, not more of the same execution.

## Research, clinical, and specialist tracks

Research reading treats each paper as a puzzle piece for a living map: overwhelm, then organise; cost per paper falls as schemas thicken. Writing runs the process in reverse. **Theme-first text analysis** trains literature essays by theme before device hunting. **Clinical learning** maps a case, learns one branch, then seeks the next patient that touches an adjacent branch. **Rapid skill acquisition** cycles minimal theory → attempt → diagnose → retry without flooding working memory. Use AI for chores, not for outsourcing the network-building work.

## Challenge and regulation links

The deep-processing month in [[wiki/Dimensions/30-Day Challenges|30-day challenges]] trains the shift from isolated remembering to comparing and judging: week one builds radar for shallow habits, week two stresses networking ideas, week three adds pressure, week four chases fluid comparison under load. Weak processing caps retrieval; hard self-tests can force depth. Pair encoding work with [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval|retrieval]] and [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation|self-regulation]] so difficulty and accuracy stay calibrated.

When maps fail, the fault is usually habit—not capacity. Waterfall layouts spray connections downward without lateral ties; spiderwebs bolt arrows onto immature groups. Both feel busy and memorable in the moment yet collapse under WPW-style teaching passes. Fixing structure beats adding color. If every gap diagnoses as higher-order, suspect the first encoding pass, not “bad writing,” and return to Aim before bolting on more flashcards.
