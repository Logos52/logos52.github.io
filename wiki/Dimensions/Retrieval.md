# Retrieval

Retrieval is the process of using and recalling knowledge from your memory. It happens whenever you bring back a fact, explain something, work through a problem, or talk through a concept. Memory is never perfect, and knowledge fades at some rate all the time, so retrieval practice is a key part of a learning system if you want to slow that decay. Retrieval practice makes knowledge more accurate, cuts errors, and raises your ability to perform at a high level of challenge in varied settings and under pressure.

A comparison used here is driving a car. If learning is like driving, retrieval practice is what lets the driver handle different ground, winding roads, and poor conditions.

Retrieval practice can feel like a poor fit when you are not studying for an exam. In many professional settings, information does not have to be memorised or brought back in the same way as it does for academic assessments. Retrieval is still a necessary component of learning in any situation. In workplace learning, retrieval can be opportunistic, meaning you might naturally need to pull the information back in varied ways during ordinary work. An effective learner can use planned retrieval practice to fill the gaps that this natural work does not cover.

Training covers three habits. First, match the retrieval method with the type of knowledge. Declarative knowledge means facts. Procedural knowledge means skills. Conditional knowledge means when and why to apply what you know. That alignment is what ensures you grasp the material and can use it fluently in the right ways, and without error.

Second, space and interleave the practice. Interleaving means coming at the information from different angles and different levels of difficulty. Spacing means giving long-term memory the time it needs to integrate what you have learned. Careful spacing and interleaving help you apply knowledge in more complex ways while making retrieval sessions more time-efficient, and this keeps high learning volumes from overwhelming you.

Third, target your gaps. Most learners subconsciously avoid their weak points because that path feels simpler and less uncomfortable. Retrieval can be used as a focused tool to uncover gaps in memory and in knowledge mastery, meaning how usable the knowledge is. Later techniques help you notice finer gaps and handle more nuanced, specific learning needs. By focusing on gaps and keeping methods aligned to real-world requirements, you save time by cutting needless re-learning and raise accuracy by staying tied to your learning goals.

Retrieval practice is bound to deep processing in two directions. Weak deep processing will also cap how good retrieval practice can be. Retrieval can also help train deep processing in the first place. Learners who have trouble with deep processing may find it hard to take on a tougher form of retrieval, such as demanding self-testing. By attempting that higher difficulty of self-testing, they force the brain to engage in deeper processing than it habitually might, which helps the brain get used to that deeper processing in the future.

There is no retrieval challenge attached to this overview. High-quality retrieval is described as one of the first major lessons in the wider training. The right retrieval strategy depends on your aims and your timetable, so a general challenge would not do it justice.

## Breaching Questions

Breaching questions is a late-stage method for finding the gaps that remain in what you know, including gaps you have no way of feeling. You write the hardest question you can think of on each weak spot, one you cannot answer yet, and the effort of trying to answer it closes the gap. The name comes from the way each question is built to breach a plateau in your learning.

<div class="callout">

<b>At a glance</b>

- The method belongs to the later stages of learning, when few gaps are left and the aim is the extra few marks on much less predictable questions.
- A known gap shows up as trouble recalling or applying something. An unknown gap is something you never knew, so you can feel confident while missing a whole perspective.
- You find weak spots by listing keywords under each learning objective, mapping how they relate, and asking whether a keyword from one objective applies to another.
- Each weak spot, or group of related ones, gets the hardest question you can write, one you have no answer for as you write it.
- Time scales with importance, from 15 to 20 minutes for a minor fact, and from half an hour up to two hours for a central concept.

</div>

## Why it works

Late in your studying there are few gaps left, and they come in two kinds that are found in different ways. A known gap shows up when you have trouble recalling or applying something. Because an unknown gap is something you never knew, you can answer confidently from what you do know while a whole perspective is missing, which makes late-stage gaps hard to find.

The keyword check in the method targets unknown gaps. Asking whether a keyword from one learning objective applies to another forces you to look for a perspective where it is relevant, and that search reveals what you had not considered. The breaching question targets a plateau, meaning a piece of knowledge that still holds you back after your earlier study methods. A question harder than anything the exam could ask gives you a strong focus. Trying to answer that question pulls you into deeper learning, usually beyond what the exam needs, which turns the weakness into a strength. Because such a question is so advanced, it also draws on several other concepts, so answering it covers connected topics as well.

## The method

You need your mind map or the subject's list of learning objectives, and time in the late stage of your studying. The first four steps find the weak spots, and the last three turn them into questions.

1. Look through your mind map for the concepts, facts or details you struggle to recall quickly and confidently. If you cannot tell, list the subject's learning objectives instead, because the assessment follows the learning outcomes.
2. Under each learning objective, list every keyword you can think of that relates to it, and map in your head how the keywords relate as you list them. You can also recall everything you know about the objective and compare it with your notes. Trouble mapping points to a known gap.
3. Once an objective is done, look for a keyword you know belongs to the topic but did not list or map. It may be a known gap or an unknown one.
4. Look for keywords that appear under one learning objective but not another, and ask whether each could apply to the other. Finding the perspective where it applies helps reveal unknown gaps.
5. Write down every weak spot you found, however small it is, and group the ones that relate to each other. A weak spot that does not group stays on its own and gets its own question.
6. For each group or single point, write the hardest question the exam could ask about it, harder than any real question would be. You must not know the answer at the time you write it.
7. Try to answer each question, because the focus it gives you is what pulls you into deeper learning, whether or not you reach a correct answer. Spend 30 minutes to two hours on a concept that is very likely to come up, or 15 to 20 minutes on a single fact you only want covered, and then you are done.

## Signals and failure points

| What you notice | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| You cannot tell where your weak spots are | Reflecting on your mind map has not turned them up | List the subject's learning objectives and recall everything for each one |
| The keywords under one objective will not map | A known gap | Add it to your list of weak spots |
| A keyword you know belongs is missing from your list | A known or an unknown gap | Add it to your list of weak spots |
| A keyword appears under one objective but not another | An unknown gap, if the keyword applies to the other objective | Ask how it could apply, and look for the perspective where it is relevant |
| Weak spots will not group | Nothing is wrong | Leave them as single points, each with its own question |
| You can answer your question as you write it | It does not breach your current understanding | Write a harder one |
| You cannot answer your question | Nothing is wrong, because a correct answer is not the point | Keep digging until the time limit |

## Cost and prerequisites

The cost is the time you spend answering each question, which depends on how important the concept is. The method is advanced because it needs you to handle heavy cognitive load, and to carry out a large amount of non-linear mapping in your head.

This method is one of two late-stage ways of adjusting how you apply what you know. The other is practice papers and practice tests, which come during the last two or three weeks after you have patched most weaknesses.

## How to practice this

1. Pick one subject and write out its learning objectives.
2. Under one objective, list every related keyword and map how they relate in your head, noting where the mapping stalls.
3. Take one keyword from that objective and ask where it would apply under a different objective.
4. Group the weak spots you noted, then write one question for each group or single point, harder than anything the exam could ask, and check that you cannot answer it as written.
5. Decide how important each concept is, then work on its question for 30 minutes to two hours or for 15 to 20 minutes, and stop when the time is up.

# Group Study

Group study can help people keep one another going, review material, and get ready for assessments. Without structure it can also make it easier to delay work and distract each other. The structure offered here is three major roles that you appoint in the group.

The leader steers how the discussion moves and presses for deeper exploration. Leaders steer and manage the group's attention. They keep the discussion aligned with the agenda and the learning objectives, encourage participation, and look for extra viewpoints and deeper discussion. They press people past the edge of what feels comfortable to know, and help the group meet cognitive difficulty, meaning work that feels hard because it stretches what people currently know. Together, the timekeeper and the fact-checker keep that role in balance. The timekeeper helps stop the discussion running too long, and the fact-checker helps keep its accuracy high.

The timekeeper keeps up the pace and pressure. They watch whether the group is running over time on a topic, which may leave too little time for other points of equal or greater importance. This role also keeps track of the main points and recaps the ground already covered when the group stalls and needs to gather its thoughts. They should pay attention to the group's energy and focus, and suggest timed breaks or step up the urgency during teaching or discussion so attention returns to a strong level.

The fact-checker brings challenge, inquiry, and accuracy. They pay careful attention to what the other members say and look for loopholes, inaccuracies, or probing questions that the group may not have thought of. They should ask questions such as why something is important, why it happens, or "So what?". They have to seek out viewpoints that were skipped and links that nobody has drawn yet. They ought to keep search tools, other resources, and an AI ready, so they can check whether statements hold up and whether a challenging question is worth asking. This role is one of the most difficult and also one of the most important. It usually goes to the person with the most expertise and the strongest higher-order thinking. In every situation, though, roles should be swapped so each person can fill each role.

# Multipass System

The multipass system uses a picture of stacked layers that each have holes. The different passes are like those layers, and they let you cover almost all of the material in just a few days. Each pass is one sweep through the material.

A related foundation is a system of core skills and steps for taking in any new material, and for thinking about and processing that information so knowledge is encoded efficiently, meaning stored in a usable form. Each learning session still uses those steps. Multipass is a strategy for deploying them in different ways. It adds advice on when to study and a broad steer for the order you meet material in. It tells you what each sitting will cover and how to break a topic into manageable blocks. That is especially useful for big, packed topics that will take more than one pass. Other ways of using the same core skills are mentioned and not described here.

There are two ways to perform the system.

Inquiry-led multipass lets relevance come from your own inquiry process. It feels natural and engaging. It is an extension of the usual core-skill sequence, split across several sittings, each one taking a changing number of learning layers. It can be less strategic when time pressure is high. It suits rapid learning of high volumes when time is more flexible, and material that needs to be fundamentally strong for later learning and application.

Objectives-led multipass lets learning objectives dictate the order. Learning stays directly relevant to most assessable material, and time spent is very likely to translate into performance results. It trades some of the quality of encoding, meaning how well the knowledge is stored, for performance strategy. That can reduce retention and the ability to handle the most higher-order or unanticipated questions. It suits imminent time pressure that demands high volumes of learning with little study time, and topics with limited long-term relevance.

Both approaches start with the logic layer and the concepts layer. Ordinary cramming starts with detail layers whose relevance goes up and down.

Whichever approach you use, emphasising those first layers helps prepare you for assessments. Most questions come out of logic and concepts, so it is much easier to extend what you know and answer a wide range of questions.

If there is time, adding some interleaving is also recommended, preferably a method such as WPW. You may also need to add a lower-order memorisation method such as flashcards at the same time.

## Opportunistic Retrieval

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Opportunistic Retrieval

Retrieval means taking information from long-term memory into working memory so you can recall it, solve problems, and answer questions. Some of that recall has to be planned, meaning you set aside time for retrieval sessions. That is the usual case in a purely academic setting. Opportunistic retrieval is the recall that happens as a natural part of the day, without extra scheduled time. For a lot of professional learning, those built-in chances are the better scenario, because they save time.

A doctor studying heart disease who then sees heart-disease patients is already doing interleaved retrieval. Study the topic on Monday, see a patient with it on Tuesday, and think back to what you learned. That is retrieval, even though it was not set up as a practice session. Software developers likewise retrieve programming knowledge as part of the work itself. You can treat those encounters as part of your spaced and interleaved practice.

After such a day, label what the session actually was. Ask whether you used the knowledge at a higher order or a lower order. Ask also whether that matched the type of knowledge involved, meaning declarative knowledge you can state or procedural knowledge you can carry out. That labeling shows the gap. If you only needed fact-level recall, and you used the knowledge purely declaratively, the declarative side may be fine. A higher-order gap is then likely, because the encounter did not ask you to use higher-order knowledge.

You then have two options. You can run a planned retrieval session aimed at the higher-order part you think you missed. Or you can try to convert the experience so it uses more higher-order knowledge. That can mean forming a different strategy, evaluating the management plan you created, or taking a more strategic, bigger-picture view of the topic. Time pressure and job duties often mean you cannot change how the task is done, and you have to move on. Either way you have still saved the time you would have scheduled just to test yourself.

If what you learn transfers closely to how you perform the job, and you use the knowledge in exactly the way you need it, opportunistic practice may be enough to keep the memory strong. You may not need any planned retrieval at all. Expertise still asks for good long-term retention and good depth. If you only need enough knowledge to solve one isolated problem or to present something to a client, and you do not need true expertise, daily recall at work may already meet the goal. The same thinking applies to an exam. You may still run planned sessions without needing perfect retrieval on every item, unless you will need real expertise in that material later.

Do not optimize past the goal. Optimization is always aimed at a goal. If you are holding yourself to a much higher standard than the goal requires, that extra optimizing is counterproductive. For a low-interest elective that only has to get you through an exam in about a month, you do not need doctoral-level higher-order expertise. Get the answers to a good-enough standard, keep memory from leaking too much, get enough retention depth, and fill the lower-order detail you need.

There is a flip side. You usually cannot control what work will force you to retrieve, but you can choose the material you prepare in advance, such as over a weekend. If you know you will have to learn a topic at some point, and you also know what work will force you to retrieve, study it in the order the work will ask for it. Do not wait for the order of a curriculum or a certificate course. A simple medical example is a cardiology department now and a renal department, working with kidneys, in two months. If a course teaches kidneys today and the heart two months later, learn the heart material now so the daily opportunities are used, and learn the kidney material later. Real life is more complicated than that, and the aim is still to match what you learn to the natural interleaving opportunities you already have. That matching makes the retrieval burden more manageable.

You can also add activities that create other orders of interleaving, such as running a teaching session for a teammate or a junior, which creates value for the team while testing your retrieval.

## Reconstruction - Retrieval Beyond Recall

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Reconstruction is retrieval that asks a learner to build new, valid connections from what they know, not just to bring back connections they already made. It sits above recognition and recall. It suits how memory works, since memory is a web of connected ideas that is rebuilt every time it is used. To reconstruct, a learner has to know each idea well enough to judge which new links hold and which cannot. The method works at the top levels of thinking, evaluating and creating, and it prepares learners for unfamiliar exam questions that ask for a shape they never practiced. Because it takes longer than recall, reconstruction works best blended with it, with more reconstruction early in learning and more recall later.

## Core takeaways

- Recognition is weakest, recall is stronger, and reconstruction is strongest.
- Reconstruction makes connections the learner has not made before from the same ideas.
- It matches how memory works, because all remembering is rebuilding.
- It finds gaps that recall misses, where a link can be remembered but the idea cannot be used differently.
- Blend it with recall, leaning on reconstruction early and recall later.

## Why the demand matches how memory works

Memory does not work like a box from which stored items are pulled out whole. It is a set of ideas held together by relationships, and those relationships give knowledge its structure and meaning. Remembering means rebuilding that structure, so every act of memory is already a reconstruction. If memory is rebuilt anyway, the most honest test of it is to ask for a rebuild directly.

## Three ways to ask for a new shape

A reconstruction prompt hands the learner familiar pieces and asks for a new arrangement. Three kinds of prompt do this:

1. Link one idea to two others in a way not studied before, and check whether the link holds.
2. Put two ideas in a new situation together and work out what follows, the way a fan might imagine two favorite characters from different stories meeting.
3. Try a fact or equation in a situation where it was never taught, because it looks similar to one where it applies, and judge whether the fact really carries over.

## Deciding two pieces cannot connect

Part of reconstruction is ruling links out. A learner has to judge that one connection makes sense, that another is impossible and that two ideas can connect in one way but not another. Those judgments require knowing each idea well, which is why reconstruction tests mastery so directly.

## How this compares with recall

Recall, in its active form, gives the learner a few pieces and asks them to bring back the rest, for example by writing an essay or explaining a difference. That is far more realistic than recognition. Reconstruction goes one step further, asking the learner to build something they have not built before from the same pieces. Recognition barely registers as mastery. Recall can test anything from basic facts to problem solving, depending on what is recalled. Reconstruction works almost entirely at the levels of evaluating and creating, though it relies on the lower levels underneath.

## What the test shows about unfamiliar exam questions

An unfamiliar exam question supplies known pieces and wants them arranged into a shape the student never thought could be made. A student who has practiced reconstruction sees that their knowledge stretches to the new shape, so the question is less of a shock. Practicing reconstruction is practice for exactly that moment.

## Knowledge a person owns can be rearranged

Knowledge that is truly owned can be shaped freely. People already do this with hobbies and stories they know well, imagining new scenarios and drawing new conclusions without effort. Reconstruction trains the same freedom in a subject, so the learner can build any relationship a new question calls for, and even propose hypotheses and conjectures of their own.

## Recall can pass while use fails

A learner can recall that a relationship exists and still be unable to use the idea in a new way. If asked to use one point to link two others and they cannot, they did not know the point as well as they thought. Recall alone never exposes this gap, because it only asks for links already made. Reconstruction is the way to find it.

## Past papers and reconstructive prompts

Past papers do have value, because they test recall and application, which is why students who have nothing better find them effective. Working through past papers to recall and apply everything is brute force, though. Reconstructive prompts are more targeted, since they seek out gaps instead of covering everything.

## How much recall to keep

Reconstruction takes longer than recall, so a mix works best. For some learners a split of 90% reconstruction to 10% recall is efficient, while others manage better with an even split. Over a course of study, most learners do best leaning on reconstruction early, when it finds gaps quickly and thoroughly. Later they shift toward recall to sharpen detailed retrieval.

## Where this demand sits among the methods

Reconstruction belongs to the later stages of a learning-skills course, after a learner can already process information well and take control of what they learn and when. It builds on the habit of working at higher levels of thinking from the start of learning, since the brain learns most efficiently there, and it brings that habit into revision.

## How to practice this

1. After learning a topic, list its main ideas on one page.
2. Pick one idea and try to link it to two others in a new way, then judge whether the link holds.
3. Note any link that cannot be made, and treat it as a gap to study.
4. Mix in recall, starting with mostly reconstruction and adding more recall closer to the exam.

## Revision

# Revision

Revision exists so you can recall the material you have studied. Revision can take more than one form. Some people only practise recognising the material. Others lean on cues to help them bring it back. True recall is better, where you can remember the material and use it even when no cue is present.

Revision means returning to what you have learned and then finding gaps and filling them. A thorough strategy will catch small details you missed when first learning the material. Many students revise only by sitting past papers or reading notes again. Those strategies are often passive and do not use a high enough level of challenge.

A safe strategy follows a few principles. Mix different methods across different orders and kinds of knowledge. Use retrieval methods chosen specifically to find your gaps and close them. Always use recall over recognition. Get more from past papers by using more active methods that add detail and explanation, rather than only solving and checking answers.

One high-yield sequence is test, target, and teach.

When you test yourself, apply your knowledge and create your own questions. Premade questions belong in the same mix. You can share these questions with friends in a small study group. Writing your own challenge questions and exam questions can work better than using past papers and ready-made practice items. Even if you do not fully know the right answer, you learn more by writing the question and trying to answer it than you would by answering a question that already exists. Premade questions save time and familiarise you with the style of questioning in your assessments, though there is no guarantee that style will stay the same. They also find gaps by testing you in ways you would not have thought of. Self-made questions strengthen your understanding of material you have already learned from a different angle, and find gaps in content you have already learned. They also let you practise retrieval in the orders and types of knowledge you need, because it is easier to aim at weak spots when you can choose the kind of challenge. Leaning too heavily on premade questions is one reason unexpected questions unsettle students so badly. Writing your own questions helps most in subjects such as maths and physics, which can trip students up with real-world applications of formulae that they do not usually think of. Good learners mix questions you write yourself with questions written by others.

When you target your retrieval, look for areas you feel shaky in, or have not used before. Begin with the weak areas, even when that feels uncomfortable.

When you teach content, some methods are more effective than others. Do not only explain. Teach, meaning change the sequence and the approach when you present the material, so it makes sense for someone who has not learned it before. An effective way to find weak spots is to say why a concept matters before you bring in any terminology, so you have to explain it in ordinary words. One example is imagining you are explaining the idea to a child of about 10 years.

Knowledge gaps are any parts you struggle to recall, any parts you get incorrect, and any parts you feel unconfident about. They are not only the times you get things incorrect. A later definition adds that a knowledge gap is any missing fact or relationship that matters and belongs. Every time you find a gap, review your material and encode it again. The methods used to learn it at first can be used again. Try to link the material more tightly, or try to understand it from a fresh angle. This helps improve retention and depth.

Different struggles point to different kinds of gaps. If you struggle to pick the right approach and strategy for solving a problem, that indicates a declarative knowledge gap. That means a gap in facts about how concepts relate to each other and what they are for. This kind of gap is common in engineering, coding, physics, and maths when too little attention is given to the conceptual network and too much focus goes to repeating procedures. If you struggle to recall information, that indicates a broad retrieval gap. Fill it with richer relational encoding or a more basic memorisation method, depending on what kind of information it is and which layer it sits in. If a large amount of information is lost, the cause is almost always a weak grouping structure. Check your maps for waterfalling, meaning all the connections spread outwards or down a hierarchy with no side-to-side links. Also check for spiderwebbing, meaning many scattered arrows that look like webs between the leaves of a tree. That happens when side-to-side links are added after the groups were already formed without using relationships. Ensure the groups feel intuitive, and beware of a simple question-and-answer habit, which can undermine the inquiry process. If you struggle with consistently solving problems because of errors, that indicates a procedural knowledge gap. That means a gap in the skill of doing the work, caused by too little practice and too little execution-style interleaving. In some cases those errors come from misunderstandings of the concepts themselves. If you have trouble applying knowledge in new contexts or in more subtle settings, that points to a higher-order knowledge gap that missing relationships have caused.

When you look for gaps, do not dodge your weak spots. Test at more than one order. Leave the answer sheet aside until you have built your own through self-study first. Checking answers too early often creates an illusion of knowledge. Trouble answering a question points to a gap, and even a correct answer might have been luck. Difficulty matters more than correctness.

Try to do all three parts of test, target, and teach at least one time before you go on to later lessons. You can continue to refine the skill over time, but the gaps you find will change how you interpret future learning, so it helps to go through this at least once or twice first. Two signs that you should spend longer practising are that you struggle to apply the methods, or that the work feels very easy yet no knowledge gaps show up. If you struggle, it is worth spending another couple of weeks practising them. If you do not struggle and also do not find any gaps, that most likely indicates the method was carried out incorrectly. Unless your knowledge really is spotless, you ought to be able to find gaps. Retrieval methods exist to surface gaps. Finding none means the practice did not work. If the method felt easy and still did not work, it may mean that you did not cover every needed order of learning or every needed kind of knowledge test.

A later recap adds three habits that should already be in use. Ensure that the gaps between revision sessions keep growing. Do at least one or two retrieval sessions in the first 10 days, then keep widening the gaps for later sessions. It is inefficient to always practise in identical conditions. The brain learns much better when each practice round has small variations. Actively look for gaps by applying the Aim step once more to the study material and to your GRINDE map. That map is the mind map refined during the Skin step against grouped, reflective, interconnected, non-verbal, directional, and emphasised checks. You will see things on that map that you do not know well, and you can deliberately ask questions to explore those gaps in a more targeted way.

## Rote Learning and Memorisation

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Rote Learning and Memorisation

Rote learning handles the outer layer of information, also called the fourth layer. These are arbitrary details that you simply have to remember. It is always a last resort. Before reaching for it, move through the three inner layers. Grasp the logic first, grow the concepts around that logic, then lock in the concrete important details. Whatever is left after that is what you memorize by repetition.

A learner who relies on memorizing is working at a low level. A learner working at a high level actively shrinks the pile of things that must be memorized. Once the reason something is true is clear, you do not need to memorize it, and learning through that understanding is much faster. The details that still belong to rote learning are specific, superficial, and often used in only one place. They do not connect to many other concepts. Examples include dates and the names of enzymes, catalysts, or proteins. They also include the exact steps or order of a process, the sequence for using a formula, a constant, or a titration number.

Two different kinds of mental work are in play. Conceptual learning is easy to remember because it fits together in a logical, intuitive way, and it holds with less effort. Rote learning is demanded by the way schoolwork is currently set up. It does not use an efficient part of the brain, and it needs time and repetition to settle. The only real time savings come from making conceptual learning as efficient as possible, which frees time for the memorizing that exam preparation will still require. If the conceptual work slips, the extra memorizing grows into a spiral.

Used together, the two approaches cover all four layers. You can bring back the specific details and also see how the pieces belong together. Once a strong foundation of logic and relationships is in place, cards used well can fill remaining gaps. A technique named the modified method of loci is described as a way to memorize hundreds of items in a single sitting. Even these memorization techniques still draw on the same retention ideas taught for conceptual work.

A list of other habits to keep running appears in the same lesson (a hunting-themed encoding system and several scheduling and focus habits). Those items are named there and not described, so they are not unpacked on this page.

That pass tests several things at once. It tests whether you can explain the whole clearly, which is a check on your higher-order structure and on which relationships you have treated as most important. It tests whether you can move into the parts and back to the whole smoothly, which is a check on the links between layers of the topic. It tests whether you can explain each part accurately, which is a check on detailed recall. Covering those levels is what makes the method demanding. It takes a great deal of practice to do well, and even someone already fluent in the program's other methods can take months to master it.

The work often feels slow. You become sharply aware of how you are speaking and of which point you will explain next. That slowness is the point. It forces a deep look at how the knowledge is organized, against questions such as what makes the most sense and why something matters. You may also feel anxious because gaps keep appearing. Those gaps are useful. They are often ones that a confident-but-shallow sense of understanding had been hiding.

A few habits help the pass go the way it should. Use reverse explanations, meaning you give the idea itself first and only then add its name. The ordinary order names the term and then defines it, which tends to stay isolated and fact-based. Explaining first and labelling last stays relational, feels more intuitive, and becomes more memorable. Look for how each part matters to the big picture. Do not lock yourself into the order you first learned or first taught. A good pass may follow a different flow from the layers of your GRINDE map, meaning the mind map refined during the Skin step. The checks on that map are grouped, reflective, interconnected, non-verbal, directional, and emphasised. Teach from what you can recall, not from notes in front of you. Sketch a map while you talk. Speak out loud so you cannot rush. Wait at least three days after finishing that GRINDE map before the pass. Use full recall to find gaps, then restudy those gaps until recall is clean. If the first attempts feel like struggle, keep going. The struggle is the gaps showing up.

One later stage of the teaching, described in the notes after an earlier stage that is not in the file, adds technical language. First you attach terms to the pieces you have already explained, which is called labelling. Then you use those terms to explain each piece in full detail, which is called technical elaboration. Introducing the terms first and explaining them later is the weaker order. Explaining the topic without leaning on the terms first is what tests whether the concepts and relationships are actually understood. By the end of this stage the listener should know the technical names and should understand how the details fit. That helps with questions that ask only for a definition or a recalled fact.

WPW is also an example of high-volume retrieval practice, meaning a pass that covers a topic thoroughly enough to catch mistakes and weak spots quickly, including both relationships and specific details. Another example is a brain dump, which is described as a weaker check on relationships and on conceptual understanding. High-volume retrieval belongs in spaced retrieval, meaning later sessions that pull the knowledge back out after some time has passed, and it is especially useful in the early and middle stretch of revision. One suggested rhythm puts a WPW pass at the end of the week. When the volume of new material is high, another pass sits mid-week as well. Before class, prestudy with inquiry-based learning through the Bear Hunter System, and make at least a basic mind map through the first two layers of learning, or the third layer if you can. During class you consolidate the first three layers. The same day after class you consolidate and add the fourth layer, Skin your mind maps, and create flashcards. Flashcards, if used, also fit into leftover scraps of time rather than a session of their own. About three or four weeks after that, and once more before a test if time allows, a later pass can use a brain dump and practice papers.

## Retrieval Challenge

The Retrieval Challenge is a four-week practice plan. It replaces rereading with closed-book reconstruction: the reader shuts the source and writes or says the key ideas from memory. Over the four weeks the attempt gets harder, and the ideas the reader fails to recall decide what gets restudied. Retrieval here means pulling an idea out of memory without looking at the source.

## Core takeaways

- Every study block ends with the source closed and the key ideas rebuilt from memory. Rereading and then quizzing is not the method.
- In week 1 the attempt counts and the score does not. A thin reconstruction is still the week's work.
- In week 2 the same recall is done as teaching, as problem-solving, and as writing, because each form finds gaps the others miss.
- In week 3 recall gets harder by one lever at a time: fewer cues, more delay, or mixed topics.
- In week 4 the reader restudies only the ideas that failed. Restudy after a correct recall adds little. Restudy after a failure adds a lot.
- Research finds that testing beats restudy after a week, and that the effect is larger when the test is harder. Those findings do not set the timing of any week in this plan.

## How the four weeks fit together

Every week uses the same closed-book attempt. What changes is the demand placed on it. Week 1 replaces review with recall. Week 2 varies the form. Week 3 raises the demand. Week 4 uses the misses to choose what to restudy. Week 1 trains the attempt and ignores the score. Week 4 uses the score to find weak areas. Because each week builds on the one before it, the month is one sequence rather than four separate tips. Every week ends with the source closed. What differs is what happens after the reconstruction: another form, a harder demand, or a restudy of only what failed.

[[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval/Spaced Interleaved Retrieval|Spaced Interleaved Retrieval]] is the standing method this month trains: retrieval spread over time and mixed across topics and forms. That page describes the full system. This challenge covers only the four weeks of closed-book practice.

This challenge is one of the five tracks under [[wiki/Dimensions/30-Day Challenges|30-Day Challenges]]. The wider dimension, beyond this month's practice, is [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval|Retrieval]].

## Week 1: closed-book recall

Week 1 replaces passive review with active retrieval. The attempt is the practice. After a first pass through the material, the reader closes the source and rebuilds the key ideas from memory. Rereading and then quizzing is not the week 1 method. The source closes before any recall starts. After a fluent reread the material feels familiar, and the reader skips the reconstruction. The minimum dose is one closed-book reconstruction per study block. That frequency is a default chosen for this plan. No study measured it.

Week 1 trains the act of retrieval, not the score. Accuracy still matters. It is scored in week 4, which uses the misses. A reconstruction that comes out thin still counts as the week's work, because the attempt is what the month trains. A reread with the page still visible is not the method.

## Week 2: changing the form of recall

Week 2 keeps the closed-book attempt and changes its form. Teaching from memory, solving problems from memory, and writing from memory replace the single method of week 1. Teaching from memory can take the shape of a whole-part-whole retell: the reader says the whole topic aloud, then each part, then the whole again. Writing from memory is the same reconstruction as week 1, now one form among three. Problem-solving requires applying the idea. A retell can state the idea without applying it. Different conditions expose different gaps. Teaching out loud can show a gap that a written reconstruction hid. A problem tests a use of the idea that a free retell never asked for. The week's product is a short note of which form found which gap. Skill at recall in one form does not carry over to an unpractised form on its own. That is why the form changes during the month rather than after it.

## Week 3: making recall harder

Week 3 raises the demand on the same closed-book attempt with three levers: fewer cues, more time between exposure and retrieval, or several topics mixed in one session. The reader changes one lever at a time, so that the cause of any drop in recall is clear. Fewer cues means the heading stays and the bullet list does not. More delay means the reconstruction happens at the next session rather than at the end of this one. Mixed topics means two related subjects share the same closed-book pass. The reader logs performance under the higher demand. The log is the week's product: what was recalled, what came out thin, and which lever caused the loss. Research on spacing is the evidence for the delay lever. Research on mixing topics is the evidence for the mixed-topics lever.

## Week 4: using misses to choose what to restudy

Week 4 treats the retrieval session as a diagnostic tool. The gaps, and the ideas that were hard to recall, are the weak areas. Those areas then get targeted re-processing. Restudy after a correct recall adds little. Restudy after a failed recall adds a lot. Week 4 restudies only what failed.

The re-processing itself is described on [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing|Deep Processing Challenge]]: relating the missed idea to other ideas and to why it matters, rather than rereading the page that did not stick.

Each miss can become the next experiment through the four steps of [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management/Kolbs Experiential Cycle|Kolbs Experiential Cycle]]: what failed, how it felt and why, what rule that suggests, and what changes next time.

## What the plan needs first, and what the research says

The plan only works on material that has already been encoded. A closed-book pass that is really a reread, with the page still visible, is not the method. Recall practised in one form does not carry over to other forms on its own. That is why week 2 varies the form.

After a one-week delay, repeated testing has beaten repeated study on a prose passage by a wide margin. Extra study trials after a correct recall have added little. Extra retrieval trials have added a lot. Across the research literature the testing effect is medium in size and grows when the test is more demanding. Those are research findings. They do not set the timing of any week in this plan. This track has no separate week files.

## What the reader has at the end of the month

At the end of the month the reader has two habits: closing the source before recall, and using the misses to choose what to restudy. The month does not produce a mastered spacing system. If week 1 still begins with a fluent reread, the week has not started. If week 4 restudies material that was already recalled, the week has skipped the part that helps. In either case the next step is to repeat that week, not to move to a wider method.

## How to practice this

1. Read the material once. Close the source before any recall. Write the key ideas from memory. If the reconstruction comes out thin, it still counts.
2. Do this closed-book reconstruction at least once per study block. Notice whether the page is still in view during recall. If it is, the attempt is a reread and not the method.
3. In week 2, recall the same material three ways: teach it aloud, solve a problem with it, and write it out. For teaching, say the whole topic, then each part, then the whole again. Write a short note of which form found which gap.
4. In week 3, make the attempt harder with one lever at a time. The levers are fewer cues, more delay, or two related topics in one closed-book pass. Log what was recalled, what came out thin, and which lever caused it.
5. In week 4, restudy only the ideas that failed. Skip restudy of anything recalled correctly. Relate each missed idea to other ideas and to why it matters, rather than rereading the page.
6. If week 1 still begins with a reread, or week 4 restudies what was already recalled, repeat that week. Do not move on to a wider method.

## Reading & Retention

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Reading for retention splits into two stages: consuming information and digesting it. Retention depends on how much stays, not how much goes in, so the two stages have to stay in balance. Digestion depends on the type of information, sorted into five kinds called PACER: procedural, analogous, conceptual, evidence and reference. Procedural information is practiced, analogies are critiqued, and concepts are mapped into a network. Evidence is stored and later rehearsed in use, and reference facts are stored and drilled with flashcards. The common failure is reading more when there is no time to digest, which leads to heavy forgetting. The repair is to consume less and digest more.

## Core takeaways

- Learning happens only when consumption is followed by digestion.
- If there is no time to digest, stop reading or switch tasks, instead of reading faster.
- Sort what you read into the five PACER types while reading.
- Practice procedures early, critique analogies, map concepts, and store evidence and reference facts for later.
- Do not spend reading time memorizing details, because the first three types carry most of the knowledge.

## Consumption and digestion have to balance

Reading has a consumption stage, when information comes in, and a digestion stage, when it is processed into long-term memory. Most people try to improve the first stage by reading faster, speeding up lectures or bingeing audiobooks. What matters is how much stays. Consuming without digesting is like overeating, and forgetting is the result. One estimate holds that up to 90 percent of what is consumed is lost. Spending less time consuming and more time digesting raises the amount of usable knowledge.

Remembering everything is not the goal anyway. A person with perfect recall but weak reasoning would win a test of memorization and lose one of problem solving. The aim is to remember what is needed, in a form that can be used.

The type of information decides how to digest it.

## PACER: five digestion jobs

| Type | What it is | Digestion |
|---|---|---|
| Procedural | How to do something | Practice it early |
| Analogous | Related to something already known | Critique the analogy |
| Conceptual | Facts, theories, principles and how they relate | Map it |
| Evidence | Specific details that make a concept concrete | Store, then rehearse in use |
| Reference | Specific details that do not change understanding | Store, then drill |

Sorting information as it arrives is part of reading actively. Using the wrong process for a type wastes time, weakens memory and makes passive reading more likely.

## Procedural information: practice it

Procedural information explains how to execute something, like a clinical examination technique, code or a language. It is digested by practice, as soon as possible. Reading it, writing notes on it and practicing a week or two later wastes most of the effort, because much of it is forgotten by then. If there is no chance to practice now, the better move is to stop or switch to something else.

## Analogous information: critique the analogy

Analogous information connects to something already known, either from another area or from earlier in the same field. A swimmer learning how muscles contract might notice it resembles their stroke. The digestion step is critique, and it is the step most people skip. Critique asks how exactly the two things are alike and how they differ. It also asks where the analogy breaks, and whether a better or extended analogy would fit. Critiquing ties the new information straight into an existing network instead of leaving it isolated.

That network idea also explains how conceptual information should be handled.

## Knowledge is a network

An expert's knowledge is not stored as a sequence of sentences. It is a highly connected network with no fixed starting point, which lets an expert see which concepts bear on a problem and how they connect. A beginner who learned at the surface sees separate concepts with no links. Books and lectures arrive in a line, but the learner's job is to rebuild the expert's network, not to remember the order of the words.

## Conceptual information: map it

Conceptual information is the "what": facts, explanations, theories, principles, relationships and applications. Most science content is conceptual, and solving problems usually needs both procedural and conceptual knowledge. Digestion means mapping. Nonlinear, network-style notes such as mind maps force the learner to think about each concept and how it connects to the others. The map grows and gets reorganized as reading continues, and analogies found along the way can shape its structure. With no time or materials to map, the answer again is to slow down and consume less.

## Evidence: store it and rehearse it in context

Evidence is detailed, often technical information such as dates, statistics or cases that make a concept concrete. Knowing how a war started is conceptual, while the specific events, people and places are evidence. Store it the moment it is spotted, in the map, a second-brain app, flashcards or a document. Rehearse it later, at the end of a day or week. Rehearsal means using it the way it will be needed: solving problems, writing detailed answers, explaining or teaching it, or using it as support in an essay.

## Reference: store it, and recall it only when required

Reference information is fine detail that does not change understanding, such as a constant's exact value, a specific gene or a list of attributes. Store it the same way as evidence. If it has to be recalled without notes, drill it with spaced repetition flashcards for a short daily session, because it will only ever be used as direct recall. Evidence and reference look alike, and mixing them up can waste hours a week.

## The working loop

While consuming, identify each piece of information by type. Practice procedures and critique analogies right away, and add concepts to a growing map. Drop evidence and reference details into storage without stopping to memorize them. Afterward, rehearse evidence in use and review reference cards. Whenever there is no time to digest, pause consumption until there is.

## The common failure and its repair

The common failure is pushing on with reading when there is no time for the right digestion, in the hope of getting through more. Reading and rereading details to force them in is a close second. Both lead to forgetting, and they hand the future self the job of finding what was lost and reading it again. The repair is to consume less, digest each type with its own process, and keep detail work out of reading time.

## What good reading looks like, and the warning signs

Good reading is slower but leaves something usable: a map that grows, analogies that have been tested, procedures already tried, and details stored for later. The main warning sign is passive reading, reaching the bottom of a page with no memory of what it said. Rereading to memorize, piling up notes without digesting, and planning to practice later are others.

## Where this fits in the vault

A knowledge base or second brain is the storage step for evidence and reference information. Conceptual maps can live there too. The store holds the material, and the learner still does the digesting through practice, critique, mapping and rehearsal.

## What the research supports, and what it does not

Most of the method comes from one learning coach's teaching and has not been independently checked. The forgetting figure is presented as an upper estimate from some studies. The benefit of analogies for retention and understanding is described as repeatedly shown. The five-type sort and its matching processes are a practical system refined over years of personal use, not a tested model.

## Objections, cost, quit signals, and checks

The obvious objection is that the system feels forced. It is unnatural on purpose, because the brain's capacity to consume is limited and unstructured reading wastes it. The cost is slower consumption and extra time spent digesting. If there is no time pressure and depth does not matter, reading freely is fine. A quick check after any session asks whether you can explain the map you built and whether you used the procedure you read about.

## How to practice this

1. Before reading, set aside time to digest as well as to read.
2. While reading, label each piece of information with its PACER type.
3. Practice procedures right away, and critique each analogy.
4. Build and reorganize a map of the concepts as you go.
5. Store evidence and reference details without stopping to memorize.
6. Later, rehearse evidence in use and review reference flashcards.
7. If digestion falls behind, stop reading until it catches up.

## Rules of Effective Memorization

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Rules of Effective Memorization

Four memory rules matter whenever you need to learn a large system of information and still be able to use it for real tasks. They were taught as the opening of a course on characters, and the examples below stay with that writing system. The same four rules keep returning. They are meaningfulness, organization, association, and visualization. Used together, they raise how much meaning the thing has for you, which is what makes it stay.

## Meaningfulness

A thing you are trying to memorize sticks better when it means more to you. The more you understand, the easier the work becomes. If only one rule survives, it should be this one.

Copying a character stroke by stroke, saying the sound and the meaning out loud, trains you to see a pile of strokes. Strokes generally do not carry meaning, so that view makes memory harder. Seeing the character as components is already better, because there are usually fewer components than strokes. Many resources still split characters into pieces that do no job inside that character. Functional components do a job, because they express sound or meaning. If you understand that, you can use the sound and meaning of the word you want to write to recall the form. You can also take a character apart while reading to recover a pronunciation or a meaning you have already learned, or to make a better guess about one you have not.

The strongest level is understanding how those functional components do their work. That includes how sound components express sound, including common variations, how meaning components express meaning, including common variations, and how sound and meaning connect across characters. For a standalone piece, or a character that is only that one piece, that means knowing its form, its meaning, and its sound.

## Organization

Brains hold organized material more readily than loose material. A string of letters that does not form words is hard to keep. The same letters with spaces that create real words become easier. Organizing those words into a meaningful sentence is easier still. Organization and meaningfulness travel together. You can organize into sequences or into categories.

Characters already offer two kinds of organization. Inside a character, notice the functional components, and across characters, notice how they relate as a system. A sound series groups characters that share one sound component. Their pronunciations are often not exactly the same. Meaning series group characters that share the same meaning component, and those meanings are not always identical to the component itself. The eye component can sit in characters about looking, seeing, appearance, sleep, and the eyes. A component for metal or gold can sit in characters about money, silver, iron, a bell, a clock, or melting. Not every meaning link is obvious in modern speech. One water component appears in a name that began as a river, then the people beside that river, then a dynasty.

It is usually a poor plan to learn a whole sound series at once. It works better to attach a new character to a word you already know well. Sound series and meaning series also do not line up with the order spoken language needs. When you learn a new character, check whether it has a sound component and try to match it with others you already know that share that piece, expecting the sounds to differ somewhat.

## Association and visualization

When you learn something new, attach it to something you already know. What you already know is usually more meaningful, and that added meaning makes the new item easier to keep. For a new character, that can mean other characters or components you already have. One learner could remember the familiar left side of a character and kept losing the right side, until that same right-hand piece turned up in another known character. After that match, the form stuck. You can also associate by sound series, meaning series, or similar form or meaning.

Things you can picture are easier to remember. You can usually walk through your house in your mind and count the doors without preparing. Many characters and components began as pictures of real objects and later lost that look. Comparing an older, more picture-like form with the modern form makes the object easier to see again, which makes the modern form easier to hold. The same component can also change differently in different surroundings, so two similar-looking pieces may not share an origin.

A less common character can be worth learning when it fits a group you are already studying, though as a rule you should start with the characters and pieces people meet most often.

## Using the rules together

Other memory rules exist and are less central. They were not covered in these videos.

While you learn, keep looking for ways to understand the object more clearly. For a single component, picture the real-world object when you can. For a character made of several components, understand the internal logic of those pieces, then look for system-level links that tie it to characters you already know. Use common characters to help you learn less common ones. Connect forms, meanings, and sounds on purpose, so later recall has more than one path. The rules work better in combination. Understanding, associating, picturing, and organizing at the same time is the strongest mix.
