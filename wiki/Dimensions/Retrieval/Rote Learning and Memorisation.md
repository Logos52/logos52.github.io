# Rote Learning and Memorisation

Rote learning is memorising facts that have no reason behind them, such as dates, names, constants, or a sequence of steps. It is the last step in learning a topic, used only for what remains once the topic makes sense. Two tools cover most of it: flashcards for single facts, and a drawn memory story for ordered lists.

## Core takeaways

- Understand the topic first, then memorise only what is left.
- A fact you understand the reason for needs no card.
- Flashcards test one fact each, in short bursts.
- Each card costs three to five future reviews.
- A big pile of cards points to weak understanding.
- Lists of 20 to 150 items suit a drawn story.

## What to memorise

Learning a topic goes in four layers: first its main logic, then the concepts built on that logic, then the important details, and last the details that have no reason behind them. Only that last layer needs rote work. About 30% of university material and 10 to 20% of school material falls there. Everything else is cheaper to learn by understanding it.

- Dates, names of chemicals, constants, and sequences are typical.
- A fact that links to many others belongs in understanding.
- Important details can go on a card as a backup.
- Rote and mental-picture methods help, but only for narrow tasks.

## Flashcards

A flashcard has a question on one side and the answer on the other, so it tests you. A card that only restates notes is a summary card, and reading it is rereading. Cards need spaced review, with the gap growing each time, which is why a digital app beats paper. The app tracks each card and schedules its next review.

- One fact per card, asked as a precise question.
- Several facts on one card make marking it impossible.
- Review in three- to ten-minute gaps, such as a bus wait.
- Never do a long batch of cards in one sitting.
- A topic spanning weeks should need fewer than 50 cards.
- Heading toward 200 cards means the concepts were not learned.

## Managing the deck

Cards pile up faster than they can be reviewed, so the deck needs pruning. Three rules, cut, combine and critique, handle most cases. A card that is too easy gets cut or merged into another, and a card that keeps failing sends you back to study the fact.

- Cut: right on its first three tries without looking.
- Combine: merge two mastered cards into a harder question.
- Critique: wrong three times in five, so stop drilling it.
- Read around a failing fact until it connects to something.
- Five to ten minutes of reading beats weeks of misses.
- Confident mistakes, once corrected, are remembered well.

## The drawn memory story

The classic method of loci places items along a route through a familiar building. The version used here drops the building. You turn each item into an image, chain the images into one story, and draw the story into your notes. About 100 items take 30 to 40 minutes and can last six months with two or three reviews.

```
item 1       item 2            item 3
butter  -->  a city made  -->  a tray the size
             of butter         of a skyscraper
        each image leads into the next
```

- Absurd: exaggerate each image until it is strange.
- Spread out: move the story through different places.
- Chained: each item causes or leads to the next.
- A rough drawing works as well as a careful one.
- Write the plain list beside the drawing to check against.
- Give each digit a fixed image to memorise numbers.

## Evidence

Three findings from the research bear on these two tools. One measures what training in the memory story does to recall. One looks at the brains of people who have trained in it. One explains why strange images work and where that stops.

- Six weeks of loci training roughly doubled word-list recall.
- Trained memorisers use the brain's systems for space and finding the way.
- Their brains are built like anyone else's.
- Strange images help because they stand out from ordinary ones.
- Strangeness helps less when every image is strange.

## Sources

- Dunlosky, J., et al. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*. Rote and imagery techniques rate moderate in utility and narrow in scope; practice testing rates high; rereading rates low. A summary card is rereading on a card. The last-resort rule is about place in the sequence, not a finding that rote cannot encode.
- Rowland, C. A. (2014). The effect of testing versus restudy on retention. *Psychological Bulletin*. Recall tests outperform restudy.
- Wozniak, P. Twenty rules of formulating knowledge. https://supermemo.guru/wiki/20_rules_of_knowledge_formulation : one thing per card, as practitioner rule, not a trial.
- Maguire, E. A., Valentine, E. R., Wilding, J. M., & Kapur, N. (2003). Routes to remembering: the brains behind superior memory. *Nature Neuroscience*. Trained memorisers recruit spatial and navigational systems; no structural brain difference.
- Dresler, M., et al. (2017). Mnemonic training reshapes brain networks to support superior memory. *Neuron*. Six weeks of loci training roughly doubled word-list recall.
- McDaniel, M. A., & Einstein, G. O. (1986). Bizarre imagery as an effective memory aid: the importance of distinctiveness. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*. Bizarre imagery helps by relative distinctiveness, the bound on the absurdity rule.
