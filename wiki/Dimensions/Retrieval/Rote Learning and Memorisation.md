# Rote Learning and Memorisation

Rote learning handles the outer layer of information, also called the fourth layer. These are arbitrary details that you simply have to remember. It is always a last resort. Before reaching for it, move through the three inner layers. Grasp the logic first, grow the concepts around that logic, then lock in the concrete important details. Whatever is left after that is what you memorize by repetition.

A learner who relies on memorizing is working at a low level. A learner working at a high level actively shrinks the pile of things that must be memorized. Once the reason something is true is clear, you do not need to memorize it, and learning through that understanding is much faster. The details that still belong to rote learning are specific, superficial, and often used in only one place. They do not connect to many other concepts. Examples include dates and the names of enzymes, catalysts, or proteins. They also include the exact steps or order of a process, the sequence for using a formula, a constant, or a titration number.

Two different kinds of mental work are in play. Conceptual learning is easy to remember because it fits together in a logical, intuitive way, and it holds with less effort. Rote learning is demanded by the way schoolwork is currently set up. It does not use an efficient part of the brain, and it needs time and repetition to settle. The only real time savings come from making conceptual learning as efficient as possible, which frees time for the memorizing that exam preparation will still require. If the conceptual work slips, the extra memorizing grows into a spiral.

Used together, the two approaches cover all four layers. You can bring back the specific details and also see how the pieces belong together. Once a strong foundation of logic and relationships is in place, cards used well can fill remaining gaps. A technique named the modified method of loci is described as a way to memorize hundreds of items in a single sitting. Even these memorization techniques still draw on the same retention ideas taught for conceptual work.

A list of other habits to keep running appears in the same lesson (a hunting-themed encoding system and several scheduling and focus habits). Those items are named there and not described, so they are not unpacked on this page.

## Open questions

- How does shrinking the pile of things that must be memorized change how the remaining details get learned?
- How does saving conceptual work for later change the amount of repetition an exam then seems to require?
