# Group Study

Group study can help people keep one another going, review material, and get ready for assessments. Without structure it can also make it easier to delay work and distract each other. The structure offered here is three major roles that you appoint in the group.

The leader steers how the discussion moves and presses for deeper exploration. Leaders steer and manage the group's attention. They keep the discussion aligned with the agenda and the learning objectives, encourage participation, and look for extra viewpoints and deeper discussion. They press people past the edge of what feels comfortable to know, and help the group meet cognitive difficulty, meaning work that feels hard because it stretches what people currently know. Together, the timekeeper and the fact-checker keep that role in balance. The timekeeper helps stop the discussion running too long, and the fact-checker helps keep its accuracy high.

The timekeeper keeps up the pace and pressure. They watch whether the group is running over time on a topic, which may leave too little time for other points of equal or greater importance. This role also keeps track of the main points and recaps the ground already covered when the group stalls and needs to gather its thoughts. They should pay attention to the group's energy and focus, and suggest timed breaks or step up the urgency during teaching or discussion so attention returns to a strong level.

The fact-checker brings challenge, inquiry, and accuracy. They pay careful attention to what the other members say and look for loopholes, inaccuracies, or probing questions that the group may not have thought of. They should ask questions such as why something is important, why it happens, or "So what?". They have to seek out viewpoints that were skipped and links that nobody has drawn yet. They ought to keep search tools, other resources, and an AI ready, so they can check whether statements hold up and whether a challenging question is worth asking. This role is one of the most difficult and also one of the most important. It usually goes to the person with the most expertise and the strongest higher-order thinking. In every situation, though, roles should be swapped so each person can fill each role.

When the group is larger than three people, add more fact-checkers. When only two people are present, drop the leader role. Rotate roles often. You may switch after two hours or three hours, or after each session.

If someone has checked out of the learning and blocks efficient progress, take them out of the group.

## Open questions

- How does rotating through directing, timekeeping, and challenge change what each person notices in the same material?
- How does being responsible for other people's accuracy change the kind of questions that get asked?
