# Group Study

Structured group study is a small study group run with fixed roles, used to quiz and teach each other before an exam. Without structure, a group tends to chat and drift, and it teaches less than studying alone for the same time. With the roles assigned, it trains fast, accurate recall under pressure and finds gaps that one person working alone would miss.

- Unstructured group discussion is a poor use of study time.
- Assign the roles of leader, timekeeper and fact-checker.
- The core activities are quizzing and teaching each other.
- The fact-checker role is the hardest to do well.
- Rotate roles so everyone plays each one.
- Remove a member who is disengaged and holding the group back.

## The roles

The leader sets the direction, the timekeeper stops the group spending too long on one point, and the fact-checker keeps the content accurate. The timekeeper and fact-checker also stop the leader from running the session alone. Explaining to others is itself a form of recall practice, and the leader and fact-checker roles force more of it.

- Leader: sets the agenda, topic and activity for each block.
- Leader: pushes people to the edge of what they know.
- Leader: asks for objections before moving to the next topic.
- Timekeeper: holds the group to the time agreed per topic.
- Timekeeper: can propose more time when a topic turns out important.
- Timekeeper: summarises when the group is stuck, and calls breaks.
- Fact-checker: listens for errors, gaps and missed connections.
- Fact-checker: asks "Why is that important?", "Why does that happen?", "So what?".
- Fact-checker: keeps a search engine, sources and an AI chatbot ready.

## How to run it

The fact-checker needs no special expertise, only the habit of questioning every statement and trying to prove it wrong. The role usually goes to the strongest member at first, and everyone should take a turn. With more than three people, add fact-checkers. With two people, drop the leader and share that job.

- Rotate roles after each session, or every two to three hours.
- Count the fact-checker's catches to make it a game.
- Quiz or teach, and avoid open-ended chat with no goal.

## A stronger variant

For practice questions, a longer method gets more out of a pair or group. Each person writes a practice exam with harder questions than the real one, and a model answer sheet to go with it. The exams are swapped and answered from memory. The method takes several sessions, and for fact-based subjects it finds more gaps per hour than most other recall methods.

- Write a practice exam with harder questions and a model answer sheet.
- Swap exams and answer from memory, marking uncertain answers.
- Write your own answer sheet for the exam you took.
- Compare the two answer sheets and discuss every disagreement.

## Evidence

A pooled analysis from 1999 of small-group learning in university science, maths and engineering found an average effect size of about 0.51, which counts as a medium effect. The gains come from structured cooperative work, and open discussion without a task does worse than a set method.
