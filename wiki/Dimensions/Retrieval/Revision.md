# Revision

Revision is going back to material already studied to find what is missing, fix it, and check that it can be used. First learning always leaves gaps, and revision is where they get caught before an exam or a job exposes them. A simple loop of test, target and teach covers most of what good revision needs.

## Takeaways

- Revision exists to find gaps and fix them.
- Test yourself, target your weakest area, teach it back.
- Start with what you are worst at.
- Recall from a blank page, without skimming notes first.
- A session that finds no gaps was probably done wrong.
- Change your method each time you revise.

## The loop

The loop is test, target and teach, and the steps can run in any order. Testing means answering questions that force you to apply the material. Targeting means spending the time on the weakest area first. Teaching means explaining a topic to an imagined student until it makes sense to them.

```
   test -----> gap found
    ^             |
    |             v
  teach <------ target
```

- Test with ready-made questions and questions you write yourself.
- Ready-made ones show the exam style and surprise you.
- Your own ones let you aim at a weakness.
- Relying only on ready-made ones leaves you weak on surprise questions.
- Most people drift toward revising what they already know well.
- Spending six hours in ten on strong topics wastes most of them.
- Teaching means choosing order, comparisons and examples.
- Reciting a list of facts does not count as teaching.
- Explain why an idea matters before naming its terms.
- Use words a ten-year-old would follow.

## Recall from a blank page

Recognising material when you see it feels like knowing it, but an exam gives no prompt. A glance at notes brings the answer back in a fraction of a second, which makes it feel known. Real recall means producing the answer with nothing in view. Skimming notes just before a recall session spoils it.

- Do not look at the material for about 48 hours first.
- Use flashcards away from your notes, for example on a bus.
- Glancing at notes first is fine only for brand-new material.

## Common mistakes

The most common mistake is revising the same way every time, which stops turning up anything new. Another is passive revision, such as rereading or recalling one item over and over. Leaning on past papers is a third. A past paper finds a weakness only by luck, the supply of papers runs out, and every question already known is time spent finding nothing.

- A blind spot is an angle on a topic you always miss.
- Blind spots are what surprise exam questions test.
- Hunt gaps starting at your weakest area and working outward.
- Ask questions about old material you cannot yet answer.
- For example, ask whether one idea could connect to another.

## Reading a gap

A gap is anything you cannot recall, get wrong, or feel unsure about. Different kinds of gap need different fixes, so it helps to name the kind before studying the material again. Study it again by connecting it more deeply or from a new angle.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| cannot pick an approach | weak grasp of how ideas relate | rebuild links between concepts |
| cannot recall | poor grouping when learned | regroup, or memorise |
| errors when solving | too little practice | mixed problem practice |
| cannot apply in new cases | missing relationships | reconstruct links |

## Timing

Space each revision further from the last, roughly two to three times the previous gap. Fit one or two revisions into the first ten days, then aim for about a week, then about a month. Teaching suits the one-week revision because it covers a lot quickly, and past papers and hard problems suit the one-month revision.

- Vary the form each time: map, speak, draw, teach.
- For long-term upkeep, move to spaced self-tests that mix topics.
