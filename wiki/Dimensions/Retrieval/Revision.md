# Revision

Revision exists so you can recall the material you have studied. Revision can take more than one form. Some people only practise recognising the material. Others lean on cues to help them bring it back. True recall is better, where you can remember the material and use it even when no cue is present.

Revision means returning to what you have learned and then finding gaps and filling them. A thorough strategy will catch small details you missed when first learning the material. Many students revise only by sitting past papers or reading notes again. Those strategies are often passive and do not use a high enough level of challenge.

A safe strategy follows a few principles. Mix different methods across different orders and kinds of knowledge. Use retrieval methods chosen specifically to find your gaps and close them. Always use recall over recognition. Get more from past papers by using more active methods that add detail and explanation, rather than only solving and checking answers.

One high-yield sequence is test, target, and teach.

When you test yourself, apply your knowledge and create your own questions. Premade questions belong in the same mix. You can share these questions with friends in a small study group. Writing your own challenge questions and exam questions can work better than using past papers and ready-made practice items. Even if you do not fully know the right answer, you learn more by writing the question and trying to answer it than you would by answering a question that already exists. Premade questions save time and familiarise you with the style of questioning in your assessments, though there is no guarantee that style will stay the same. They also find gaps by testing you in ways you would not have thought of. Self-made questions strengthen your understanding of material you have already learned from a different angle, and find gaps in content you have already learned. They also let you practise retrieval in the orders and types of knowledge you need, because it is easier to aim at weak spots when you can choose the kind of challenge. Leaning too heavily on premade questions is one reason unexpected questions unsettle students so badly. Writing your own questions helps most in subjects such as maths and physics, which can trip students up with real-world applications of formulae that they do not usually think of. Good learners mix questions you write yourself with questions written by others.

When you target your retrieval, look for areas you feel shaky in, or have not used before. Begin with the weak areas, even when that feels uncomfortable.

When you teach content, some methods are more effective than others. Do not only explain. Teach, meaning change the sequence and the approach when you present the material, so it makes sense for someone who has not learned it before. An effective way to find weak spots is to say why a concept matters before you bring in any terminology, so you have to explain it in ordinary words. One example is imagining you are explaining the idea to a child of about 10 years.

Knowledge gaps are any parts you struggle to recall, any parts you get incorrect, and any parts you feel unconfident about. They are not only the times you get things incorrect. A later definition adds that a knowledge gap is any missing fact or relationship that matters and belongs. Every time you find a gap, review your material and encode it again. The methods used to learn it at first can be used again. Try to link the material more tightly, or try to understand it from a fresh angle. This helps improve retention and depth.

Different struggles point to different kinds of gaps. If you struggle to pick the right approach and strategy for solving a problem, that indicates a declarative knowledge gap. That means a gap in facts about how concepts relate to each other and what they are for. This kind of gap is common in engineering, coding, physics, and maths when too little attention is given to the conceptual network and too much focus goes to repeating procedures. If you struggle to recall information, that indicates a broad retrieval gap. Fill it with richer relational encoding or a more basic memorisation method, depending on what kind of information it is and which layer it sits in. If a large amount of information is lost, the cause is almost always a weak grouping structure. Check your maps for waterfalling, meaning all the connections spread outwards or down a hierarchy with no side-to-side links. Also check for spiderwebbing, meaning many scattered arrows that look like webs between the leaves of a tree. That happens when side-to-side links are added after the groups were already formed without using relationships. Ensure the groups feel intuitive, and beware of a simple question-and-answer habit, which can undermine the inquiry process. If you struggle with consistently solving problems because of errors, that indicates a procedural knowledge gap. That means a gap in the skill of doing the work, caused by too little practice and too little execution-style interleaving. In some cases those errors come from misunderstandings of the concepts themselves. If you have trouble applying knowledge in new contexts or in more subtle settings, that points to a higher-order knowledge gap that missing relationships have caused.

When you look for gaps, do not dodge your weak spots. Test at more than one order. Leave the answer sheet aside until you have built your own through self-study first. Checking answers too early often creates an illusion of knowledge. Trouble answering a question points to a gap, and even a correct answer might have been luck. Difficulty matters more than correctness.

Try to do all three parts of test, target, and teach at least one time before you go on to later lessons. You can continue to refine the skill over time, but the gaps you find will change how you interpret future learning, so it helps to go through this at least once or twice first. Two signs that you should spend longer practising are that you struggle to apply the methods, or that the work feels very easy yet no knowledge gaps show up. If you struggle, it is worth spending another couple of weeks practising them. If you do not struggle and also do not find any gaps, that most likely indicates the method was carried out incorrectly. Unless your knowledge really is spotless, you ought to be able to find gaps. Retrieval methods exist to surface gaps. Finding none means the practice did not work. If the method felt easy and still did not work, it may mean that you did not cover every needed order of learning or every needed kind of knowledge test.

A later recap adds three habits that should already be in use. Ensure that the gaps between revision sessions keep growing. Do at least one or two retrieval sessions in the first 10 days, then keep widening the gaps for later sessions. It is inefficient to always practise in identical conditions. The brain learns much better when each practice round has small variations. Actively look for gaps by applying the Aim step once more to the study material and to your GRINDE map. That map is the mind map refined during the Skin step against grouped, reflective, interconnected, non-verbal, directional, and emphasised checks. You will see things on that map that you do not know well, and you can deliberately ask questions to explore those gaps in a more targeted way.

- How does explaining why something matters before you name it change what you discover you cannot yet teach?
