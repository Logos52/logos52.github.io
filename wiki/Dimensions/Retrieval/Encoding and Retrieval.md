# Encoding and Retrieval

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Learning has two halves. The first half, encoding, gets information into long-term memory. The second half, retrieval, is any later use of that information. Both matter, but they pay off on different timescales. Retrieval skills take a day or two to learn and improve results quickly, so they come first. Encoding is the bigger long-term lever, raising both retention and depth, but it takes weeks to years to get good at. Early on, retrieval should fill most of study time, often 70% or more, because weak encoding lets knowledge leak away. As encoding improves the share falls, to under 20% for an experienced learner, but it never reaches zero. A simple spaced schedule of reviews after about a day, a week and a month keeps the leak in check.

## Core takeaways

- Encoding stores information, and retrieval uses it, whether by self-testing, teaching, explaining, solving problems or discussing.
- Weak encoding wastes effort, since about half of new material can be gone after a week.
- Learn retrieval first for a fast win, then build encoding for the long-term gain.
- Retrieval can take 70% or more of study time at first, falling below 20% as encoding improves.
- Some retrieval always remains, because memory always leaks.

## Why retrieval never drops to zero

Knowledge behaves like water in a bucket with a hole. Better encoding shrinks the hole, but the hole never closes completely, because some forgetting always remains. Retrieval works like scooping up what drips out and tipping it back in. It is repetitive and takes time, but it keeps performance up while encoding slowly improves.

Retrieval also does a second job. Much of learning is invisible, so a learner can misunderstand something between reading it and testing it without knowing. Testing exposes those gaps. The cycle runs from testing to finding gaps and building fluency, then to re-encoding by studying again and finally to testing again later to confirm the gap closed. Stronger encoding cuts the amount of retrieval needed, and stronger retrieval finds gaps and guides re-encoding more effectively.

## Where this week's study time goes

For most learners, retrieval should take 70% or more of study time at first, and that is normal. A weekly pattern spreads the work across sessions of different depth:

- At the start of the week, prestudy the concepts and how they relate, with a very basic mind map and no memorizing.
- After each class day, revise that day, deepen understanding, extend the map and make flashcards for details.
- At the end of the week, revise everything with an interleaved retrieval method, go back to weak spots and start memorizing details.
- In spare moments, run flashcards.
- At the end of the month, go over the month's material using a different interleaved method.

## The early cost, and why it falls

Leading with retrieval has a cost. Retrieval does not raise baseline retention, so it involves repetition, and at first the total hours spent studying can creep up a little. That is the price of keeping knowledge while the leak is fixed. Gaps also cost more the longer they go unfound, because knowledge builds in layers and a mistake made early warps every layer that rests on it. Testing soon after learning keeps that cost low.

Prestudy lowers the cost over time. When time allows only one, solid prestudy beats going back over old material, since better prestudy means less is forgotten and less review is needed later.

## The retrieval schedule

A simple version of spaced repetition reviews material after roughly a day, a week and a month. The first review comes within a day or two of learning. About 7 to 10 days later comes the second, and 3 to 4 weeks after that, the third. The timing does not need to be exact. The schedule works best as fixed blocks, such as a half-day block closing each week and a day or so at the end of each month. Heavy content calls for an extra mid-month or mid-week review. The weeks before a test call for more practice papers and challenge questions.

## What changes as encoding improves

As encoding gets better, less leaks out, so retrieval shrinks from most of study time to under a fifth for an experienced learner. The month-end review starts to feel surprisingly easy because little has been forgotten. Fluency also becomes the main target of retrieval. Fluency is how fast information comes back and how complex the uses it supports. Some facts can simply be looked up, but knowledge meant for real expertise needs high fluency, and the reason for learning something sets the level needed.

## How to practice this

1. Spend a day or two learning a good retrieval method before working on encoding.
2. Put fixed weekly and monthly review blocks into the calendar.
3. Review new material after about a day, a week and a month.
4. Test soon after learning to catch gaps while they are cheap.
5. Choose good prestudy over extra review when time is short.
