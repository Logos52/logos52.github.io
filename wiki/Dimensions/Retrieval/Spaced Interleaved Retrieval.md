# Spaced Interleaved Retrieval

> Encoding and retrieval are two halves of one job: stronger first passes mean less topping-up, but some retrieval always remains because memory decays. Spaced interleaved retrieval (SIR) uses same-day retrieval, meaning recall on the day you learn the material, then after 1 day, after 1 week, and after 1 month on a loose schedule, changing the test format each pass so gaps show while there is time to re-encode. Treat extra retrieval time as a measure of encoding debt, not a reason to quit testing.

Encoding and retrieval use different enough skills that each is worth practicing separately, leaving wider method families and unplanned recall to [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval|Retrieval]] while focusing here on return timing and session design.

## A workable spacing rhythm

A semi-regular pattern works without a perfectionist calendar. A common pattern follows same-day retrieval, after 1 day, after 1 week, and after 1 month before shifting to loose maintenance until an assessment window:

- **Same day** after learning new material.
- **~1 day**, **~1 week**, **~1 month**, then loose maintenance until an assessment window.
- About **three to four weeks before** a high-stakes test, sweep everything the assessment needs.

One sample week: learn Monday–Tuesday with same-day retrieval, on Wednesday interleave Mon–Tue (plus Wed if new), on Thursday learn with a same-day pass, on Friday–Saturday interleave the last two weeks, and at month end interleave the last four weeks. With strong encoding, most of a week’s material may survive a few hours of interleaved review, while weak encoding forces longer passes and more debt.

Expect **two to four weeks** to settle into self-scheduled SIR before judging encoding improvements. Overall study time may not fall immediately.

## When a test finds a gap

Finding gaps is the goal of testing, so start by **re-encoding with the original method**, reading with sharper attention to how the last attempt failed. If uncertainty returns, assume weak or wrong links. Widen context, tie to problems you understand, or compare at a higher level. Some items need several rounds.

Cut retrieval only when tests stop finding new gaps, not because sessions feel heavy.

## Encoding sets the ceiling

Poor first encoding multiplies gap-filling cost (~5× repetition per gap) and can trap you in relearning loops. As encoding improves, retrieval sessions shorten. **Micro-retrieval** pauses before writing notes in a lecture so you recall first and write keywords and sketches rather than transcribing. **External check** pairs self-testing with at least one comparison against a reliable source (mark scheme, vetted bank, or scope-limited tutor tool) so tests do not miss unknown unknowns.

## Choosing methods (interleaving menu)

Pick **declarative** or **procedural** emphasis, then a few methods, not the whole catalogue.

**Drop** passive rereading, repetitive execution beyond real challenge, passive practice questions (glance at answers), and wandering group chat.

**Declarative subjects** (sciences, history, law, language structure) start with lower-order tools such as cover-copy-check and simple flashcards, move through mid-order relational cards, map brain dumps, and relational questions, and finish with higher-order evaluative questions, modified whole-part-whole teaching, evaluative discussion, and extended questions. Teach immediately after first encounter for micro-retrieval.

**Procedural subjects** (code, maths execution, clinical skills, conversation) progress retrieved execution from simple to integrative to applied and edge-case challenges, while declarative cards may still support embedded facts.

**Mixed syllabi** often start from the declarative timeline (prestudy, encoding day, same-day teaching, week-one map dump with flashcards for fact holes, weeks 2–4 escalate question quality, month-end pooled test, later evaluative discussion or WPW), then add procedural retrieval as skill work grows.

Use AI for card generation or gap-checking, not for outsourcing the relationship work that encoding requires.

## Microlearning when time fragments

If days rarely open a full hour, run **Prime → Map → Explore → Dive → Consolidate** cycles: name a tight target, list resources, sketch main ideas, deep-dive one chunk, then zoom back out before the next dive. Five- to thirty-minute blocks can stack if you consolidate so spaced gaps do not erase fragile encoding. Offload searchable detail to a notes store, and schedule a longer retrieval block weekly unless work already forces recall.

Microlearning is additive, not a substitute for a deep session when you have one. The failure mode is endless shallow dives without consolidation or without the week-scale interleaves above. Then SIR’s spacing benefits never attach to the fragments.
