# Spaced Interleaved Retrieval

Encoding, meaning the first work of getting knowledge to stick, and retrieval, meaning bringing that knowledge back from memory to use it, are two sides of the same work. You cannot be both efficient and accurate without both. When encoding is stronger, you forget less and need less retrieval. Some retrieval is always needed, because memory is never sealed against decay. Encoding skill also takes time to build, and during that time retrieval is what limits how much already-learned material slips away.

The two processes influence each other. They are discussed separately here because the actions that improve retrieval are not the same as the actions used for a first pass of encoding, and keeping them separate makes the techniques easier to practise.

[[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval|Opportunistic Retrieval]] covers the difference between sessions you schedule and retrieval that happens as a natural part of work. This page is about how to space those sessions, what to do when a test finds a gap, and how retrieval and encoding limit each other.

## A loose spacing schedule

An ultra-strict calendar is not required. A semi-regular rhythm that keeps you on top of the material is enough. A common pattern is same-day retrieval, then another pass after 1 day, then after 1 week, then after 1 month, followed loosely.

One workable week looks like this. Monday and Tuesday, new material is learned and retrieved on the same day. Wednesday holds an interleaved session covering Monday and Tuesday, and Wednesday too if new material was learned that day. Thursday's new material again gets a same-day pass. Friday or Saturday, an interleaved session covers content from the last 2 weeks. At the end of the month, an interleaved session covers the last 4 weeks. About 3 weeks to 4 weeks before an assessment, retrieve everything that assessment will need.

That pattern gives a same-day pass, a mid-week pass, an end-of-week pass, and an end-of-month pass. The end-of-week pass looks back across 2 weeks, and the end-of-month pass looks back across 4 weeks. You come back to the material inside 1 day to 3 days after you first learned it, then again 1 week to 2 weeks later, then again 2 weeks to 4 weeks later. Combined with prestudy and study on the day itself, that means the same material is encoded and then re-encoded through interleaved retrieval around 6 times in a given month.

This can look like too much if encoding is still weak, because a whole month of content sounds huge to revisit. With stronger encoding, about 80 percent to 90 percent of the material is still there by the end-of-week pass, so one week's material can be revised in a few hours. Each later interleaved session reduces decay and re-encodes what remains, so the end-of-month pass for 4 weeks of material may take half a day. Prestudy and learning in batches, ahead of class, also concentrates the work, with spaced retrieval of a few hours each week.

If encoding is still low, the same schedule is harder, and the shortfall has to be met with more retrieval, because both processes are needed for performance. As encoding improves, retrieval takes less time. In the short term, a strict spaced interleaved retrieval schedule is a useful foundation.

## When a test finds a gap

Finding a gap is the point of retrieval, not a failure of it. Testing also makes the material stickier when you go back to it. The first move is to use the same method you used to learn it. Revisit the material, read it again, and think it through given the way the answer just went wrong. Attention is sharper the second time, and that alone often closes the gap.

If you still feel unsure, or the same gap shows up again, assume the earlier connections were too weak or not accurate. Look for a different way to connect the material. That can mean going a little beyond the original scope. Make it more relevant, link it to problems you already understand, or build a bigger-picture comparison. Some items take several rounds.

## What retrieval cannot do on its own

Retrieval is only as good as the encoding it starts from. Encoding is what slows the leak. Retrieval keeps topping up the bucket so you have time to patch that leak. Encoding carries the long-term change. Retrieval carries short-term performance.

If the first encoding is poor, there will be many gaps. Sessions run longer, filling those gaps takes longer, and you have to test more often to check they have closed. Each gap can take about 5 times as much repetition to fill, which piles up as learning debt. If almost all of the learning is coming from practice tests and rereading, with little real encoding, the work becomes a loop of constant relearning.

Practise spaced interleaved retrieval with a few different methods until it feels usable, including making schedules and getting used to testing yourself this way. For most people that settling-in period is about 2 weeks to 4 weeks. Then keep improving encoding and watch what that does to how much retrieval you need. Retrieval time should fall because tests find fewer gaps, not because you decided to cut it. The time you still spend on retrieval is a useful signal of how many gaps encoding is still leaving behind. Overall study time may not drop for weeks or months, and that is normal.

## Two extra habits during the work

Micro-retrieval applies retrieval within seconds or minutes of first meeting the material. In a lecture, think through what was said before you write. Delaying the notes creates a small space between hearing and recalling. Do not write until you have made sense of it, and prefer keywords, short statements, arrows, and sketches over full sentences.

Self-testing can miss gaps you do not know enough to look for. At least one retrieval method, preferably a testing method, should check your knowledge against a reliable outside source. That source might be a formal answer, a question bank, or a well-constrained tool trained on the scope of your assessment.

# Interleaving Table

The menu is large, and you do not need to read all of it. Pick whether the material you are working on is mostly declarative or mostly procedural, then choose a few methods to try. Declarative knowledge is facts, concepts, and logic, the kind of content you encode as material. Procedural knowledge is the skill of doing the work. Many subjects mix both.

## Using chat tools with these methods

AI can save time on low-effort chores, such as collecting keywords or pulling several sources into one place so you are not splitting attention across many tabs. It can write test questions for you, which saves the time of writing them by hand and can give you an outside check on what you know. It can also answer exploratory questions the way a tutor might.

Do not use it as a judge of truth on a nuanced or complex idea. These tools do not understand truth the way a careful reader hopes they do. Do not use them to skip the thinking that forms a network of knowledge, such as building a model, judging relationships, or working out why something matters. Getting a ready-made "why it matters" can drop you into a passive understanding mode that is much weaker. Until you can tell that passive mode from active work under load, avoid using AI to chunk and organise information. If you do use it that way, challenge what it gives you and look for alternatives.

The Feynman method, a mind-map brain dump, and the advanced group method for practice questions can sit across more than one order.

Cover-copy-check means covering part of your notes, reproducing it from memory, then checking. It helps short-term retention of basic information, especially labels on diagrams. Image-occlusion flashcards are a more practical version of the same idea.

Teaching an idea you have only just worked through, right after that first encounter, is a useful form of micro-retrieval. Isolated and simple relational teaching work especially well in that slot.

## How the menus combine

A mostly procedural subject can use a simple path. Start with lower-order methods and move toward higher-order ones. The skills often build on themselves, which supplies some repetition naturally, and lower-order competence is tested inside the higher-order methods.

A mostly declarative subject needs more structure.

# Microlearning System

Microlearning means learning in many separate short intervals rather than in one long sit. It works best for people whose days almost never open a block longer than about an hour. Teachers have used it with competitive athletes who train 30 hours or 40 hours a week while also studying at university. It has also served people who hold more than one job, or who only find study time late at night. The system is built to make use of gaps as short as 5 minutes.

It is not a better replacement for a long study session. If you can sit with a large stretch of material, do that with your usual methods. You can still add this system on top of those longer sits, which is how many working professionals use it.

## Five stages, cycled through the day

The work always moves through stages, and you may run more than one cycle in a day.

Prime comes first. Name exactly what you intend to learn, whether that is a bound exam topic or something your job now requires. Keep this step short. Limit the target to about as much as 1 hour or 2 hours of ordinary sitting would hold. A list of questions to answer is a strong way to do it. Keywords, objectives, outcomes, or job measures can also work. The point is a tight target for everything that follows.

Map comes next, and it is still preparation. List the resources you can draw on, notice what sits in each one, and plan how you will move through them. Study itself has not begun.

Explore is the first real pass through those resources. Pull out the main ideas that match what you primed, usually 3 main ideas or 4, plus how they relate. Because the prime was only 1 hour or 2 hours of material, this pass stays small. Sketch the logic quickly until the main ideas and their links feel clear.

Dive picks one relevant chunk and learns it more deeply, adding to whatever nonlinear notes you already use. Repeat the dive on that same point until you have fleshed it out. Do not walk down the list and dive on every item in a row.

Consolidate after every dive. The stages may sit hours apart. You might prime for 10 minutes on a train, map for 3 minutes while waiting for an elevator, explore for 20 minutes at lunch, and take a first dive 4 hours later while water boils. That spacing breaks the story and weakens encoding if you just keep diving. After each dive, step back to the outline you built in the Explore stage and look for new links. Then either restart the cycle, if the prime was off or new resources appeared, or continue from Explore or Dive. The loop is big picture, dive, big picture again.

Those bursts can be 5 minutes, 10 minutes, 15 minutes, 20 minutes, or 30 minutes and still add up. Short sessions seem to produce more learning than their length would suggest. Why is not settled. One possibility is that the time pressure sharpens concentration and encoding in a way that does not appear in a long sit.

## Offload details, then retrieve on purpose

Minor details can go into a searchable personal notes store so the short sessions stay on relationships and the main ideas. If you truly must memorize something, add it to a spaced card deck during Dive or Consolidate and keep those items few.

Because the first encoding comes in fragments, you also need a larger retrieval pass at some point, or several smaller ones, unless daily work already forces you to use what you learned. A weekend block of 4 hours or 5 hours can serve as that pass. People who apply a new language all day, or a small-business owner who uses the same ideas about 20 times a day, may not need an extra session. If a week ends and you can see that you have not retrieved enough, add one.

A tablet and stylus make the system easier to run in small gaps, though the stages themselves do not depend on a particular device.
