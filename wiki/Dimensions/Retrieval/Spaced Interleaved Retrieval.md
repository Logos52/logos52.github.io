# Spaced Interleaved Retrieval

Encoding, meaning the first work of getting knowledge to stick, and retrieval, meaning bringing that knowledge back from memory to use it, are two sides of the same work. You cannot be both efficient and accurate without both. When encoding is stronger, you forget less and need less retrieval. Some retrieval is always needed, because memory is never sealed against decay. Encoding skill also takes time to build, and during that time retrieval is what limits how much already-learned material slips away.

The two processes influence each other. They are discussed separately here because the actions that improve retrieval are not the same as the actions used for a first pass of encoding, and keeping them separate makes the techniques easier to practise.

[[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval/Opportunistic Retrieval|Opportunistic Retrieval]] covers the difference between sessions you schedule and retrieval that happens as a natural part of work. This page is about how to space those sessions, what to do when a test finds a gap, and how retrieval and encoding limit each other.

## A loose spacing schedule

An ultra-strict calendar is not required. A semi-regular rhythm that keeps you on top of the material is enough. A common pattern is same-day retrieval, then another pass after 1 day, then after 1 week, then after 1 month, followed loosely.

One workable week looks like this. Monday and Tuesday, new material is learned and retrieved on the same day. Wednesday holds an interleaved session covering Monday and Tuesday, and Wednesday too if new material was learned that day. Thursday's new material again gets a same-day pass. Friday or Saturday, an interleaved session covers content from the last 2 weeks. At the end of the month, an interleaved session covers the last 4 weeks. About 3 weeks to 4 weeks before an assessment, retrieve everything that assessment will need.

That pattern gives a same-day pass, a mid-week pass, an end-of-week pass, and an end-of-month pass. The end-of-week pass looks back across 2 weeks, and the end-of-month pass looks back across 4 weeks. You come back to the material inside 1 day to 3 days after you first learned it, then again 1 week to 2 weeks later, then again 2 weeks to 4 weeks later. Combined with prestudy and study on the day itself, that means the same material is encoded and then re-encoded through interleaved retrieval around 6 times in a given month.

This can look like too much if encoding is still weak, because a whole month of content sounds huge to revisit. With stronger encoding, about 80 percent to 90 percent of the material is still there by the end-of-week pass, so one week's material can be revised in a few hours. Each later interleaved session reduces decay and re-encodes what remains, so the end-of-month pass for 4 weeks of material may take half a day. Prestudy and learning in batches, ahead of class, also concentrates the work, with spaced retrieval of a few hours each week.

If encoding is still low, the same schedule is harder, and the shortfall has to be met with more retrieval, because both processes are needed for performance. As encoding improves, retrieval takes less time. In the short term, a strict spaced interleaved retrieval schedule is a useful foundation.

## When a test finds a gap

Finding a gap is the point of retrieval, not a failure of it. Testing also makes the material stickier when you go back to it. The first move is to use the same method you used to learn it. Revisit the material, read it again, and think it through given the way the answer just went wrong. Attention is sharper the second time, and that alone often closes the gap.

If you still feel unsure, or the same gap shows up again, assume the earlier connections were too weak or not accurate. Look for a different way to connect the material. That can mean going a little beyond the original scope. Make it more relevant, link it to problems you already understand, or build a bigger-picture comparison. Some items take several rounds.

## What retrieval cannot do on its own

Retrieval is only as good as the encoding it starts from. Encoding is what slows the leak. Retrieval keeps topping up the bucket so you have time to patch that leak. Encoding carries the long-term change. Retrieval carries short-term performance.

If the first encoding is poor, there will be many gaps. Sessions run longer, filling those gaps takes longer, and you have to test more often to check they have closed. Each gap can take about 5 times as much repetition to fill, which piles up as learning debt. If almost all of the learning is coming from practice tests and rereading, with little real encoding, the work becomes a loop of constant relearning.

Practise spaced interleaved retrieval with a few different methods until it feels usable, including making schedules and getting used to testing yourself this way. For most people that settling-in period is about 2 weeks to 4 weeks. Then keep improving encoding and watch what that does to how much retrieval you need. Retrieval time should fall because tests find fewer gaps, not because you decided to cut it. The time you still spend on retrieval is a useful signal of how many gaps encoding is still leaving behind. Overall study time may not drop for weeks or months, and that is normal.

## Two extra habits during the work

Micro-retrieval applies retrieval within seconds or minutes of first meeting the material. In a lecture, think through what was said before you write. Delaying the notes creates a small space between hearing and recalling. Do not write until you have made sense of it, and prefer keywords, short statements, arrows, and sketches over full sentences.

Self-testing can miss gaps you do not know enough to look for. At least one retrieval method, preferably a testing method, should check your knowledge against a reliable outside source. That source might be a formal answer, a question bank, or a well-constrained tool trained on the scope of your assessment.
