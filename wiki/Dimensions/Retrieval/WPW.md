# WPW

Depending on a good teacher is a poor position to be in. The skill of teaching yourself is what the whole-part-whole technique, or WPW, is for. You look at a topic as a whole, break it into parts, and then return to it as a whole.

That pass tests several things at once. It tests whether you can explain the whole clearly, which is a check on your higher-order structure and on which relationships you have treated as most important. It tests whether you can move into the parts and back to the whole smoothly, which is a check on the links between layers of the topic. It tests whether you can explain each part accurately, which is a check on detailed recall. Covering those levels is what makes the method demanding. It takes a great deal of practice to do well, and even someone already fluent in the program's other methods can take months to master it.

The work often feels slow. You become sharply aware of how you are speaking and of which point you will explain next. That slowness is the point. It forces a deep look at how the knowledge is organized, against questions such as what makes the most sense and why something matters. You may also feel anxious because gaps keep appearing. Those gaps are useful. They are often ones that a confident-but-shallow sense of understanding had been hiding.

A few habits help the pass go the way it should. Use reverse explanations, meaning you give the idea itself first and only then add its name. The ordinary order names the term and then defines it, which tends to stay isolated and fact-based. Explaining first and labelling last stays relational, feels more intuitive, and becomes more memorable. Look for how each part matters to the big picture. Do not lock yourself into the order you first learned or first taught. A good pass may follow a different flow from the layers of your GRINDE map, meaning the mind map refined during the Skin step. The checks on that map are grouped, reflective, interconnected, non-verbal, directional, and emphasised. Teach from what you can recall, not from notes in front of you. Sketch a map while you talk. Speak out loud so you cannot rush. Wait at least three days after finishing that GRINDE map before the pass. Use full recall to find gaps, then restudy those gaps until recall is clean. If the first attempts feel like struggle, keep going. The struggle is the gaps showing up.

One later stage of the teaching, described in the notes after an earlier stage that is not in the file, adds technical language. First you attach terms to the pieces you have already explained, which is called labelling. Then you use those terms to explain each piece in full detail, which is called technical elaboration. Introducing the terms first and explaining them later is the weaker order. Explaining the topic without leaning on the terms first is what tests whether the concepts and relationships are actually understood. By the end of this stage the listener should know the technical names and should understand how the details fit. That helps with questions that ask only for a definition or a recalled fact.

WPW is also an example of high-volume retrieval practice, meaning a pass that covers a topic thoroughly enough to catch mistakes and weak spots quickly, including both relationships and specific details. Another example is a brain dump, which is described as a weaker check on relationships and on conceptual understanding. High-volume retrieval belongs in spaced retrieval, meaning later sessions that pull the knowledge back out after some time has passed, and it is especially useful in the early and middle stretch of revision. One suggested rhythm puts a WPW pass at the end of the week. When the volume of new material is high, another pass sits mid-week as well. Before class, prestudy with inquiry-based learning through the Bear Hunter System, and make at least a basic mind map through the first two layers of learning, or the third layer if you can. During class you consolidate the first three layers. The same day after class you consolidate and add the fourth layer, Skin your mind maps, and create flashcards. Flashcards, if used, also fit into leftover scraps of time rather than a session of their own. About three or four weeks after that, and once more before a test if time allows, a later pass can use a brain dump and practice papers.
