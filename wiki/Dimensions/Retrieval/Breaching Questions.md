# Breaching Questions

Breaching questions are practice questions you write for yourself, each made harder than anything the exam is likely to ask. They are for the late stage of study, when most gaps are closed and results have stopped improving. Trying to answer them exposes the few gaps that normal practice misses, including ones you did not know you had.

- Late in study, the remaining gaps are few and hard to find.
- Some gaps you feel, and others sit under a confident wrong answer.
- Write the hardest question you can imagine for each weak spot.
- Searching for the answer finds gaps, even if you never reach it.
- Spend more time on important concepts, less on minor facts.
- The method needs a well-organised grasp of the topic first.

## How it works

A gap is either known or unknown. A known gap shows up as slow or shaky recall. An unknown gap is a whole angle on the topic you never considered, so you feel sure of your answer and still miss the point. A question built to be harder than the exam forces you past what you already know, which is where both kinds of gap show up.

```
known gap:    you notice slow, shaky recall
unknown gap:  you feel sure, and miss a whole angle
                  |
     a question harder than the exam
                  |
          both kinds show up
```

## How to do it

Work from your notes or concept map, or from a course's list of learning objectives if weak spots are hard to see. For each objective, recall everything you know, compare it with your notes, and write down anything that felt uncertain. Then write questions for those spots that you cannot yet answer, and work on them.

- List every weak or shaky area, however small.
- Group items that relate to each other and leave the rest single.
- For each group or item, write the hardest question you can.
- Try to answer it, going beyond the syllabus where needed.
- Key concept: spend 30 minutes to 2 hours.
- Minor fact: spend 15 to 20 minutes.

Digging for an answer usually covers neighbouring topics as well, so one question often closes several gaps. The method fits alongside past papers in the last two to three weeks before an exam.

## Where it fails

The method needs a learner who can hold many related ideas in mind at once and tolerate being stuck. Used too early, before the topic is well organised, breaching questions tend to be vague or out of reach. Feeling confident is no protection, because confidence reflects what you can recall and says nothing about what you never learned.

## Sources

- Kruger, J., & Dunning, D. (1999). Unskilled and unaware of it. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. Confidence can survive the absence of the knowledge that would reveal the miss.
- Koriat, A. Metacognitive monitoring. Confidence tracks what gets retrieved, not what was never encoded. Neither paper isolates this protocol.
