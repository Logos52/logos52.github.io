# Multipass System

The Multipass System is a way of covering a block of material, a whole unit, all five weeks of it, where every pass crosses the entire block and the early passes stay shallow on purpose. Each sweep goes a little deeper than the last, so the shape of the subject and the reasons inside it are in place before any leftover fact is touched.

The system was built to run inside a compressed window. Cramming is taking a massive block of knowledge and processing it in a short span of time, usually before an exam. Done correctly, cramming is useful. The useful version is not the one the word usually names. It keeps the same compression and changes two things: the technique the window runs on, and where the window sits in the calendar. Those two changes are the whole difference between the useful version and the usual one.

## Core takeaways

- A pass is one sweep of everything in scope, complete in coverage and shallow in depth. Three passes is the usual count, four or five when one depth needs a couple of days, and each pass belongs on its own day where the calendar allows.
- Logic and concepts go down first. Details come last and only if time remains, because details do not retain without the logic under them.
- There are two steerings. With no exam emergency, follow the questions the material raises. Under strong pressure, or for a topic with low long-term relevance, take the easiest objectives first from everywhere in the block.
- Conventional cramming lifts retention from about thirty percent to forty-five or fifty under stress. Adding hours does not close the rest of the gap; raising retention does.
- Any system that runs in a short window has three tests to pass: it lets the reader skip strategically, it reaches retention and depth sufficient for the best available outcome, and it is efficient enough to leave sleep whole.
- The least intuitive change is the most helpful one: for a test in August, finish the block in February, and spend the months between topping up.

## How most people cram

The usual version takes the heap of content and runs the familiar techniques at it, faster and for longer. Where three hours a day for a week might have done, twelve hours a day gets forced in. Progress is linear, front to back through the units, so wherever time runs out is where the material ends. Notes keep getting written after they have decayed into scribbles, because studying without notes feels unimaginable. All of it happens at the last minute.

This half-works, for real reasons. Pressure narrows attention, and narrowed attention raises retention and makes flow easier to reach. An imminent exam makes everything feel relevant. The short horizon means low retention costs little, since the material only has to survive a few days. The rushing also does some good: it forces skipping, and skipping is the right instinct. The trouble is where those gains come from and where the skips fall.

## Why more hours do not close the gap

The retention arithmetic is the main problem. Study that normally holds thirty percent might climb to forty-five or fifty under pressure. That lift comes from stress, and it is nowhere near the ninety-plus a working system aims at. Adding hours does not make up the difference. At fifty percent retention, half of every studied hour is already wasted, so closing the gap on hours alone means doubling them, and in practice tripling them, because the added hours pull efficiency and sleep down with them. At ninety percent retention, a ten percent bump in hours covers the same gap.

Speed does not make up the difference either. Most people already study at eighty or ninety percent of the top speed their technique allows. Changing the technique can double or triple that speed. Forcing the same technique faster mostly raises the error rate and the skipping.

The skips fall on the wrong things. The difficult concepts get dropped because they look expensive, and the cheap details keep being transcribed. Details with no concepts under them have nowhere to attach, so they fall out too. Push the hours up anyway and sleep goes, efficiency goes with it, retention falls further, and still more hours are needed.

Added up, conventional cramming buys slightly higher retention with many more hours, minus everything skipped, minus what the lost sleep takes back. There is no margin if it misses, because the time is too short for a second try. There is a real cost even when it lands, since the same result took more stress and effort than it needed to. It also forfeits revision, which is what turns knowledge into the ability to apply it, which is why the usual version does worst on curveball questions. And it has a ceiling: it holds only while the curriculum is easy. Students it served well for years can meet a serious course load and find it gone. At university density, a single lecture can carry a week of high-school material.

The repair keeps what compression does well and changes exactly two things: the technique the window runs on, and where the window sits in the calendar. Raising retention closes the gap; adding hours does not. Any system that wants to run a short window has three tests to pass.

## The three guidelines

1. The system lets you skip strategically. The two working questions are what will be assessed and what can be afforded to miss. Linear coverage that reaches eighty percent of the material forfeits up to a fifth of the questions, because curricula hang at least one question on nearly every objective. Strategic coverage holds nearly all of what is examinable and drops only what is least likely to be asked.
2. The system reaches retention and depth sufficient for the best possible outcome. When time is short, the filter is what translates into answering questions: the highest retention available per hour spent.
3. The system is efficient enough to leave sleep whole. The knowledge has to be accessible on the day, and access depends on sleep: the consolidation that files each day's encoding, and the next-day sharpness the exam itself runs on. In the usual version, lost sleep is the direct product of the hours strategy, which is why fixing the technique fixes the nights.

The pass structure carries the three guidelines: the skipping is in the layer order, the depth is in the deepening from pass to pass, and the efficiency is what leaves the nights whole.

## One pass is the whole block

A pass is one sweep of everything in scope: cross all of a unit at the first depth, then cross all of it again, deeper. The early sweeps are not skims. A skim samples. A first pass here is complete in coverage and shallow in depth, touching every part of the block at the logic layer before any part gets detail. A deep pass that never reaches the back of the block leaves a hole nothing later covers. Shallow complete sweeps miss different things each time, so what one drops the next catches.

Three passes is the usual count, four or five when one depth needs a couple of days. The passes belong on separate days where the calendar allows, a schedule that spaces the encoding by construction.

The scope is larger than instinct suggests: take the largest block a sweep can honestly cross. Studying biology, sweep the whole of biology at once. There is a sizing limit: a subject so large that one sweep would take weeks gets split into topics and swept topic by topic. The natural home of the system is large, dense material where the relationships carry the marks.

## Logic first, details last

The order inside the sweeps is the strategy. Logic and concepts go down first. Details come last, and only if time remains, because details do not retain without the logic under them, so the conventional order ends up doing the work on its details twice.

The time budget flips with it. Details routinely take half the study time or more while making up the minority of what a subject is. Most marks come from logic and concepts, and pure detail-recall questions tend to score low. The flip gives logic and concepts the larger share of the hours and leaves details the remainder, an allocation closer to how the knowledge itself is structured.

The layers are not assigned to the passes. They emerge: as the chunks shrink, the questions change on their own. A pass is not tidily one layer; the third usually straddles two. The progression is a tendency to follow, never a schedule to enforce. The layer model itself is [[Layers of Learning]]'s subject.

## What it leaves untouched

Multipass is not a way of studying. It decides what is being studied and when, and changes nothing else. Sessions run exactly as they already run, on [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Bear Hunter System|Bear Hunter System]] in most of this vault's cases, or on whatever encoding method is already in hand. This is [[Order Control]] raised from the session to the block.

## Inquiry-led steering

When no deadline is pressing, the sweeps steer by inquiry: follow what is currently worth asking, pass by pass. The first pass runs the biggest chunks on the basic questions, what is this, what does it mean, why does it matter, how does it fit together, and builds the framework. On the next pass the questions get harder on their own, because the framework makes sub-concepts visible that were invisible before. This is the pass where the concept layer takes shape, and nothing needs escalating by hand. A third pass chases sub-concepts and specific processes, feeding them into the map or onto cards. A fourth, if time exists, goes after genuinely fine detail.

The steering is [[Aim]]: writing what the material must answer before opening it. Of the two steerings this is the quicker one and the more pleasant to run. Its encoding is the deepest, and the skill it demands is the highest, which also makes it the steering for material that future learning has to stand on. A reader with no questioning habit cannot run it yet.

## Objectives-led steering

Under a hard deadline, or for a topic that will not matter much past the exam, the sweeps steer by the syllabus instead. Each pass takes whichever objectives currently rank easiest, as many as one session holds: given six hours, however many objectives six hours can cover. It takes them from everywhere in the block at once, so what the early passes leave behind is knowledge in scattered spikes, fragmented on purpose. Later passes join them. The spikes get worked into a structural map, the gaps met while joining become the next pass's aims, and what was hard last pass re-ranks as easy once its neighbours are known. The map-building is also the repair for what this ordering skips: the usual chunk-finding never happens up front, and joining the spikes does that chunk-finding then.

Objectives are the right list because a curriculum is legally obliged to publish them and the assessment is obliged to build on them, interpreted loosely enough that curveballs survive. The ordering has a real price: it spends encoding depth and curveball resilience to be easier under pressure. When nothing is left on the list, the run is finished, often by the fourth pass. If not, it runs again.

The routing rule between the two steerings is the system's own default. No exam emergency: inquiry-led. Strong pressure or low long-term relevance: objectives-led. Both share everything that matters: every pass covers the block, and the logic goes down first.

## How the run ends

A finished run closes with a clean sweep in either steering: one final pass, checked against the objectives, the list that keeps it off low-yield material, to find what every earlier sweep missed. The sweep itself is not optional; the follow-ups are. Facts that will not derive go to [[Rote Learning and Memorisation|Flashcards]] or a scene from [[Rote Learning and Memorisation|Method of Loci]]. If time allows, an interleaving pass sits on top. What the sweep finds feeds ordinary [[Revision]].

The system's own model of a five-day run: the first pass leaves perhaps a tenth understood, the second a quarter, the third, taken in halves, over half, and the final pass is a clean sweep against the objectives, closing around eighty percent coverage held at high retention. The usual approach manages thirty or forty percent of a topic. A share of the material chosen this way can cover nearly all of what turns out to be examinable. Those figures are the system's teaching model, not trial results.

## When the run does not finish

An unfinished run is where the ordering matters most. Reaching only the second pass still leaves logic and concepts banked across the whole block. The bet behind that position has four parts. First, what the exam will ask is unknowable either way, but whatever it asks, it will be probing understanding of the topic. Second, understanding is built mostly out of logic and concepts; the system puts the share at 80 percent. Third, the arithmetic: against a paper of a hundred questions drawn from a thousand or ten thousand askable things, memorising a few hundred wins about four in ten even assuming perfect recall of every one, while deriving answers from understood logic wins closer to seven in ten and stretches to questions nobody planned for. Fourth, some fine details stick without ever being memorised, at no extra cost. The figures are the system's own accounting of the bet, not measured results.

## Cram early

The easiest and most helpful change is the least intuitive one: finish early. If the test is in August, finish the block in February. The months between belong to topping up, to finding gaps with occasional [[Spaced Interleaved Retrieval]], which takes over once the sweeps are done, and to everything else in a life, stress-free and guilt-free. The freed time is the point. The window was never supposed to sit against the date. The usual reputation of cramming belongs to the version with neither change made: familiar techniques, run against the deadline. With both changes made, the compressed window ends months before the date and the final stretch is the lightest of the whole project.

If the bad version is ever truly forced, the three guidelines are the salvage: skip by examinability instead of difficulty, run the highest-retention technique you own, and do not give up sleep. The last-minute version costs more through stress than it returns through coverage.

## What it costs and where it fails

This is an advanced move, by the system's own account. Without order control, a questioning habit, and a working way to take notes, the passes have nothing to run. The prerequisites belong to the encoding method, not to multipass. Without them in place, the first job is to build them.

The evidence is equally plain. The layer model and the odds come from the system's own teaching material. The outside record supports spreading encoding episodes across days over one massed effort, and conceptual structure helping knowledge travel; neither is a trial of this protocol.

The felt price: every pass costs a full traverse of the block, so the first two feel like nothing is being learned. Deliberate shallowness reads as failure to anyone who needs a finished chapter for reassurance.

The quit signals are structural. A second sweep no faster than the first means the first was not shallow: sections got finished instead of the block covered. No questions available at the end of pass one means inquiry-led is not available yet, and objectives-led is the route. A clean sweep returning the same gaps means the passes are not deepening.

Working is checkable. Pass two runs faster over the same material than pass one did. Its questions are about how parts connect rather than what things are. By the clean sweep, most remaining gaps are arbitrary facts rather than mechanisms.

The whole block on every pass, shallow first, is what makes the run safe wherever it stops. Because every sweep crossed everything, an interrupted run leaves a working structure over the whole subject rather than a finished front and an untouched back. A completed run gets the same benefit: a high count of high-yield points covered, retention that keeps recall high, and application that comes out correct because the structure of how the knowledge fits together went in first.

## The outside record

What research calls cramming, massed last-minute restudy, loses to spaced practice on every delayed test that has been run. That is the usual version, not the repaired one. The repaired system has no published trial behind it; its numbers are its own model. One fact stands regardless of system: the night after a heavy day of encoding cannot be repaid later.

## How to practice this

1. Take the largest block one sweep can honestly cross, a whole unit or a whole subject. Cross all of it at the logic layer before any part gets detail. Notice whether the sweep reached the back of the block.
2. Run the next pass on a separate day. Notice whether it runs faster than the first over the same material. If it does not, the first pass finished sections instead of covering the block.
3. Before opening the material, write what it must answer. Notice whether the questions get harder on the second pass without being pushed. If no questions come after pass one, steer by the objectives instead.
4. Under a hard deadline, take the easiest objectives from everywhere in the block, as many as one session holds. Notice the gaps met while joining the spikes into a map; those are the next pass's aims.
5. Close with one clean sweep checked against the objectives. Notice whether the remaining gaps are arbitrary facts or mechanisms. Facts that will not derive go to cards or a scene.
6. Finish the block months before the test. Notice that the final stretch is the lightest of the whole project. Do not cut sleep after a heavy day of encoding; that night cannot be repaid later.

## Open questions

- How shallow is shallow enough on a first pass: where does a layer stop being a layer and become a skim?
- Where is the block-size line past which sweeping a whole subject costs more than sweeping it topic by topic?
- Does the question ladder still form for a reader with no prior structure in the domain, or does the first pass stall?
- Does objectives-led's encoding cost show up on a delayed test, or only on curveballs?
- Does material encoded in a compressed window hold on a delayed test as well as the same material encoded across a longer stretch?
- How early does "early" have to be for a given test date?
- What does "sufficient depth" settle at when the goal is not a graded exam?
