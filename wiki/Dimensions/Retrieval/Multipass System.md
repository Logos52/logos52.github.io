# Multipass System

Multipass is a study system for learning a large block of material in a few days by going through all of it several times, each time in more depth. It was built for cramming, which is a large amount of study packed into the days before an exam. It covers more of the exam, with better recall, than ordinary study does in longer hours.

## Takeaways

- Every pass covers the whole block, shallow at first.
- Logic and concepts come first, details come last.
- Most exam questions draw on logic and concepts.
- The study method inside each session stays the same.
- End with one last pass that looks for what was missed.
- Finish early, then keep it fresh by testing yourself at intervals.

## Why ordinary cramming fails

Most people cram by running their usual study technique faster and for longer in the last few days before an exam. That technique might keep about 30% of what they study, and the stress of the exam lifts that to 45 or 50%. They were already working near their top speed, so going faster means skipping things and making more mistakes. The extra hours come out of sleep, and less sleep lowers recall again.

- Rushed learners skip the hard concepts and copy easy details.
- The details then have nothing to attach to and fade.
- Covering 80% front to back can miss a fifth of questions.
- A cram should skip what is least likely to be tested.
- It should keep enough sleep to hold what was learned.

## How it works

Each pass is one trip through the entire block, for example a unit that took five weeks of classes, and a single pass leaves gaps. Gaps from different passes rarely fall in the same places, so three or four passes together cover almost everything. In a worked example, understanding sits near 10% after day one, 25% after day two, and 60% after days three and four. A fifth day of revision brings coverage to about 80%, held at about 90% recall.

```
pass 1  logic      ####                      ~10%
pass 2  concepts   ##########                ~25%
pass 3  details    ####################      ~60%
sweep   gaps       ########################  ~80%
```

- Three or four passes is normal.
- A large topic may need two days at one depth.
- Passes run on separate days.
- Details make up few marks but take most study time.
- Possible exam questions outnumber real ones ten to a hundred times.
- Reasoning from logic answers questions nobody memorised.

## Two ways to steer the passes

Something has to decide what each pass covers. In an inquiry-led run, the learner's own questions decide it. In an objectives-led run, the course's published list of learning objectives decides it. Both start with logic and concepts, and the choice between them depends on how close the exam is and how long the material needs to last.

- Inquiry-led: pass one asks what it is, why it matters, how it fits.
- Later questions get more detailed without being forced.
- Inquiry-led is fastest and keeps the most, but needs skill.
- It suits a run where the time is flexible.
- Objectives-led: each pass takes the easiest objectives that fit one session.
- That leaves scattered pieces across the whole block.
- The next pass joins the pieces into a map of relations.
- Gaps found while joining become the next questions.
- Objectives-led keeps less and handles surprise questions worse.
- Use it when the exam is close or the topic is short-lived.

## Finishing and after

The last pass is a clean sweep. It goes through the block once more and checks it against the learning objectives, since assessments are built from them, and revises whatever is missing. Facts that cannot be worked out from the logic go to flashcards or another memorisation tool. If time allows, add a session of teaching the material back to someone, or of practice that mixes topics, on top.

- The system needs good notes and a questioning habit first.
- Cram early, ideally months before the exam.
- Use the months after for occasional self-testing sessions, spaced out.
- The percentages above are teaching estimates, never measured.
