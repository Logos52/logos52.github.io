# Opportunistic Retrieval

Opportunistic retrieval is recall practice that happens because work makes a person use what they are learning. A doctor who reads about heart disease on Monday and treats a heart patient on Tuesday has practised recall without setting any time aside. Counting that use means a working learner can schedule fewer study sessions, and sometimes none.

- Any use of knowledge from memory counts as retrieval practice.
- Students mostly need planned sessions.
- Workers often get the same practice free from the job.
- Work spread over days already spaces the practice out.
- Label each work use by its depth and its kind of knowledge.
- Plan a session only for what work did not test.
- Order study to match what work will ask next.

## How it works

Retrieval practice means pulling knowledge out of memory, and each pull strengthens that memory. In school, nearly all of it has to be scheduled. At work, the job often decides when recall happens, so a developer recalls programming knowledge while writing code and a medical student recalls what was studied while diagnosing a patient. When work uses the knowledge much as it was studied, planned sessions can drop to none.

- Any gap between study and use beats studying in one block.
- A day or a week later at work is already spaced.
- Different cases at work mix topics, which trains telling them apart.
- A close match between study and job means little extra practice.
- A distant match still needs planned sessions.

## How to use it

After a piece of work that used the knowledge, look back and name what kind of use it was. Lower-order use is recalling a fact or following a known step. Higher-order use is comparing options, judging a plan or building a strategy. Knowledge also comes in two kinds, knowing facts and knowing how to do something, and a task may test only one of them.

- Ask what depth the task used: fact recall or judgment.
- Ask whether it used facts, a procedure, or both.
- Whatever was not used is a gap in practice.
- Fix one: run a short planned session on that part.
- Fix two: do the next task in a more demanding way.
- More demanding means evaluating a treatment plan or designing an approach.
- Time pressure at work often rules out fix two.

## Arranging study around work

A learner cannot choose what work brings, but can often choose the order of study. If a course teaches kidneys now and hearts in two months, and the job is in cardiology now and kidneys later, study hearts first. Daily work then supplies the recall practice. Teaching a colleague or a junior what was learned adds one more recall event, and that person learns too.

- Flashcards fit into small gaps in the working day.
- They cover plain facts without a dedicated session.
- Stop at the depth the job needs.
- An exam-driven student still needs a planned schedule.
