# Opportunistic Retrieval

Retrieval means taking information from long-term memory into working memory so you can recall it, solve problems, and answer questions. Some of that recall has to be planned, meaning you set aside time for retrieval sessions. That is the usual case in a purely academic setting. Opportunistic retrieval is the recall that happens as a natural part of the day, without extra scheduled time. For a lot of professional learning, those built-in chances are the better scenario, because they save time.

A doctor studying heart disease who then sees heart-disease patients is already doing interleaved retrieval. Study the topic on Monday, see a patient with it on Tuesday, and think back to what you learned. That is retrieval, even though it was not set up as a practice session. Software developers likewise retrieve programming knowledge as part of the work itself. You can treat those encounters as part of your spaced and interleaved practice.

After such a day, label what the session actually was. Ask whether you used the knowledge at a higher order or a lower order. Ask also whether that matched the type of knowledge involved, meaning declarative knowledge you can state or procedural knowledge you can carry out. That labeling shows the gap. If you only needed fact-level recall, and you used the knowledge purely declaratively, the declarative side may be fine. A higher-order gap is then likely, because the encounter did not ask you to use higher-order knowledge.

You then have two options. You can run a planned retrieval session aimed at the higher-order part you think you missed. Or you can try to convert the experience so it uses more higher-order knowledge. That can mean forming a different strategy, evaluating the management plan you created, or taking a more strategic, bigger-picture view of the topic. Time pressure and job duties often mean you cannot change how the task is done, and you have to move on. Either way you have still saved the time you would have scheduled just to test yourself.

If what you learn transfers closely to how you perform the job, and you use the knowledge in exactly the way you need it, opportunistic practice may be enough to keep the memory strong. You may not need any planned retrieval at all. Expertise still asks for good long-term retention and good depth. If you only need enough knowledge to solve one isolated problem or to present something to a client, and you do not need true expertise, daily recall at work may already meet the goal. The same thinking applies to an exam. You may still run planned sessions without needing perfect retrieval on every item, unless you will need real expertise in that material later.

Do not optimize past the goal. Optimization is always aimed at a goal. If you are holding yourself to a much higher standard than the goal requires, that extra optimizing is counterproductive. For a low-interest elective that only has to get you through an exam in about a month, you do not need doctoral-level higher-order expertise. Get the answers to a good-enough standard, keep memory from leaking too much, get enough retention depth, and fill the lower-order detail you need.

There is a flip side. You usually cannot control what work will force you to retrieve, but you can choose the material you prepare in advance, such as over a weekend. If you know you will have to learn a topic at some point, and you also know what work will force you to retrieve, study it in the order the work will ask for it. Do not wait for the order of a curriculum or a certificate course. A simple medical example is a cardiology department now and a renal department, working with kidneys, in two months. If a course teaches kidneys today and the heart two months later, learn the heart material now so the daily opportunities are used, and learn the kidney material later. Real life is more complicated than that, and the aim is still to match what you learn to the natural interleaving opportunities you already have. That matching makes the retrieval burden more manageable.

You can also add activities that create other orders of interleaving, such as running a teaching session for a teammate or a junior, which creates value for the team while testing your retrieval.
