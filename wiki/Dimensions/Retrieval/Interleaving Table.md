# Interleaving Table

The interleaving table is a list of recall methods, sorted by the kind of knowledge being practised and by how deep the practice goes. Interleaving means retrieving the same material from different angles and at different levels of difficulty, instead of repeating the same question. The table helps a learner pick two or three methods that fit a subject, and drop the ones that waste time.

## Core takeaways

- First decide whether the material is facts and concepts, or skills.
- Then pick methods at low, middle and high depth.
- Rereading, rewriting and repeating mastered tasks give almost nothing.
- Write or speak every answer in full, never only in your head.
- The quality of each session matters more than exact spacing.
- Mixing problem types beats blocking them in most subjects.

## How it works

Knowledge is either declarative or procedural. Declarative knowledge is facts, concepts and logic, such as history, law or grammar rules. Procedural knowledge is the ability to perform a skill, such as solving a maths problem, speaking a language or writing code. Each type is practised at low, middle and high depth: recalling single facts or steps, applying them to straightforward problems, and handling complex problems where many ideas affect each other.

| Depth | Facts and concepts | Skills |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low | Cover and recall, simple flashcards, list-style brain dump | Repeat the skill from memory, simple challenges |
| Middle | Two-idea flashcards, map-style brain dump, explain how ideas relate | Combine skills, change the numbers in a problem |
| High | Rank ideas by importance, group debate on what matters most | Full projects, unusual edge cases, add new conditions |

- Rereading, rewriting and relistening without a task: drop them.
- Repeating what you can already do easily: drop it.
- Checking an answer after only thinking it through: drop it.
- Group discussion without a goal: drop it.

## Methods worth knowing

A few methods need more explanation than the table gives. Questions that ask you to judge which relationships matter most have the strongest effect on depth, and writing them in one session and answering them in another gives two rounds of practice. With practice papers, marking your confidence as well as your answer finds more gaps, since a correct but unsure answer still points to a weak spot. When teaching, an imaginary student works better than a friend, because a friend fills in your gaps without noticing.

- Rate your confidence on every practice question.
- Treat an unsure correct answer as a gap.
- Teach out loud, since speaking forces each step to be explicit.
- For skills, move to combining skills soon after the basics work.
- Let AI write questions and gather sources, and do the linking yourself.

## A schedule for facts and concepts

The methods fit into a schedule over the first two months after learning a topic. The early sessions use broad methods to find big gaps quickly, and later ones use harder, multi-level questions. For skill-heavy subjects, the same schedule starts with fact methods and switches to skill methods once the concepts are solid.

- Before the lesson: skim for the big picture.
- Day 1: learn the topic, and explain each part as you go.
- Days 2 to 5: map-style brain dump, plus flashcards for gaps.
- Days 7 to 14: write link and ranking questions with model answers.
- One month: swap a practice exam with a friend and mark both.
- Two months: group debate on what matters most, or another exam swap.

## Evidence

Interleaving has been tested many times. A pooled analysis from 2019 of 59 studies found an average benefit of 0.42 in effect size. A classroom trial in 2020 with 787 students found interleaved maths practice scored 61% against 38% for blocked practice a month later. Learners still tend to rate blocked practice as more effective right after doing better with interleaving.

- Largest for telling apart similar things such as painting styles, about 0.67.
- Smaller for maths, about 0.34.
- For single word lists, blocked practice did better.

## Sources

- Dunlosky, J., et al. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*. Rereading: low utility.
- Rohrer, D., & Taylor, K. (2006). The effects of overlearning and distributed practise on the retention of mathematics knowledge. Extra same-difficulty problems past first success: no gain at 1 or 4 weeks.
- Kornell, N., & Bjork, R. A. (2008). Learning concepts and categories: Is spacing the “enemy of induction”? Fluency illusion: blocked practice rated as more effective immediately after a test already done better under interleaving.
- Rowland, C. A. (2014). The effect of testing versus restudy on retention. *Psychological Bulletin*. Recall outperforms recognition; testing with feedback d = 0.73 vs without d = 0.39.
- Springer, L., Stanne, M. E., & Donovan, S. S. (1999). Effects of small-group learning on undergraduates in STEM. *Review of Educational Research*. d ≈ 0.51. Structured cooperative work can raise achievement; undirected discussion underperforms a protocol.
- Skinner, C. H., McLaughlin, T. F., & Logan, P. (1997). Cover, copy, and compare. Short-term retention of basic information, mostly with school-age children.
- Bisra, K., et al. (2018). Inducing self-explanation. Prompted self-explanation g = 0.55 across 64 reports.
- Rohrer, D., Dedrick, R. F., Hartwig, M. K., & Cheung, O. S. (2020). A randomized controlled trial of interleaved mathematics practice. *Journal of Educational Psychology*. 787 students, 54 classes; interleaved problem types 61% vs blocked 38%, d = 0.83 at one month.
- Pan, S. C., & Rickard, T. C. (2018). Transfer of test-enhanced learning. Elaborated retrieval as a transfer condition; realistic practice does not automatically transfer.
- Dresler, M., et al. (2017). Mnemonic training reshapes brain networks. Six weeks of loci training roughly doubled word-list recall.
- Brunmair, M., & Richter, T. (2019). Similarity matters: A meta-analysis of interleaved learning and its moderators. *Psychological Bulletin*. 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, overall g = 0.42. Paintings ≈ 0.67, mathematics ≈ 0.34, expository text non-significant, words g = −0.39 (blocking wins).
- Carvalho, P. F., & Goldstone, R. L. The discrimination account of interleaving: the effect tracks similarity that needs discriminating.
- Fiorella, L., & Mayer, R. E. (2013, 2014). Learning by teaching. Teaching and explaining as a retrieval angle.
