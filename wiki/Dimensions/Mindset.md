# Mindset

Mindset is how a person reads and responds to difficulty: what they believe about their own ability, and how they handle stress, mistakes and setbacks. It decides whether a mistake gets used as information or avoided as a threat. Since every skill is learned by making mistakes and correcting them, mindset limits how fast every other part of learning can improve.

- Skills grow through a loop of trying, failing, reflecting and trying again.
- Mindset sets how willing you are to enter that loop.
- Everyone is fixed in some areas and growth-minded in others.
- Tying results to identity makes every setback feel like a threat.
- Mindset can be trained, and the change usually takes months.
- Short mindset interventions have small measured effects.

## How it works

Mistakes are how skill improves, in the way a new skier falls many times on the first day. A person who treats a mistake as a verdict on their ability avoids the situations that produce mistakes, and so improves slowly. A person who treats a mistake as feedback looks for those situations. Avoidance is strongest when self-worth rests on results, because then every setback threatens who the person is.

- Fixed habits: avoiding uncertain tasks and sticking to safe methods.
- Fixed habits: seeking approval and grades, comparing yourself to others.
- Fixed habits: defensiveness or disengagement after a mistake.
- A simple reframe: add "yet" to "I'm not good at this".

## Training it

Training runs in phases. First you learn to notice your own fixed habits, which are usually invisible. Then you make many small, cheap mistakes instead of one large costly one, reflect on each to find what to change, and repeat until mistakes start to feel like progress. After a setback, ask what "therefore I am..." conclusion you drew about yourself, which processes actually led to the setback, and what you will try differently next time. A practice plan turns these phases into one exercise a week.

- Week 1: break a hard task into small steps and start sooner.
- Week 2: after each attempt, ask what barrier or condition it showed.
- Week 3: shorten attempts, stop at the first mistake, reflect briefly.
- Week 4: practise a trait you believe you cannot change.

## Evidence

The link between mindset and school results is real and weak, a correlation of about 0.10 across large pooled studies. Self-compassion after a failure raised people's motivation to improve more than a boost to self-esteem did. Self-worth that depends on results is linked to fragile achievement and anxiety under pressure.
