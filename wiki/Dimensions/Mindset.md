# Mindset

Your mindset shapes how you perceive and react to situations, and that influences the choices you make and the actions you take. It filters how you interpret all your experiences. A comparison used here is wearing tinted glasses, which shape what you notice, how you read it, and what you do next.

A growth mindset is the view that your abilities, your intelligence, and even who you take yourself to be can grow and get better. It encourages you to treat challenges as chances to learn, and to read mistakes as useful feedback, and positions success as something you actively create. A fixed mindset is the belief that those traits stay as they are and cannot be changed. It leads you to define yourself by what you have already done or cannot yet do, sidestep challenges, fear failure, and hold onto certainty, leaving what else is possible unexplored. Improvement is slower because comfort zones go unchallenged and new learning experiences are not taken up. It also requires an unrealistically high bar of certainty before trying something new, even when a failed attempt would cost almost nothing. A fixed mindset can lock you into self-doubt or into endless striving. A growth mindset helps you take on challenges, treat setbacks as chances to learn, and stay rooted in the knowledge that growth keeps going.

Building a growth mindset reaches past academic or professional results. Accepting where you actually are, treating yourself with compassion, and attending to the process while leaving outcomes in the background are described as essential practices for psychological well-being.

Whether your mindset leans growth or fixed is shaped in a major way by the environment around you, especially the type of feedback and praise received during formative years. Praising children for how smart they are, such as saying they are so smart, can push them toward a fixed mindset, so they become more likely to dodge challenges so they can keep that perceived intelligence. Praising effort and strategies, such as saying they worked really hard on this, encourages a growth mindset and encourages resilience and a liking for learning. Parents' own mindsets influence their interactions. Parents who hold a fixed mindset may act in more controlling ways and put performance ahead of learning, which can weigh on how a child stays driven and meets hard tasks. Parents who hold a growth mindset more often encourage exploration and learning from mistakes. Families, cultures, or societies that put mastery and personal development ahead of performance and outside approval tend to grow a growth mindset. In highly competitive settings where failing costs a lot, growing a growth mindset can be much harder. People who hold a growth mindset typically have stronger academic performance and resilience.

When a fixed mindset becomes intertwined with identity and self-worth, it builds a brittle base where success and intelligence look like inborn, fixed traits. People then avoid challenges, fear failure, and resist feedback, because any setback threatens performance and identity together. Struggle may be perceived as proof you are not enough, which brings doubt, unease, and a hesitation to take risks. Over time this can stall personal development and shrink what feels possible, because the drive to prove yourself crowds out the wish to learn. A reframe offered here is to treat mistakes and setbacks as signs of effort and learning, not as a verdict on your worth. Each time you notice yourself thinking you are just not good at something, add "yet" and ask what you can learn from this instead.

Cultivating growth mindsets is described as one of the most difficult challenges in the research. Fixed mindsets often take a span of years to shift, even with steady effort, because a fixed mindset fights the very growth you need if you are going to build a growth mindset at all. On analysis of over 5,000 learners, mindset improvement through the training described here can show up in only 60 days. Mindset is also the dimension with the largest short-term improvement, meaning under 6 months. Improvement in mindset is tied more strongly to improved academic confidence and performance than any other dimension.

Everyone carries a mix of fixed and growth mindsets. It is completely ordinary to feel sure of yourself and open to growth in some areas, while ducking challenges or doubting yourself in others. Training involves four phases. The first is building awareness. Most people do not consciously see the beliefs that hold them back, and the work is to see invisible habits and mindset tendencies. A comparison used here is trying to ride a bike without seeing why you keep losing balance. If each fall is treated as proof that you just cannot bike, you quit. Once you see the problems clearly, you realise there are steps you can take to begin getting better. The second phase is learning how to fall. Falling is frightening unless you know a safe way to land. To make it easier to learn from mistakes, you first learn how to make many small mistakes at high speed, rather than one big mistake that hurts. Failure itself is not what is frightening. The fallout is. By changing that fallout and making faster, more frequent, and less serious mistakes, you begin to reframe how you engage with mistakes and learning. The third phase is getting up and getting better. Mistakes do not automatically produce learning. Once you fall, you have to see what to change on the next try, using reflection and analysis that identify exactly what to change. Over time you notice how quickly you improve when you stop being afraid. The fourth phase is enjoying the ride. Repeated spells of making mistakes, learning from them, and improving train the brain to treat mistakes differently, slowly replacing old fixed-mindset habits. You begin to enjoy the process, feeling excitement at the learning you know you will uncover and the growth you are able to experience.

Four sets of habits support that shift. Awareness and recognition means noticing fixed-mindset tendencies and the way they shape behaviour, seeing how mindset affects learning and actions, and reflecting on what triggers those tendencies. Process-oriented thinking means moving attention from outcomes onto the process of getting better, and setting clear, achievable steps toward growth, and setting realistic win criteria around skill growth and small gains. Emotional self-regulation means handling fear of failure and self-doubt, and learning to accept discomfort and move through it as part of growth, and building resilience in the face of challenges. Reflection and adaptation means trying new strategies while accepting you will not get them right immediately. It also means looking back at how well attempts worked so you can pull out what they teach. That is how confidence grows through incremental improvements.

## Open questions

- How does practising many small, less serious mistakes change how scary a larger challenge feels?
