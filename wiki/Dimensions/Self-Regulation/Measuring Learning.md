# Measuring Learning

Most learners measure study by how much they covered, and five lectures in two hours feels efficient. That number leaves out how much is remembered a week later, the hours spent relearning what was forgotten, and whether the knowledge can be used. A better measure is how much usable, retained knowledge each hour of study produces, checked one week later.

## Takeaways

- Content covered per hour is a poor measure of learning.
- Feeling fluent while studying is a poor guide to later recall.
- Measure retention one week later, at each depth the test needs.
- Efficiency is mastery and retention divided by total hours.
- Weak methods give less and less as hours rise.
- Work first on whatever holds the total down.

## Levels of mastery

Knowledge can be held at different depths, and tests ask for different depths. The five levels below combine two published scales of learning depth, Bloom's revised taxonomy and the SOLO taxonomy. Deeper levels come with better retention and the ability to solve harder problems. The step from level 3 to level 4 makes the largest difference, because judging which links matter forces much deeper processing than listing them.

| Level | What you can do | Typical test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Recall isolated facts and terms | Fact recall |
| 2 | Explain one concept on its own | Describe a process |
| 3 | Relate and compare concepts | Compare and contrast |
| 4 | Judge which links matter most | Complex, unfamiliar problems |
| 5 | Create new ideas and hypotheses | Research |

- Methods aimed at higher levels also produce the lower levels.
- Methods aimed at lower levels produce only lower levels.
- Start with higher-level methods, and add fact drills nearer the test.

## How to measure

The calculation turns a vague sense of progress into a number that can be tracked week to week. One week is the gap because a sensible review schedule has a review about a week after first study, and retention at one week predicts retention at the test. The number shows where the gap is, level by level. It also shows whether more hours are still buying progress.

- Find the lowest and highest levels the test will ask for.
- Test your retention of each level one week after study.
- Divide each result by the retention the test requires.
- Average those results to get total mastery.
- Add up every hour spent studying and reviewing the subject.

## A worked example

A second-year economics student is tested from level 1 up to level 4. After a few weeks of study, his one-week retention is 60% at level 1 against 80% required, 60% against 90% at level 2, 50% against 70% at level 3, and 30% against 60% at level 4. Dividing gives 75%, 67%, 71% and 50%, which average to 66%. He has studied 30 hours, so his efficiency is 66% over 30 hours.

- His weakest level is 4, at half of what the test needs.
- With weak methods, 60 hours might bring him only to 76%.
- Doubling hours for ten points means the method has hit its limit.

## Where it goes wrong

A number that becomes a target tends to get pushed up in ways that stop tracking the goal. Coverage and fluency are the easiest to push up, because they are easy to count and they feel like learning. Students pick rereading more than any other method for the same reason. Tracking one-week retention by level avoids both problems.

- Hours logged and pages covered measure input only.
- Forgetting grows with volume, so full retention at high levels is rare.
- Improve no more than two things at once.
- Relearning time counts as study time.
