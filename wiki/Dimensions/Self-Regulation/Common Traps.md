# Common Traps

The screen ahead of the week's next method names a deathly spiral, a bandwagon pick, and a misfit. Catching any of them is worth more than adding another method.

A trap is a behaviour whose harm runs far out of proportion to its size. The pattern underneath is energy spent for little return — hours of covering, copying, or repeating that leave the next week no cheaper. A good method added on top of one of these does not cancel it. The stack still produces what the trap allows, which is why screening a method before it becomes the week's work is the cheaper move.

## Deathly spirals

A deathly spiral is a method that helps this week and makes next month harder. It is entered because it answers the immediate next problem — the chapter that has to be covered, the pile that has to shrink — and in answering that problem it takes the opportunity to develop. The usual way it takes that opportunity is by consuming the time a change of method would need.

A method that feels effective now often produces worse retention later. Rereading, massed practice, and highlighting all pay in the session and charge the weeks after it. The charge has a name. **Learning debt** is the extra work a later, busier self has to do because today's method covered content without building the skill or the structure that would have made later work cheaper.

Later work gets more expensive the deeper a spiral runs, and less time remains to leave it. What actually locks is not a mood. The schedule, the notes, and the day's plan come to assume the method, so leaving costs more than the method itself.

The classic spiral is having no time to build learning skill because covering content with a method that does not build skill consumes all the time. The method holds because it answers the demand to cover content. Long-term it forces a no-win choice between failing and giving up.

**A method worth adopting gets cheaper as skill in it rises.** The screening question before depending on one is whether getting better at it saves time or adds it. The safe move is not starting a spiral. The usable form is replacement rather than abstinence: cover less, with a method that does not mortgage the future. Intentions to stop covering until skills exist fail; replacement intentions work.

## The bandwagon fallacy

A spiral often starts with how a method gets chosen in the first place. Using a technique because others do is the bandwagon fallacy: common is not the same as good. The common methods are often the ones visibly successful people use, which is what makes the pull hard to see.

Nine people use a method and one of them succeeds. Every visible success used it, so the record reads as a hundred per cent while the actual rate is nearer one in nine. Those who failed with the same method stay quiet.

Doing a thing well and knowing what made it work are separate skills. A successful practitioner's account of their own method is weak evidence for that reason — people have poor access to the causes of their own behaviour.

**Judging a method honestly needs both halves: who used it and succeeded, and who used it and failed.**

An unfamiliar method feels uncomfortable on first use, and that feeling is not evidence against it.

## Misfitting a technique

A method chosen without knowing how it works can also be a misfit: using a technique incorrectly, or on a problem it cannot solve. Enough force does drive a round peg into a triangular hole, and the peg comes out chipped and the hole wrecked.

The right technique tends to make short work of the problem. Heavy effort with no result points at the tool or the moment — and the effort that signals a misfit is effort that produces only coverage, not effort that produces retrieval, comparison, or reconstruction. A method can be hard and right. The test behind that clause lives on [[The Technique Is Only as Good as the Thinking It Produces]]: a method that looks right and is not producing the thinking it was chosen for.

The check runs in order. First: is the method being run as designed? If the execution is off, the execution is fixed and the method is re-tested. Only when execution is right and results still do not come is the tool a poor fit for the job, and then it is switched rather than pushed harder.

A superior method reaches the same result for far less effort and keeps paying as more effort goes in. An effort-hungry method caps out and cannot be pushed past its ceiling.

The same ceiling shows up as hours of copious notes never re-read, whose writer could not say afterwards what was in them — a misfit that has nothing to do with cards.

## When all three land on one tool

One ordinary tool is where the three meet. [[Flashcards]] are useful for spaced retrieval schedules and for micro-learning — study in sessions of a few minutes. Overreliance is the trap, not the tool. The cards get picked because they are everywhere, run as the whole system rather than one scheduling device, and compound into a stack too large to leave.

The mechanism is a chain. Dependence on more cards becomes learning debt paid in future repetition. Thin knowledge networks keep understanding shallow, so more repetition is used to fill the depth gaps. Less time remains for any other method. The stack grows daily and the cost of leaving rises with it. Once the stack is the system, the day's learning is defined as getting through it — and the stack is too big to get through, so both the learning and the time that would fund a different method are gone.

```
A card costs about a minute to make and about thirty seconds to review, and holds after three to five reviews — roughly three minutes each. A hundred cards is about five hours booked before anything is learned. Every card added moves the total. The estimate is one a reader re-runs with their own numbers.
```

## What this list does not cover

The three traps on that stack are not a map of the field. They are the most expensive local failures. The field already names two of them — short-term ease bought at long-term cost, and copying the visible winners — and no prevalence data supports a claim that these three account for most stalled progress. A method that feels hard is not thereby a trap. The misfit signal is effort without the thinking it was supposed to produce.

The two-question test is a habit of judgment, not a trialled decision procedure. Catching a trap is monitoring, and monitoring is a trainable layer rather than a matter of vigilance — the subject of [[Metacognition - The Control Layer|metacognition]].

Being inside one looks like this from the inside: the method is the reason there is no time to change the method, and the cost of leaving is higher this month than last. The screening question costs a minute before adopting. The audit of what is already running costs one honest session, and it is cheapest early.

Screening runs ahead of the next method, and it repeats, because a new method does not cancel a trap already running. The three checks answer three different things: whether skill in the method saves time or adds it; who used it and succeeded and who used it and failed; whether execution is right before the tool is blamed. [[Marginal Gains]] is the offensive half of the same project — small improvements stacked. Gains accumulate only where nothing is cancelling them.
