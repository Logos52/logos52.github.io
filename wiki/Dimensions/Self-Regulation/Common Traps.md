# Common Traps

Traps, in this lesson, are habits that cause outsized harm. Few techniques are powerful enough to transform learning on their own. A single trap can be harmful enough to make hard work and otherwise sound methods useless. These three traps are especially common, and watching for them is a high-yield place to start.

The first is a deathly spiral, using a method that helps in the short term and harms you later. The deeper it goes, the less time and room there is to get out, so the advice is to avoid these entirely. The extra work your later self must do to pay for today's weak methods is called learning debt. Because that later self is likely to be even busier, the debt makes overwhelm more likely as time passes. A common spiral is spending so much time covering material with a weak method that there is no time left to improve the method. Covering the material meets the immediate need. Later, when volume or difficulty rises, the missing skill leaves only failing or giving up.

The second is the bandwagon fallacy, which means using a technique because other people use it. Common is not the same as good. The pattern is hard to see because successful people often use common methods, and people who failed with the same methods talk about it less. Success with a method also does not mean the person can teach it. To tell whether a method works, you need both the people who used it and succeeded and the people who used it and did not. One illustration makes the counting problem plain. If eight people out of nine used a method and only one person succeeded, every success still used the method, which hides the eight failures.

The third is a misfitting technique, which means using a method in the wrong way or in the wrong situation. The fitting method often makes short work of the problem. If a method takes a great deal of effort and still does not produce the result, it may be the wrong tool. Two methods can reach the same result with very different effort. The one that needs less effort is the better approach. Raising effort on that method can raise the result further. Raising effort on the heavier method may barely move the result, because the method itself is the limit.

Flashcards, especially in apps, show how the three traps can arrive together. They are useful for spacing out retrieval and for very short sessions only a few minutes long. Leaning on them because they are popular can turn them into the wrong tool and into a spiral. A card system can create the need for still more cards. It creates learning debt by asking your later self to repeat more so the material stays covered. It also struggles to build connected knowledge, which leaves memory weaker and understanding thinner, so still more repetition is needed to fill gaps. Time for other methods shrinks. Each day more cards arrive, and the cost of leaving the spiral rises.

People who build learning software are often not trained as teachers. Advice that would change the product can be hard to take, because it touches how the product makes money.

## Open questions

- How does a method that helps today create work that a busier later self cannot pay?
- How does counting only the people who succeeded with a method change whether that method looks safe to copy?
