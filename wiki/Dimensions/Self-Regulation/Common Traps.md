# Common Traps

A trap in learning is a study habit whose harm outweighs the benefit of the good methods used alongside it. One trap can cancel weeks of work with good methods, so finding and removing traps usually pays more than adding a new technique. Three come up most often: a short-term fix that grows into a debt, a method copied because others use it, and a method used on a problem it cannot solve.

## Takeaways

- One trap can undo the gains from good methods.
- A fix that helps now can create extra work later.
- A popular method is not proof that the method works.
- High effort with poor results means the method needs checking.
- Some good methods feel hard, so effort alone proves nothing.
- Flashcard overuse can fall into all three traps at once.

## The spiral

A spiral starts with a method that helps this week and costs more later. The cost is extra work to make up for what the weak method missed, such as repeating material that was never understood. Later weeks are usually busier, so the debt grows while the time to pay it shrinks. The longer the spiral runs, the harder it is to get out.

- The common case is having no time to learn better methods.
  - Weak methods use up all the study time.
  - Covering the content feels urgent, so nothing changes.
- The end point is to fail or to give up.
- Avoiding the spiral is easier than escaping it.

## The bandwagon

A method is often used because many people use it. The common methods are the ones many successful people used, which makes them look proven. Successful people often do not know what caused their success, since people have poor access to the causes of their own behaviour. The people who used the same method and failed rarely talk about it.

- To judge a method, find who used it and succeeded.
- Then find who used it and failed.
- Rereading and highlighting feel effective and do little for memory.
- A 2013 review rated practice testing and spaced practice highest.

## The misfit

A misfit is a real technique used on a problem it cannot solve, or used incorrectly. The right technique makes short work of the problem it fits. When the same result takes far more effort with one method than with another, the lower-effort method is the better one, and it keeps paying as effort rises. A method that does not fit stops improving results however much effort goes in.

```
result
  ^              fits
  |            /
  |          /     ______ misfit
  |        /  ____/
  |      / __/
  |    /_/
  +-------------------------> effort
```

Effort on its own is a weak signal, since some effective methods feel hard. The test is effort compared with results over several weeks.

## Flashcard overuse

Flashcards are useful for spacing reviews over time and for study sessions a few minutes long. Relied on for everything, they fall into all three traps. They get adopted because many others use them, they get used for understanding that cards cannot build, and the daily review load grows until nothing else fits.

- Cards hold isolated facts, which do not form a network.
- Poorly connected facts fade, so more cards and reviews follow.
- New cards arrive every day, and the review pile grows.
- Less time remains for other methods, or for learning them.

With no time for other methods, the facts stay isolated and the loop runs again. Stopping a trap works better with a plan for what to do instead, since people who plan a replacement action change more than people who plan only to stop.
