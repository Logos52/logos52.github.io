## Sources

- Westman & Eden 1997, *Journal of Applied Psychology*: a two-week holiday reduced burnout; the effect was largely gone three weeks back.
- de Bloom, Geurts & Kompier 2013, *Work & Stress*: vacation well-being fades within days to a few weeks.
- Sonnentag, S. (2018). The recovery paradox: Portraying the complex interplay between job stressors, lack of recovery, and poor well-being. *Research in Organizational Behavior* 38, 169–185. High load itself blocks detachment, so the people who most need recovery are least able to take it.
- Sonnentag, S., & Fritz, C. (2007). The Recovery Experience Questionnaire. *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology* 12(3), 204–221. Four experiences: relaxation, psychological detachment, mastery, control. Confirmatory factor analysis, N=930. Failed detachment: rest filled with the unfinished week does not restore.
- Sonnentag, S., & Fritz, C. (2015). Recovery from job stress: the stressor–detachment model. Detachment as the hinge; the model behind failed detachment.
- Meijman & Mulder 1998: incomplete recovery plus continuing demands accumulates load.
- McEwen 1998: allostatic load: a nervous system that never stands down.
- Maslach & Leiter 2016: burnout as a process, not an event.
- Fritz, Sonnentag, Spector & McInroe 2010: weekend recovery is incomplete under high weekday load.
- Alhola & Polo-Kantola 2007: sleep loss hits attention and working memory.
- Newbury et al. 2021: sleep and memory.
- Hirshkowitz et al. 2015, *Sleep Health*: adults 7–9 hours.
- Singh et al. 2023, *British Journal of Sports Medicine*: umbrella review: physical activity reduces depression, anxiety, and distress.
- Schuch et al. 2016, *Journal of Psychiatric Research*: exercise as a treatment for depression.
- Basso & Suzuki 2017, *Brain Plasticity*: a single bout of exercise can move mood.
- Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton 2010, *PLOS Medicine*: social relationships predict mortality at the scale of other major risk factors.
- Okabe-Miyamoto, Folk, Lyubomirsky & Dunn 2021: in-person contact outperforms online for well-being.
- Golden et al. 2005, *American Journal of Psychiatry*: bright-light therapy for seasonal and non-seasonal depression.
- Penders et al. 2016: light therapy for non-seasonal depression, modest positive.
- Roenneberg & Merrow 2016, *Current Biology*: light as the main circadian zeitgeber.
- Goyal et al. 2014, *JAMA Internal Medicine*: 47 RCTs: modest effects on anxiety and depression at eight weeks; weak on attention, sleep, and positive mood; not better than active treatments including exercise.
- Ryan & Deci 2000: autonomous, competence-supporting activity is less draining than controlled activity of the same duration.
- Blascovich & Mendes 2000: challenge versus threat appraisal.
- Jamieson, Mendes & Nock 2013: reappraising arousal as functional improves performance.
- Gross 2015: emotion regulation; reducing the amplitude of the internal reaction.
- Bernstein et al. 2015: mindfulness as decentering.
- Maier & Seligman 2016: agency: identifying remaining choices under constraint.
- Sonnentag, S., Binnewies, C., & Mojza, E. J. (2008). "Did you have a nice evening?" *Journal of Applied Psychology*. Next-day residue of failed detachment.
- Sonnentag, S., Binnewies, C., & Mojza, E. J. (2010). Staying well and engaged when demands are high. Detachment associated with better next-day engagement, not less.
- Wendsche, J., & Lohmann-Haislah, A. (2017). A meta-analysis on antecedents and outcomes of detachment from work. Better well-being, lower exhaustion.
- Kaplan, S. (1995). The restorative benefits of nature. Attention Restoration Theory; soft fascination.
- Berman, M. G., Jonides, J., & Kaplan, S. (2008). The cognitive benefits of interacting with nature. *Psychological Science*. Nature walk improved backwards digit-span versus an urban walk; the walk was about fifty minutes.
- Ohly, H., et al. (2016). Attention Restoration Theory: a systematic review. Working-memory effects are the most consistent nature finding.
- Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement. Pre-decide; the minimum viable first action is that form.
- Hagger, M. S., et al. (2016). A multilab preregistered replication of the ego-depletion effect. 23 labs, N=2,141. The resource account failed.
- Vohs, K. D., et al. (2021). A multisite preregistered paradigmatic test of the ego-depletion effect. Registered replication; same correction.
- Wiehler, A., Branzoli, F., Adanyeguh, I., Mochel, F., & Pessiglione, M. (2022). A neuro-metabolic account of why daylong cognitive work alters the control of economic decisions. *Current Biology*. Glutamate in lateral PFC after a full day of hard cognitive work: day-scale, not an hour-long fuel gauge.
- Hardwick, R. M., Forrence, A. D., Krakauer, J. W., & Haith, A. M. (2019). Time-dependent competition between goal-directed and habitual response preparation. Habit-timing under fatigue; the replacement reason for pre-decide.
