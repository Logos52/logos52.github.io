# Self-Management

Self-management is the set of habits, routines and surroundings a person builds so that the work they mean to do gets done. It covers time, tasks, focus, planning and habits. A learner with good study techniques and no time set aside to use them gets little from the techniques, so this is usually the first thing to fix.

## Takeaways

- Build systems so that daily work needs little willpower.
- A plan that only works on a good day will fail.
- List every past barrier before choosing a fix.
- Track real time against a schedule to find the barriers.
- A stopgap is fine while the habit behind it is retrained.
- The fix that works often feels uncomfortable at first.

## How it works

Most people run on intentions, such as "I need to exercise", or on plans, such as "I will study for an hour after work". A plan fails on the day the person is tired, the traffic is bad or something else comes up, and from then on only willpower carries it. A system is a set of routines that produces the result on the worst day of the week. It is built by listing everything that made earlier plans fail and then finding the lowest-effort way around each barrier, and any plan that needs willpower or motivation to start goes back for redesign.

- Expect the plan to fail, and write down why it would.
- Test each plan against a bad day.
- Move back and forth between barriers and easier options.
- Write down what to do when tired and when not.
- Keep a nap as a stopgap while the sleep problem gets fixed.

Habits run without the intention that started them, and people with high self-control reach their goals mainly through better habits, resisting temptation no more often than other people. The idea that each decision drains a limited store of willpower failed a large replication in 2016, and cutting daily decisions is still useful, since each decision is a point where the plan can stop.

## An example

An accountant working full time planned to study for his professional exams every evening after getting home. The plan failed most days, because the drive was long, he arrived tired, and dinner and family came first. Pushing harder would have worked for a few weeks. Working through the barriers one at a time turned up other options, and each option raised a new barrier that then got its own fix.

- Study at the office after work and miss the traffic.
- Ask the family to move dinner an hour later.
- Study before work, which needs an earlier bedtime.

## Accept or address

The quickest way to find barriers is to check a schedule against what happened. Make a schedule, track the real time, compare the two, and name each gap. Each barrier then gets a decision: accept it, meaning nothing reasonable can change it, or address it, meaning change something to remove it or shrink it. Accepting is reasonable when the fix costs too much.

- Most people accept a barrier before trying to address it.
- Many accepted barriers are uncomfortable to fix, and still fixable.
- An accepted barrier can still have its effect reduced.

## A four-week practice

Energy, focus, distraction and follow-through affect each other, so it is hard to tell which one matters most. Four weeks of time tracking and small experiments show it faster than guessing. The cycle can be run again whenever things slip, and the results change as circumstances change.

- Week 1: track time and note what helped or hurt focus.
- Week 2: change one factor and review the effect every two days.
- Week 3: plan and run one ideal day.
- Week 4: cut those habits to the smallest set that works.

The habits that made the ideal day work are carried into normal days first, and the smallest set left after week four is the routine to fall back on when ill or low on energy.
