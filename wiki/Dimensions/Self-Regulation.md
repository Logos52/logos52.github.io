# Self-Regulation

Self-regulation is the ability to watch your own learning while it happens, judge whether it is working, and change it. It matters most when outside structure drops away, such as the move from school to university or into study on your own. A learner who cannot adjust falls back on whatever way of studying came naturally, and results drop when the material or the setting gets harder.

- Self-regulation runs on awareness of your own thinking.
- Mistakes are expected, and the skill is noticing them and steering.
- Most learners read difficulty as a sign that a method is failing.
- Judging methods only by exam results gives feedback too late.
- Each adjustment is an experiment: guess, try, observe, refine.
- Change at most about a fifth of a study system at once.

## How it works

The standard model is a cycle in three phases: plan before the work, monitor during it, and reflect after it. Each pass through the cycle produces a small change to try next time. Three things have to be in place for the changes to be good ones. Without them a learner either does not notice what is happening, or notices and picks the wrong fix.

- Awareness: seeing what your mind is doing during study.
- Tolerance of useful difficulty: effortful methods often feel worse and work better.
  - Getting used to them can take weeks or months.
- Theory: knowing which adjustments tend to work, and why.

School programmes that train these skills find a moderate average gain, an effect size of about 0.69. The skills are trainable, and a learner at an early level usually reflects only when something goes obviously wrong.

## Map your learning flow

The first exercise is to write down how you learn now. Follow one piece of information through every method you use, from first meeting it to the test, and write what you do, when, and why. Mark each place where you are unsure what you do or why you do it. Those places are where attention pays first, and the most useful maps are detailed and honest about what the writer does not know.

## A four-week practice

A month of small experiments turns the map into a better system. Many methods turn out to be habits left over from school, kept because they are familiar. The week 1 list holds, for each method, its expected effect, its real effect and how it feels to use. One week 2 change is a move from recognising, which is finding something familiar, to recalling, which is producing it from memory, and most people recognise a coin while few can draw one.

- Week 1: list every method you use now.
- Week 2: change the methods that are ineffective or not engaging.
  - Practise the way the knowledge will be used.
  - Link details to the bigger topic.
  - Use recall, since tests ask for recall.
  - Cut setup work, such as notes written only to be reread.
- Week 3: move time between methods, self-test every 3 to 7 days.
- Week 4: combine the best into a system at most 20% new.

The weeks build on each other, and the cycle can be run again after any big change.

## Self-regulation and self-management

This vault splits the two. Self-management builds the time, habits and surroundings outside the study session, and self-regulation steers what happens inside it. Researchers use the two terms loosely and with overlap, so the split is a coaching convenience.
