# 30-Day Challenge - Deep Processing

A four-week practice for changing what the mind does with new material at the moment it is met: comparing it to what is already known and judging which links matter, in place of reading it and holding the words. The month starts by noticing which of the two the mind is doing, then asks more of the comparing each week, so that by the end it runs without a timer and less repetition is needed later.

## Core takeaways

- There are two ways to handle a new fact or idea. One way, call it the isolated mode, remembers and understands the fact on its own, by rereading, rewriting or repeating. The other, the relating mode, compares the fact to other facts and ideas for similarities and differences, then judges which of those matter, so that the fact sits in a network of related facts. Material held in a network is retained longer and understood more fully.
- Effort tells which mode is running: relating costs mental effort, and the isolated mode feels easy and often brings on drowsiness. That difference in feeling is how the mode gets caught mid-session.
- The same three questions run all month: what does this mean, how does it connect, why does it matter. Only how much is asked of them rises, week by week.
- Week 1 changes nothing except awareness. A tally mark each time the isolated mode is noticed is all week 1 asks.
- Overwhelm in week 2 and uncertainty in week 3 are expected. Both mean the practice is working.
- Most people need more than four weeks and repeat week 4.
- Any material works: next term's content, a new topic, an old weak one, a set of encyclopaedia articles. The content is only practice material; the relating mode is what is being trained.

## The four weeks

- Week 1, notice the mode
  - A sheet of paper beside the work. A tally mark each time thinking is noticed in the isolated mode.
  - Every 30 minutes, pause and rebuild the big picture of the session so far.
  - End of each session, a short written reflection: how each mode felt for effort, engagement, memory and understanding.
  - The 30-minute rebuild is weaker than relating at first pass, and gaps remain. Week 1 produces awareness and a falling share of time in the isolated mode.
- Week 2, relate on a clock
  - Every 15 minutes, 5 minutes comparing what was just covered: similarities, differences, trends, influences.
  - Every hour, 15 minutes doing the same comparing across the whole topic so far.
  - A relation is unclear: one or two minutes of looking up, then move on.
  - Notes change only for the relations that matter most.
- Week 3, rank the relations
  - Keep the week 2 clock.
  - Each new relation gets a value out of five for its importance to the whole topic.
  - Unsure of the value: up to three minutes of reading, then assign the best value available.
  - Week 3 is usually the hardest of the four weeks. Gaps show, relations that felt obvious become uncertain, and more reading is needed to judge them.
- Week 4, run it unprompted
  - No clock. Whenever the isolated mode is noticed, compare.
  - Only relations rated four or five go into the notes.
  - The big picture is lost: build a simple overarching structure for the topic.
  - Three minutes is the cap on any exploring.

```
demand
  ^
  |  week 4  run it unprompted   notes keep 4 and 5
  |  week 3  rank each relation  value out of 5
  |  week 2  relate on a clock   15+5 min, 60+15 min
  |  week 1  notice the mode     tally per catch
  +-------------------------------------------->
     same questions: meaning, connection, importance
```

## What success looks like

- Moderate comfort with the effort and uncertainty of relating and ranking, rather than avoiding them or picking only the easy links.
- New material gets related to the big picture often, with no timer running.
- A curiosity gets explored and settled within a few minutes when the network needs it.
- Until these three hold, week 4 runs again.

## Where it gets hard

- Relating feels tiring. It costs more effort per minute, and the cost falls as it becomes habit.
- Sessions feel slow. A session runs longer at first, and fewer repeat sessions are needed later for the same amount remembered.
- The relating mode is rare at first. Normal for anyone without the habit, which is why week 1 asks only for awareness.
- Relations are hard to find. Narrow what is looked for to similar, different, trend, influence. A note on the desk asking how the last two items are similar helps.
- Too many relations in week 2. More links than can be held is expected. Week 3's ranking reduces the overwhelm, in the way that a maze is easier once the main path is marked.
- Obvious links go uncertain in week 3. The uncertainty comes from noticing variables that were ignored before, and that noticing is what strengthens the network. Full certainty is never available; decide on the best reading within the three minutes.
- A value will not settle. Explain the relation twice, once in detail and once in a sentence a stranger would follow; the one that holds decides.
- Relating does not replace learning each item on its own. Both are needed.
- The tally never falls and the effort never eases. The habit is not landing; run week 4 again. Rereading harder is the isolated mode again.

## Sources

- Craik, F. I. M., & Lockhart, R. S. (1972). Levels of processing: A framework for memory research. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*. Deeper (semantic) encoding produces better memory than shallow (orthographic or phonemic) encoding.
- Craik, F. I. M., & Tulving, E. (1975). Depth of processing and the retention of words in episodic memory. Semantic encoding outperformed case and rhyme.
- Hunt, R. R., & Einstein, G. O. (1981). Relational and item-specific information in memory. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*. Relational processing is not a replacement for item-specific encoding.
- Bjork, R. A. (1994). Memory and metamemory considerations in the training of human beings. Higher-effort encoding feels slower in the session and saves later repetition.
- Fiorella, L., & Mayer, R. E. (2013). The relative benefits of learning by teaching and teaching expectancy. Explaining at more than one level of abstraction is how a score that will not settle gets tested.
- Chi, M. T. H., de Leeuw, N., Chiu, M.-H., & LaVancher, C. (1994). Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding. Pressley, M., et al. (1987). Elaborative interrogation. The three questions are a usable stand-in: meaning, connection, importance.
