# 30-Day Challenge - Deep Processing

The Deep Processing Challenge is a four-week practice plan. In each week the reader catches the moment when something only feels familiar, and each week the demand on that catch goes up. The move practised through the month is a set of three questions: what the material means, how it connects to other ideas, and why it matters.

Deep processing, in this plan, is encoding that relates an idea to other ideas and to why it matters. It does not stop at the feeling that the idea is familiar. Deep processing is the dimension this challenge trains.

## Core takeaways

- Isolated thinking remembers and understands an item without comparing it to anything. It feels easy and often makes the reader drowsy. Connected thinking compares, then judges which comparisons matter, and it costs effort.
- The move stays the same across the four weeks and the demand on it rises. Week one notices the mode, week two relates on a timer, week three scores the links, and week four runs the move without a timer.
- Relating takes time away from covering new material. In the second week about two-fifths of desk time goes to relating. In return the material needs less repetition later and can be used more flexibly.
- Scoring each link out of five makes links that seemed obvious feel uncertain. That uncertainty is the work of the third week. It is not a sign of doing the exercise wrong.
- Every session in every week ends with a written reflection. Without it the session's practice is wasted.
- A month produces awareness of the mode and a partly formed habit. Fluency takes longer, and most people repeat the fourth week.

## How the four weeks fit together

The move stays the same in all four weeks. The demand on it rises each week: notice the mode, relate on a timer, score what the relating produced, then run the move without a timer. The weeks are graded because the processing cannot be controlled step by step at reading speed. The parts are practised separately, then combined. In a real session only the automatic version of the move works.

Every session in every week ends with a written reflection. Without the reflection the session's practice is wasted.

Any material works: next term's reading, an old weak topic, or a list built from public encyclopaedia articles. The aim of the month is the skill.

## Week 1: noticing the mode

The first week does one thing: it makes visible which mode the reader is in. Isolated thinking remembers and understands an item without comparing it to anything. It feels easy, and it often makes the reader drowsy. Connected thinking compares, then judges which comparisons matter, and it costs effort. That feeling of effort is the signal. Noticing it is what makes the catch possible.

| Questions in isolated thinking | Questions in connected thinking |
|---|---|
| What does this mean? What are they trying to say? This feels important, so it needs repeating. | How does this relate to that? How is it similar to or different from what is already known? What is an analogy for it? Where does it sit in the big picture? What job does it do alongside the others? What trend runs through them? |

The week's product is a tally. The reader makes one mark on paper beside the work for each catch, for the whole session. Every half hour the session pauses, and the pause is spent rebuilding a big-picture view of everything covered so far. The rebuild is what keeps the session from going isolated. The pause on its own does not. A written reflection closes the session.

A year's habit of isolated reading does not go away in one week. What the first week produces is awareness. Upgrading material after the fact is weaker than processing it deeply on the first pass: gaps and thin retention survive the upgrade. Over the following weeks, the share of session time spent isolated goes down. The reader can start relating an item before understanding of it is complete. Some item-specific encoding is still needed.

## Week 2: relating on a timer

With the mode visible, the second week adds a demand. The reader looks for connections of four kinds: relationships, contrasts, implications, and dependencies. After each fifteen minutes of covering, five minutes go to comparing what was just covered: similarities, differences, trends, influences. Every hour, fifteen minutes go to the same pass over the whole topic so far. Three fifteen-and-five cycles fill an hour: forty-five minutes covering and fifteen relating, plus the hourly fifteen. A seventy-five-minute block therefore gives forty-five minutes of new coverage, and about two-fifths of desk time goes to relating. Those intervals are the defaults of this plan. No trial measured them.

That is the cost of the plan: about two-fifths of desk time goes to relating, and less new material gets covered in the hour. Higher-effort encoding feels slower during the session. In return the material needs less repetition later, and what was learned can be used more flexibly.

The reader writes each connection down. An item held in a network of connections is retained more strongly and understood more deeply, and each further item is easier to place. An unclear relationship gets one or two minutes of looking up, then the session moves on. Feeling overwhelmed means the reader is finding more links than the reader can hold. That is expected in this week. Looking for relationships in general is hard. Naming the four kinds is what makes them findable. A note on the desk that reads "how are these similar?" is enough of a reminder.

## Week 3: scoring the links

The third week raises the demand from finding links to scoring them. Each new relationship gets a score out of five for how much it matters to the wider topic. Scoring is harder than finding. It also fixes the second week's overwhelm: once the links that matter most are marked, the whole set is easier to hold. When a score will not settle, the reader explains the concept at more than one level of abstraction, as if to people with different background knowledge. The point where the explanation fails is the gap the unsettled score was pointing to.

The reader may look things up for three minutes, then assigns the score anyway. Full certainty is not available, and there is no time to chase it. The aim is as much certainty as three minutes allows. Scoring makes links that seemed obvious feel uncertain. That uncertainty is the work of the week. It is not a sign of doing the exercise wrong.

This is usually the hardest week. Gaps become obvious, links that felt straightforward turn uncertain, and settling a score often means reading further than the week's own material.

One example of a third-week block:

```text
A seventy-five-minute block on how a feedback loop works.
Three fifteen-then-five cycles on the stretch just covered.
One relationship written down: negative feedback resembles a thermostat and does not resemble a snowball.
Score: four. It is the main path through the topic.
A three-minute lookup on whether the analogy holds for biological loops.
That one relationship reached the notes. The other links stayed as thoughts.
```

## Week 4: running the move without a timer

The last week has no scheduled intervals. The reader goes back to material that stayed surface-level and runs the now-fluent move on it: whenever isolated thinking starts, the reader builds links. Only links that score four or five reach the notes. Where the big picture is missing, the reader imposes a simple overall structure. Looking things up is capped at three minutes. The reader considers more links than in earlier weeks and writes fewer of them down. The reader compares how the same material was encoded before and after the re-processing. That comparison is the week's test.

Success has three parts, all at once. The reader is moderately comfortable with the effort and the uncertainty: the reader does not avoid them and does not pick only the easy links. New material gets related to the big picture often. Curiosity gets followed to clarity when clarity is needed.

## What the month leaves

After the month the reader can notice isolated thinking when it starts, and has a partly formed habit of relating. The reader does not have mastery. Most people take longer than four weeks to meet the three success criteria. The fourth week gets repeated. It is not the end of the practice. A month produces awareness. Fluency takes longer.

A month of upgrading material after the fact does not equal processing it deeply on the first pass. Processing deeply on the first pass is what the habit is for. The intervals and the lookup caps are defaults chosen for this plan. No trial produced those exact numbers. Some item-specific encoding is still needed. The plan claims that relating can begin early. It does not claim that isolated encoding is useless.

If the tally does not fall across the month and the effort does not ease, the plan is not working for the reader. The next step then is to repeat the fourth week rather than start on the next dimension. The challenge is not a harder form of re-reading.

## How to practice this

1. In the first week, keep a tally beside your work, one mark each time something only feels familiar. Notice that isolated reading feels easy and can make you drowsy, while comparing costs effort.
2. Every half hour in the first week, pause and rebuild a big-picture view of everything covered so far. Notice whether the session drifts back to isolated reading between pauses. Do not expect the tally to fall in the first week.
3. In the second week, after each fifteen minutes of covering, compare for five and write each connection down. Every hour, spend fifteen more minutes comparing across the whole topic so far. Notice whether you find more links than you can hold, which is expected.
4. In the third week, score each relationship out of five for how much it matters to the topic. Cap any lookup at three minutes, then assign the score anyway. Notice which links that seemed obvious now feel uncertain.
5. In the fourth week, drop the timer and build links whenever you catch isolated thinking starting. Write down only links that score four or five. Compare how the same material was encoded before and after you re-processed it.
6. End every session in every week with a written reflection. Across the month, notice whether the tally falls and the effort eases. If neither changes, repeat the fourth week.

## Sources

- Craik, F. I. M., & Lockhart, R. S. (1972). Levels of processing: A framework for memory research. *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*. Deeper (semantic) encoding produces better memory than shallow (orthographic or phonemic) encoding.
- Craik, F. I. M., & Tulving, E. (1975). Depth of processing and the retention of words in episodic memory. Semantic encoding outperformed case and rhyme.
- Hunt, R. R., & Einstein, G. O. (1981). Relational and item-specific information in memory. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*. Relational processing is not a replacement for item-specific encoding.
- Bjork, R. A. (1994). Memory and metamemory considerations in the training of human beings. Higher-effort encoding feels slower in the session and saves later repetition.
- Fiorella, L., & Mayer, R. E. (2013). The relative benefits of learning by teaching and teaching expectancy. Explaining at more than one level of abstraction is how a score that will not settle gets tested.
- Chi, M. T. H., de Leeuw, N., Chiu, M.-H., & LaVancher, C. (1994). Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding. Pressley, M., et al. (1987). Elaborative interrogation. The three questions are a usable stand-in: meaning, connection, importance.
