# 30-Day Challenge - Deep Processing

The Deep Processing challenge is four weeks of ordinary study sessions with one job added: each new fact gets compared with facts already known, and the learner judges which of the links between them matter. Reading a fact and holding its words is the habit the month replaces. The first week only asks the learner to notice which of the two the mind is doing, each week after asks more of the comparing, and by the end the comparing starts on its own and less repetition is needed later.

- New material can be held alone or compared with other material.
- Compared material sits in a network and lasts longer.
- Comparing takes effort, and holding words alone feels easy.
- Three questions run all month: meaning, connection, importance.
- Week 1 asks only for noticing, with a tally mark.
- Overwhelm in week 2 and doubt in week 3 are expected.
- Most people repeat week 4 at least once.

## The two modes

A new fact can be handled in an isolated mode or a relating mode. In the isolated mode the fact is remembered on its own, by rereading it, rewriting it or repeating it. In the relating mode the fact is compared with other facts for what is similar and what is different, and the learner then judges which of those links matter. The relating mode takes more effort, and that difference in effort is how a learner catches which mode is running in the middle of a session.

- Isolated mode: reread, rewrite, repeat, and it feels easy.
- Relating mode: compare, judge the links, and it feels effortful.
- Drowsiness during study is a sign of the isolated mode.
- Any material works, including next term's content or an old weak topic.

## The four weeks

The same three questions run through all four weeks: what a thing means, how it connects, and how much it matters. What changes is how much each week asks of them. Week 1 asks only for awareness of which mode is running. Weeks 2 and 3 put the comparing on a clock and then add a ranking of the links, and week 4 takes the clock away, so the comparing has to start without a prompt.

- Week 1, notice the mode
  - Keep a sheet of paper beside the work.
  - Make a tally mark each time the isolated mode is caught.
  - Every 30 minutes, rebuild the big picture of the session so far.
  - End each session with a short note on how each mode felt.
- Week 2, relate on a clock
  - Every 15 minutes, spend 5 minutes comparing what was just covered.
  - Every hour, spend 15 minutes comparing across the whole topic.
  - Look for similarities, differences, trends and influences.
  - An unclear relation gets one or two minutes of lookup.
  - Notes change only for the relations that matter most.
- Week 3, rank the relations
  - Keep the week 2 clock.
  - Score each new relation out of five for importance.
  - Unsure of a score: read for up to three minutes, then decide.
- Week 4, run it unprompted
  - No clock, so compare whenever the isolated mode is caught.
  - Only relations scored four or five go into the notes.
  - Big picture lost: build a simple structure for the topic.
  - Cap any exploring at three minutes.

## What success looks like

The month has done its work when the comparing starts without a timer and the effort no longer puts the learner off it. The learner can also sit with a ranking that stays uncertain, where before only the easy links got picked. Week 4 is repeated until the comparing starts on its own, and most people repeat it at least once.

- Moderate comfort with the effort and doubt of ranking.
- New material gets related to the big picture unprompted.
- A curiosity gets settled within a few minutes when it matters.

## Where it gets hard

Comparing costs more effort per minute than rereading, so sessions run longer at first. The cost falls as the comparing becomes a habit, and fewer repeat sessions are needed later for the same amount remembered. Week 3 is usually the hardest week, because links that felt obvious in week 2 turn uncertain once the learner notices factors that were ignored before. Full certainty is never available, so the learner decides on the best reading within three minutes.

- Relations hard to find: look only for similar, different, trend, influence.
- A desk note asking how the last two items are similar helps.
- Too many links in week 2: week 3's ranking cuts them down.
- A score will not settle: explain the relation in detail, then simply.
- Relating does not replace learning each item on its own.
- Tally never falls: rereading harder is the isolated mode again.

- Craik, F. I. M., & Tulving, E. (1975). Depth of processing and the retention of words in episodic memory. Semantic encoding outperformed case and rhyme.
- Hunt, R. R., & Einstein, G. O. (1981). Relational and item-specific information in memory. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*. Relational processing is not a replacement for item-specific encoding.
- Bjork, R. A. (1994). Memory and metamemory considerations in the training of human beings. Higher-effort encoding feels slower in the session and saves later repetition.
- Fiorella, L., & Mayer, R. E. (2013). The relative benefits of learning by teaching and teaching expectancy. Explaining at more than one level of abstraction is how a score that will not settle gets tested.
- Chi, M. T. H., de Leeuw, N., Chiu, M.-H., & LaVancher, C. (1994). Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding. Pressley, M., et al. (1987). Elaborative interrogation. The three questions are a usable stand-in: meaning, connection, importance.
