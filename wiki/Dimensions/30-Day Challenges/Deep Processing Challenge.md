# 30-Day Challenge - Deep Processing

The Deep Processing Challenge is a four-week practice for noticing how you think while you learn. It trains you to shift toward higher-order work, meaning comparing new information with other information to find similarities, differences, and trends, then judging how important those comparisons are, so facts and concepts form a network. Lower-order thinking treats information in isolation. The focus there is on remembering and understanding without comparing on purpose the material with anything else. It shows up as re-reading, re-writing, or constant repetition.

This challenge asks you to follow the weeks in order and not rush ahead. The skill being trained matters more than whether the material you practise on is perfectly relevant. If you do not have something to learn right now, you can get a head start on future content, or pick a new topic you have been wanting to learn. You can also relearn something you feel weak in, or build your own set of readings with search tools and encyclopedia articles.

Higher-order thinking has several benefits. Memory and retention of new information improve, so less repetition is required. Understanding becomes deeper and more nuanced. You can use knowledge more intuitively and in more demanding ways. The work is also more engaging and enjoyable. People often avoid it because it takes more mental effort, while lower-order thinking is easy and often makes you drowsy. This first week uses those differences in mental effort as a signal.

## Week 1: build your radar

A large part of getting better at deep processing is learning to take up higher-order thinking on purpose. The aim is to recognise when you are thinking at lower orders and switch to higher orders. That starts by building a radar, meaning becoming aware of how you are thinking.

Whenever you sit down to study or learn, place a sheet of paper beside you. Each time you catch yourself thinking at a lower order, add a tally mark. Lower-order thoughts focus on isolated remembering and understanding, such as asking what something means, asking what they are trying to say, or feeling that something is important and needs to be repeated to be remembered. Higher-order thoughts compare and judge in a connected way. They ask how this relates to another concept, how it is similar or different to what you already know, or what an analogy would be. They also ask how it fits in the big picture, what function it serves, or what the trends are. Each type of thinking feels distinctly different.

Every 30 minutes, stop and try to form a clear wide view of everything covered so far in that sitting, so the work does not stay isolated and lower-order. When the sitting ends, look back at how lower-order thinking compared with higher-order thinking. Notice how much mental effort it asked of you, how engaged you felt, and how memory and understanding seemed to fare.

Do not skip that end-of-session reflection. Reflection converts those experiences into learning. Frequent reflection helps you gather useful learning from new experiences much faster than most people do.

The pause every 30 minutes can upgrade some lower-order work after the fact, though that is still weaker than working at higher orders from the outset. Gaps in knowledge and weak spots in retention will remain. In this week the focus is on a strong sense of when you are engaging in different orders of thinking. Across the weeks, a lower proportion of your time should be spent in lower orders, and the quality of your learning should improve.

This week trains deep processing by making you more aware of your thinking processes, helping you catch less helpful types of thinking, and giving you experience at deliberately adjusting those processes.

A common misconception is that you need lower-order thinking to understand information before you can do higher-order thinking. Understanding is not a type of thinking. It is an outcome or symptom.

Higher-order thinking does ask for more mental effort, and it produces far more effective learning for the minutes you spend. With time it gets easier as those processes turn into new habits. A single sitting can run longer while you are still unused to thinking this way. It saves time later by cutting down how much later repetition and extra study sessions to reach the retention and depth you need.

If you are rarely thinking at higher orders, that is normal for people without deep processing habits. The first step is not to try to change years of habits in one week. It is to build a strong awareness of when you are engaging in lower-order thinking.

## Week 2: networking

By now you should have begun to notice how lower-order thinking feels next to higher-order thinking, and how higher-order thinking changes retention and depth of understanding. This week looks at relational thinking, a core skill inside deep processing. Relational thinking means being able to build knowledge networks. Knowledge that lives in a network is held more firmly and understood more deeply. Whether the item is a tiny technical fact or a broad concept, the more tightly you can seat it in the network, the easier learning becomes.

After every 15 minutes, take 5 minutes to look at what you have studied in those 15 minutes and compare concepts, key words, terminology, and facts with each other. Look for likenesses, differences, trends, and relationships or influences. After each hour, spend 15 minutes repeating that comparison for everything you have studied in the whole topic so far. If there are any relationships you are unsure of, you can spend one or two minutes looking them up. If you cannot reach a conclusion in that time, move on. As you do this, you may want to add notes, or recast the notes you already have, so they show some of these new insights and links. Use that time for the most important and relevant changes. When each sitting ends, look back at how linking information to other information felt, and how tying it to the big picture felt. Notice how that seemed to change retention and depth of understanding.

You may find quite a few new relationships, which can feel overwhelming. That is normal, and it is a sign that you are thinking in the right way. For now the focus is on finding links and treating each piece of information in light of the others. Over time those relationships can help reduce content overwhelm, and you may even discover strategies that make it more manageable this week.

If you are struggling to express the relationships in your notes, that is also normal, and it means you are probably doing the work correctly. Later training covers how to improve note-taking to hold more relationships. For now, do what you can and try to settle into thinking in relationships.

If it is difficult to think of relationships, that is a sign you are not used to relational thinking. Most people struggle when they hunt for relationships in a vague way. Give your mind a sharp target by looking, on purpose, for similarities, differences, trends, and influences. You can even keep a note on your desk asking how these are similar, to anchor yourself.

## Week 3: pressure

By this week you should be more comfortable exploring relationships, and more experienced at tolerating the discomfort and mental effort that exploration involves. This week adds pressure by applying a higher standard to those thought processes.

Continue as you have for the second week. Whenever you notice new relationships and connections, assign a significance score out of five points, compared with other relationships. Significance is determined by its importance for the wider topic or big picture. If the significance is unclear, give yourself three minutes to check a source, read a bit more, or dig into the topic. Try to pick the most accurate score you can in that window. Keep reflecting when each sitting ends, and this time also notice how putting a clear score on a relationship's importance changes retention and depth of understanding.

The instructions are simple, and this week may be the hardest part of the month. Gaps in what you know should stand out more clearly. Links that once felt simple start to look uncertain and get questioned. You will probably have to read and learn more than you did before so you can get clear enough to score them.

A fully certain value judgment is generally out of reach, and the time needed for that kind of clarity may not be realistic. The aim is not a perfectly certain judgment. It is to try to gain as much certainty as you can before making a judgment. This extra step drives deep processing to a higher level, and it can also raise the uncertainty you feel while you study. That happens because you are noticing more variables you used to ignore, and that thicker awareness strengthens your network and deepens understanding and memory.

A comparison used here is a maze. The maze is easier to navigate when you actively try to map the correct path. Prioritising and evaluating the significance of relationships is one strategy that helps make the work less overwhelming in the same way. It is a higher level of thinking than only identifying relationships.

## Week 4: fluidity

By this week you should have had experience with the extra uncertainty and mental effort of assigning significance to relationships. You should be reasonably experienced with thinking relationally. You should also have insights into how some kinds of mental effort affect retention and depth of understanding, and be familiar with exploring further to gain clarity. This final week combines those experiences so the processes can become a more flexible, fluid habit of thinking.

Whenever you catch yourself slipping into lower-order thinking, build networks by lining ideas up against each other and hunting for likenesses, contrasts, trends, and links. Try to write down only the relationships that feel worth at least four out of five points. Whenever it is unclear how the present material ties to the big picture, try to join the networks you already have to the wider topic. Aim for a simple structure that covers the topic as a whole. If you need extra reading to get clear, give yourself 3 minutes to look. When each sitting ends, look back at how easy or hard it was to form links, rank them, and look further.

You will have succeeded here if the uncertainty and mental effort of thinking of relationships and scoring their importance feel moderately manageable. That is different from avoiding the work, or hunting only for simple relationships that are easier to invent. You should also be able to tie new information to the big picture often, and follow curiosities and get clear when that is needed to form networks.

Most people will probably need more than four weeks to meet those marks. If this week's work still feels uncomfortable and you want to strengthen the new habits, you can run this week's challenge again as often as you like.

## Open questions

- How does becoming aware of lower-order thinking, before you try to change years of habits, change what you do in a study session?
- How does assigning a value to how important a relationship is change the uncertainty you feel, and what you remember later?
