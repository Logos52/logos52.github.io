# 30-Day Challenge - Mindset

The Mindset challenge is four weeks of practising a skill in a way that makes being wrong cheap. Each week makes a wrong attempt cost less and teach more, so the next attempt gets made rather than put off. The last week points the same practice at a trait the learner believes is fixed, such as creativity.

## Core takeaways

- A miss costs most when it is both likely and large.
- Smaller, sooner attempts lower both the chance and the size.
- A miss teaches only when one change and a new attempt follow.
- Make one change per miss, so the next miss can be read.
- Count practice in cycles of attempt, feedback and adjust.
- Wanting instructions before trying is often fear of being wrong.
- The month ends with evidence about one trait and cheaper attempts.

## The four weeks

All four weeks run one cycle: make an attempt, find what went wrong, change one thing, and attempt again. Week 1 makes each attempt small enough that a miss costs little. Week 2 turns each miss into one change, which is where a miss becomes information, and week 3 fits more of those cycles into the same hour. Week 4 points the cycle at a trait the learner has written off as fixed, such as creativity or ease with people, because such traits have usually never had structured attempts made on them.

- Week 1, lower the cost of a miss
  - Pick a skill with no known way to do it or improve.
  - Break it into steps small enough to rarely go wrong.
  - Combine a few steps and watch where the misses come.
  - Start attempts fast, since delay makes a miss feel bigger.
  - The week is won by trying, missing and trying again.
- Week 2, turn each miss into one change
  - Start with one timed attempt of about an hour.
  - Ask one question about the miss, make one change, go again.
  - Every three cycles, count the attempts before results improved.
  - Complex skills need more cycles than expected.
  - Do not quit at two cycles.
- Week 3, more cycles in the same time
  - Shorter attempts, worked as hard as before.
  - Stop at each error, diagnose for ten to fifteen minutes, repeat.
  - Fit an hour of work into forty-five minutes.
  - Some skills show a miss slowly, so shorten attempts gradually.
- Week 4, test a trait believed fixed
  - Typical targets: creativity, critical thinking, social fluency.
  - At most an hour of study, then small attempts.
  - Write daily, and end with one line on how fixed it looks.
  - Seven days give evidence, and a habit takes months.

## Reading a miss

A miss teaches something only once the learner knows what kind of miss it was. Early on, two kinds can be seen without help: a method that did not produce its result, and a procedure that was not carried out as planned. A third kind arrives when an experienced person names the miss, and hearing that corrects how the learner reads the first two. With practice, the learner's own diagnosis takes over from the experienced person.

- Outcome mismatch: a method meant to raise memory did not.
- Procedure fail: the notes stayed linear when links were planned.
- External: an experienced person points out the miss.
- Separate a barrier in the way from a condition that is missing.
- The line between barrier and missing condition sharpens with more misses.

## Where it fails

The plan fails when the attempts never happen and the month turns into a set of sayings about being wrong. It also fails when a miss gets described in kinder words while it still costs as much, because the training is in lowering the cost. Programmes that try to change mindset by messages alone show very small effects at scale, so week 4 is run as an experiment and claims no change in the person. What week 4 leaves behind is seven days of written evidence about one trait.

- A month with no attempts: run the week again.
- Kinder words about a miss, with the same cost: no training.
- Declaring the trait changed after week 4 goes past the evidence.

Any week can run again, and week 2 works as a second pass. After this month, the Deep Processing and Retrieval challenges are easier to start, because their techniques cost less to try and fail at.

## Sources

- Metcalfe, J. (2017). Learning from errors. *Annual Review of Psychology*, 68, 465–489. The learning value of an error is gated by whether the next attempt happens; a correction after the miss is what helps.
- Keith, N., & Frese, M. (2008). Effectiveness of error management training. *Journal of Applied Psychology*. Error-management training outperforms error-avoidant training.
- Kapur, M. (2008). Productive failure. *Cognition and Instruction*, 26(3). Struggling on a problem before instruction produced better transfer than being taught first. Also Kapur 2016 review of productive failure.
- Ericsson, K. A., Krampe, R. T., & Tesch-Römer, C. (1993). The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance. The unit is attempt–feedback–adjust, not hours.
- Sisk, V. F., Burgoyne, A. P., Sun, J., Butler, J. L., & Macnamara, B. N. (2018). To what extent and under what circumstances are growth mind-sets important to academic achievement? *Psychological Science*, 29(4). Mindset interventions are very small at scale. The week-4 experiment still runs; a conversion is not claimed.
