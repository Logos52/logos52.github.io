# 30-Day Challenge - Mindset

A four-week plan for getting used to being wrong while learning a skill. Each week makes a wrong attempt cheaper and more useful, so the next attempt happens instead of being put off.

## Core takeaways

- A miss costs a lot when it is both likely and large. Shrink the attempt and take it sooner, and both drop.
- A miss teaches nothing on its own. It teaches when one specific change follows it and another attempt runs.
- Make one change per miss. Two changes at once leave the next miss unreadable.
- The unit of practice is attempt, feedback, adjust, not hours. Cycle speed is the learning rate.
- Wanting instructions before trying is the fear of being wrong. Run a small attempt anyway; one run shows more than planning.
- A trait you have written off as fixed is often one you have never given structured attempts. The fourth week tests that.
- The month ends with a week of evidence about one trait and a cheaper next attempt. Personality stays as it was.

## The four weeks

- Week 1, breaking your fall: lower the cost of a miss.
  - Pick a skill where you do not know how to do it or how to get better at it.
  - Break it into steps until each step has a low chance of error, combine a few, and watch where the misses come.
  - Delay inflates the stakes. Get into attempts fast and test combinations quickly.
  - Win condition for the week: try, miss, try again. A failed week carries no penalty.
- Week 2, net to trampoline: turn each miss into one change.
  - Cycle: attempt, diagnose the miss, make one change, attempt again.
  - Starting dose: one timed attempt of about an hour, one question about the miss, one change, another attempt.
  - Every three cycles, count how many attempts it took before the result moved.
  - Complex skills take more cycles than expected. Knowing that keeps you from quitting at cycle two.
- Week 3, frequency: more cycles in the same time.
  - Shorter attempts, worked as hard as before. Stop on the error, spend ten to fifteen minutes on diagnosis, then go again.
  - Same volume in forty-five minutes instead of an hour. A quarter-hour giving feedback, half a minute speaking.
  - Some skills are slow to show a miss; a cycle that is too short reports nothing. Start conservative and shorten gradually.
- Week 4, breaking chains: aim the week 2 cycle at a trait you believe is fixed.
  - Creativity, critical thinking, social fluency are typical targets.
  - At most an hour of study, small attempts, the same cycle as weeks 1 to 3.
  - Write daily. Close the week with one line on how fixed the trait looks now.
  - Seven days do not prove the belief false. They give evidence about how the trait responds to practice. Habit takes months.

```
Week 1   shrink the attempt, take it sooner     -> cost down
Week 2   miss -> one change -> attempt          -> information up
Week 3   shorter attempts, same intensity        -> cycles per hour up
Week 4   same process on a "fixed" trait         -> evidence
```

## Reading a miss

- Three kinds of miss show up.
  - Outcome mismatch: a method meant to raise memory or depth did neither.
  - Procedure fail: you meant to link ideas, you produced linear blocks.
  - External: an experienced person names the miss. The external kind calibrates the other two.
- Early on the first two dominate. With experience, self-diagnosis takes over.
- In diagnosis, separate a barrier (something in the way) from a missing condition (something needed and absent). The difference gets clearer after several misses.

## Where it fails

- A month with no attempts is a slogan month. Run the week again.
- Reframing a miss in kinder words without lowering its cost trains nothing. Training happens in what a miss costs.
- Announcing at the end of week 4 that the trait has moved is a pep talk. The week produces evidence about one trait; the verdict waits on months of practice.
- Programmes that try to change mindset with messages show very small effects at scale. Week 4 here is an experiment, and no conversion is claimed.

Any week can run again, and week 2 works as a second pass. After the month, the Deep Processing and Retrieval challenges become testable, because their techniques now cost less to try and fail at.

## Sources

- Metcalfe, J. (2017). Learning from errors. *Annual Review of Psychology*, 68, 465–489. The learning value of an error is gated by whether the next attempt happens; a correction after the miss is what helps.
- Keith, N., & Frese, M. (2008). Effectiveness of error management training. *Journal of Applied Psychology*. Error-management training outperforms error-avoidant training.
- Kapur, M. (2008). Productive failure. *Cognition and Instruction*, 26(3). Struggling on a problem before instruction produced better transfer than being taught first. Also Kapur 2016 review of productive failure.
- Ericsson, K. A., Krampe, R. T., & Tesch-Römer, C. (1993). The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance. The unit is attempt–feedback–adjust, not hours.
- Sisk, V. F., Burgoyne, A. P., Sun, J., Butler, J. L., & Macnamara, B. N. (2018). To what extent and under what circumstances are growth mind-sets important to academic achievement? *Psychological Science*, 29(4). Mindset interventions are very small at scale. The week-4 experiment still runs; a conversion is not claimed.
