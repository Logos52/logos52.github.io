# 30-Day Challenge - Self-Regulation

The Self-Regulation Challenge is a four-week plan for finding out what each of your study methods does and changing the ones that do nothing. It is for a learner who has used the same methods for years without checking them. At the end there is a written map of the methods and a slightly changed routine built from what the map showed.

## Core takeaways

- Change nothing in the first week. Write the methods down and watch what each one does.
- Many methods survive from school days out of habit and no longer serve a purpose.
- Being able to recognise a fact when you see it is different from producing it from memory, and tests ask you to produce it.
- Change one thing at a time, then test yourself every three to seven days to see what the change did.
- A method that works usually feels harder than one that does not. Moving toward the easier method is a common mistake.
- The new routine should differ from the old one by about a fifth at most; bigger rebuilds fail.
- The month is the first pass of a loop that repeats for as long as you study.

## The four weeks

The plan trains three things: noticing your own thinking, watching which method causes which effect, and changing a method in line with your goal. Every step happens inside study sessions you would have anyway. The cost is a sheet of paper and a few minutes of notes after each session.

- Week 1, mapping: change nothing.
  - Divide a sheet into four columns: the method, the effect you expect it to have on memory and understanding, the effect you observe after using it, and how easy or hard it feels and why.
  - Add methods all week as you notice them.
  - A guess in the expected column is fine. It is the first observation, and a later one corrects it.
- Week 2, judgment.
  - From the sheet, pick the methods that do little or that leave the mind passive. Modify or replace them.
  - Four ways to modify a method: make it resemble how the knowledge will be used; connect the details to the big picture; use it to find gaps, asking for recall rather than recognition; remove set-up work that produces no learning, such as notes written only to be reread later.
  - Every few days, note what the change did to effect and engagement.
  - There is no right adjustment. Each change is a guess, tested by using it, and discomfort is the usual sign of a new habit forming.
- Week 3, refinement: keep week 2 going.
  - Vary how much time and effort each method gets, so the effect of each one shows on its own.
  - Test yourself every three to seven days: practice questions, a small project that uses the material, or a brain dump, which means writing everything you remember with nothing in front of you.
  - Common findings: a method that took a lot of time can be cut without loss; a method that got little time does more when given more; where the time goes reflects habit, not effect.
  - One week is too short for this. It runs for weeks or months.
- Week 4, systemisation.
  - Put the methods that worked, with their adjustments, into one sequence. A daily schedule helps.
  - Keep the new sequence within about 20% of the old one.
  - Run it for a week or more, with small adjustments.
  - If it does worse than the old one, the change was too big; redesign with less changed.
  - After a couple of weeks of stable running, map again on the same sheet.

```
week 1      week 2      week 3         week 4
map ------> judge ----> isolate -----> systemise
change      modify      vary time,     one sequence,
nothing     or replace  test 3-7 days  <=20% new
  ^                                        |
  +------- after two stable weeks ---------+
```

Recognition is finding something familiar when you see it. Recall is producing it with nothing in front of you. Most people recognise a coin; few can draw one with its details. Exams ask for recall, so a review that only asks whether a page makes sense is the kind of method the sheet should flag.

## Where it fails

- Changing methods in week 1 skips the map, so there is nothing to judge against.
- Rebuilding the whole routine in week 4 breaks the 20% limit. The usual result is overload and more errors.
- A long list of adjustments that never becomes one routine is worse than a slightly changed routine, because nobody runs the list.
- When the problem is tiredness, load, or a day with no room to study, the [[wiki/Dimensions/30-Day Challenges/Self-Management Challenge|Self-Management Challenge]] is the track for it. That plan handles the conditions of the day.
- After any of these, the next move is the same week again.

## Sources

- Zimmerman, B. J. (2002). Becoming a self-regulated learner: An overview. *Theory Into Practice*, 41(2), 64–70. Monitoring and control are parts of a cycle of forethought, performance, and self-reflection. The four-column sheet is one way to begin the monitoring.
- Winne, P. H., & Hadwin, A. F. (1998). Studying as self-regulated learning. In D. J. Hacker, J. Dunlosky, & A. C. Graesser (Eds.), *Metacognition in educational theory and practice*. Erlbaum. Describes studying as a COPES loop: conditions, operations, products, evaluations, standards.
- Nelson, T. O., & Narens, L. (1990). Metamemory: A theoretical framework and new findings. In G. H. Bower (Ed.), *The psychology of learning and motivation* (Vol. 26). Academic Press. Names monitoring and control as the two operations of metamemory.
- Jacoby, L. L. (1991). A process dissociation framework: Separating automatic from intentional uses of memory. *Journal of Memory and Language*, 30(5), 513–541. Shows that recognition and recall are separate processes. Finding an item familiar is not the same as producing it.
- Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning. *Psychological Science*, 17(3), 249–255. Retrieval practice works better than restudy that only asks for recognition.
- Wood, W., & Rünger, D. (2016). Psychology of habit. *Annual Review of Psychology*, 67, 289–314. Habits triggered by cues explain why leftover methods stay in use after they stop being useful.
