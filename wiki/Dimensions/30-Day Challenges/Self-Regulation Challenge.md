# 30-Day Challenge - Self-Regulation

The Self-Regulation challenge is a four-week plan for finding out what each study method a learner uses actually does, and for changing the ones that do nothing. It suits someone who has used the same methods for years without checking them. At the end there is a written map of the methods and a routine changed a little, built from what the map showed.

## Takeaways

- In week 1 write the methods down and change nothing.
- Many methods survive from school out of habit alone.
- Recognising a fact is easier than producing it.
- Change one thing, then self-test every three to seven days.
- A method that works usually feels harder than one that does not.
- Keep the new routine within about a fifth of the old one.
- The month is the first pass of a loop that keeps repeating.

## The four weeks

The plan trains three habits: noticing one's own thinking, seeing which method causes which effect, and changing a method to fit the goal. Every step happens inside study sessions that would have happened anyway, so the cost is a sheet of paper and a few minutes of notes after each session. Week 1 builds the sheet. Weeks 2 and 3 change and test the methods, and week 4 turns what worked into one routine.

- Week 1, mapping: change nothing
  - Split a sheet into four columns.
  - Columns: method, expected effect, observed effect, how hard it feels.
  - Add methods all week as they come up.
  - A guess in the expected column is fine.
  - Later notes correct the guess.
- Week 2, judgment
  - Pick the methods that do little or leave the mind passive.
  - Modify or replace each one.
  - Every few days, note what the change did.
  - Each change is a guess tested by use.
  - Discomfort is normal.
- Week 3, refinement: keep week 2 going
  - Vary the time each method gets, so its effect shows alone.
  - Self-test every three to seven days.
  - Tests: practice questions, a small project, or a brain dump.
  - This step usually runs for weeks or months.
- Week 4, systemisation
  - Put the methods that worked into one sequence.
  - Keep the new sequence within about 20% of the old one.
  - Run it a week or more, with small adjustments.
  - Worse than the old one: the change was too big.
  - After two stable weeks, map again on the same sheet.

A brain dump is writing everything remembered about a topic with nothing in front of you.

## How to change a method

A method can be changed in a few ways, and each of them moves it closer to how the knowledge will be used. The most important change is to make the method ask for recall in place of recognition. Recognition is finding something familiar when it is in front of you, and recall is producing it with nothing in front of you. Most people recognise a coin and few can draw one with its details, and exams ask for recall.

- Make it resemble how the knowledge will be used.
- Connect the details to the big picture.
- Use it to find gaps, by asking for recall.
- Drop set-up work that teaches nothing, such as notes only reread.

Two findings are common in week 3: a slow method can be cut without loss, and a neglected method does more when given more time.

## Where it fails

Most failures come from skipping a step or overdoing one. Changing methods in week 1 leaves no map to judge against later. Rebuilding the whole routine in week 4 usually brings overload and more errors, and a long list of adjustments that never becomes one routine does not get run. After any of these, the same week runs again.

- Changing methods in week 1: no baseline to compare with.
- A full rebuild in week 4: overload and more errors.
- A list of fixes that never becomes a routine.

When the problem is tiredness, load, or a day with no room to study, the [[wiki/Dimensions/30-Day Challenges/Self-Management Challenge|Self-Management Challenge]] covers it, since that plan works on the conditions of the day.

## Sources

- Zimmerman, B. J. (2002). Becoming a self-regulated learner: An overview. *Theory Into Practice*, 41(2), 64–70. Monitoring and control are parts of a cycle of forethought, performance, and self-reflection. The four-column sheet is one way to begin the monitoring.
- Winne, P. H., & Hadwin, A. F. (1998). Studying as self-regulated learning. In D. J. Hacker, J. Dunlosky, & A. C. Graesser (Eds.), *Metacognition in educational theory and practice*. Erlbaum. Describes studying as a COPES loop: conditions, operations, products, evaluations, standards.
- Nelson, T. O., & Narens, L. (1990). Metamemory: A theoretical framework and new findings. In G. H. Bower (Ed.), *The psychology of learning and motivation* (Vol. 26). Academic Press. Names monitoring and control as the two operations of metamemory.
- Jacoby, L. L. (1991). A process dissociation framework: Separating automatic from intentional uses of memory. *Journal of Memory and Language*, 30(5), 513–541. Shows that recognition and recall are separate processes. Finding an item familiar is not the same as producing it.
- Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning. *Psychological Science*, 17(3), 249–255. Retrieval practice works better than restudy that only asks for recognition.
- Wood, W., & Rünger, D. (2016). Psychology of habit. *Annual Review of Psychology*, 67, 289–314. Habits triggered by cues explain why leftover methods stay in use after they stop being useful.
