# 30-Day Challenge - Self-Regulation

The Self-Regulation Challenge is a four-week practice plan for treating study methods as tools, inventorying what each actually does, and locking only a slightly new sequence. Self-regulation here means watching what those methods do, then changing the ones that do not do it.

## Week 1 — Mapping

The first week fills a four-column sheet and changes nothing yet. Mapping, here, is that inventory. Column one lists the methods actually used. Column two records the effect each is thought to have on memory and depth. Column three records the effect observed after using it. Column four records how easy or hard it feels, and why. Methods are added all week as they show up. The observation is the sheet, not a diary of focus drift.

Uncertainty on the early rows is normal. A thoughtful estimate beats waiting for accuracy. Many methods will turn out to be leftover habit, not a chosen tool. That leftover-habit reading is what the week is for. A row that cannot yet name an observed effect still belongs on the sheet; the estimate is the starting observation.

Three pieces sit under the month: noticing thinking, watching cause and effect, and making a goal-aligned change. The noticing and monitoring skill those first two name is trained by [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation/Metacognition - The Control Layer|Metacognition - The Control Layer]]. This week only records. The change waits for week 2.

## Week 2 — Judgment

The second week works from the sheet. Methods that are ineffective or not mentally engaging — often the passive ones — are picked, then modified or replaced. Reflection happens every few days.

The sheet's dead rows are what the adjustment works on. Four prompts steer it. The first is practising how the knowledge will have to be used. The second is connecting details to the big picture. The third is finding gaps, with recall preferred over recognition. The fourth is removing set-up that does not process anything: notes written only to be reread are the usual case.

Recognition versus recall is a nod of familiarity versus being able to produce the thing. A familiar coin can be recognised and still cannot be drawn from memory. Tests usually want the drawing. Recognition-based review is the method to drop. [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval/Spaced Interleaved Retrieval|Spaced Interleaved Retrieval]] is the standing recall method that distinction points at.

There is often no right adjustment, only a hypothesis and an observation. That loop is the one [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management/Kolbs Experiential Cycle|Kolbs Experiential Cycle]] already names: what happened, how it felt and why, what rule that suggests, what changes next time. Discomfort means a new habit is being trained, not that the week is failing.

## Methods as tools

The month trains methods as tools: know what each is for, and how to use it. Maintaining awareness and making corrections without losing the thread is a skill the sheet produces, not a week's do. Conditions that break under load belong to [[wiki/Dimensions/30-Day Challenges/Self-Management Challenge|Self-Management Challenge]], which owns the pressure week. That week is a planned perfect day, not this month's capstone. This month's later weeks isolate variables and install a slightly new sequence.

## Week 3 — Refinement

The later weeks isolate those adjustments. The question now is which of them actually caused the change. Time and effort are varied across methods, so cause and effect stay readable. A test runs every three to seven days. Questions check whether an item can be produced. A small project asks the method to do a job. A brain dump shows what is left when no prompt is offered. Those three forms are this system's defaults, not a measured law.

The usual discoveries land in that isolation. Fading a long-used method often does not hurt. A small-time method was often doing more than it seemed. Time spent tracks habit, not effectiveness. A method that took a large share of the week can shrink without the test score moving; a short method that felt like a side ritual can be the one the score was leaning on.

That isolating is the lifelong skill. One week is not enough. Weeks or months of the same loop are normal. The honest limit of week 3 is that it starts the loop rather than finishing it.

## Week 4 — Systemisation

The last week designs a learning system that uses the better methods in a logical sequence. A daily schedule is allowed. The house cap is about a 20% change: a slightly new system, not a new life. That fifth is a default against overload, not a measured law. The new sequence runs for a week or more. Adjustments stay minor. Week 4 exists because an unconsolidated adjustment list becomes the next overload. The findings from weeks 2 and 3 have to sit inside a sequence that can be run on an ordinary day, or they stay a list.

If the new system fails, the change was probably too large. The next move is a redesign that puts more of the old sequence back, not a larger rebuild. After a couple of weeks of stability, mapping, then adjust, then experiment can run again. The remount uses the same four-column sheet. It does not start from a blank kit.

## What the month leaves

What the month leaves is a slightly new sequence that has been run long enough to be familiar, not a new life. A failure means the change was too large.

A week 1 that starts changing methods has skipped the sheet. A week 4 that rebuilds the whole system is the failure mode the 20% cap exists to stop. A reader whose problem is load is on the wrong track: that month is next door. Any of those is the quit signal. The next move is the week again, or the Self-Management track if the day itself is what breaks.

## Open questions

How a reader who uses only two methods fills a four-column sheet.

When leftover-habit methods should be kept because they still do one small useful job.
