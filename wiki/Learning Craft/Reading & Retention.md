# Reading & Retention

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Reading for retention splits into two stages: consuming information and digesting it. Retention depends on how much stays, not how much goes in, so the two stages have to stay in balance. Digestion depends on the type of information, sorted into five kinds called PACER: procedural, analogous, conceptual, evidence and reference. Procedural information is practiced, analogies are critiqued, and concepts are mapped into a network. Evidence is stored and later rehearsed in use, and reference facts are stored and drilled with flashcards. The common failure is reading more when there is no time to digest, which leads to heavy forgetting. The repair is to consume less and digest more.

## Core takeaways

- Learning happens only when consumption is followed by digestion.
- If there is no time to digest, stop reading or switch tasks, instead of reading faster.
- Sort what you read into the five PACER types while reading.
- Practice procedures early, critique analogies, map concepts, and store evidence and reference facts for later.
- Do not spend reading time memorizing details, because the first three types carry most of the knowledge.

## Consumption and digestion have to balance

Reading has a consumption stage, when information comes in, and a digestion stage, when it is processed into long-term memory. Most people try to improve the first stage by reading faster, speeding up lectures or bingeing audiobooks. What matters is how much stays. Consuming without digesting is like overeating, and forgetting is the result. One estimate holds that up to 90 percent of what is consumed is lost. Spending less time consuming and more time digesting raises the amount of usable knowledge.

Remembering everything is not the goal anyway. A person with perfect recall but weak reasoning would win a test of memorization and lose one of problem solving. The aim is to remember what is needed, in a form that can be used.

The type of information decides how to digest it.

## PACER: five digestion jobs

| Type | What it is | Digestion |
|---|---|---|
| Procedural | How to do something | Practice it early |
| Analogous | Related to something already known | Critique the analogy |
| Conceptual | Facts, theories, principles and how they relate | Map it |
| Evidence | Specific details that make a concept concrete | Store, then rehearse in use |
| Reference | Specific details that do not change understanding | Store, then drill |

Sorting information as it arrives is part of reading actively. Using the wrong process for a type wastes time, weakens memory and makes passive reading more likely.

## Procedural information: practice it

Procedural information explains how to execute something, like a clinical examination technique, code or a language. It is digested by practice, as soon as possible. Reading it, writing notes on it and practicing a week or two later wastes most of the effort, because much of it is forgotten by then. If there is no chance to practice now, the better move is to stop or switch to something else.

## Analogous information: critique the analogy

Analogous information connects to something already known, either from another area or from earlier in the same field. A swimmer learning how muscles contract might notice it resembles their stroke. The digestion step is critique, and it is the step most people skip. Critique asks how exactly the two things are alike and how they differ. It also asks where the analogy breaks, and whether a better or extended analogy would fit. Critiquing ties the new information straight into an existing network instead of leaving it isolated.

That network idea also explains how conceptual information should be handled.

## Knowledge is a network

An expert's knowledge is not stored as a sequence of sentences. It is a highly connected network with no fixed starting point, which lets an expert see which concepts bear on a problem and how they connect. A beginner who learned at the surface sees separate concepts with no links. Books and lectures arrive in a line, but the learner's job is to rebuild the expert's network, not to remember the order of the words.

## Conceptual information: map it

Conceptual information is the "what": facts, explanations, theories, principles, relationships and applications. Most science content is conceptual, and solving problems usually needs both procedural and conceptual knowledge. Digestion means mapping. Nonlinear, network-style notes such as mind maps force the learner to think about each concept and how it connects to the others. The map grows and gets reorganized as reading continues, and analogies found along the way can shape its structure. With no time or materials to map, the answer again is to slow down and consume less.

## Evidence: store it and rehearse it in context

Evidence is detailed, often technical information such as dates, statistics or cases that make a concept concrete. Knowing how a war started is conceptual, while the specific events, people and places are evidence. Store it the moment it is spotted, in the map, a second-brain app, flashcards or a document. Rehearse it later, at the end of a day or week. Rehearsal means using it the way it will be needed: solving problems, writing detailed answers, explaining or teaching it, or using it as support in an essay.

## Reference: store it, and recall it only when required

Reference information is fine detail that does not change understanding, such as a constant's exact value, a specific gene or a list of attributes. Store it the same way as evidence. If it has to be recalled without notes, drill it with spaced repetition flashcards for a short daily session, because it will only ever be used as direct recall. Evidence and reference look alike, and mixing them up can waste hours a week.

## The working loop

While consuming, identify each piece of information by type. Practice procedures and critique analogies right away, and add concepts to a growing map. Drop evidence and reference details into storage without stopping to memorize them. Afterward, rehearse evidence in use and review reference cards. Whenever there is no time to digest, pause consumption until there is.

## The common failure and its repair

The common failure is pushing on with reading when there is no time for the right digestion, in the hope of getting through more. Reading and rereading details to force them in is a close second. Both lead to forgetting, and they hand the future self the job of finding what was lost and reading it again. The repair is to consume less, digest each type with its own process, and keep detail work out of reading time.

## What good reading looks like, and the warning signs

Good reading is slower but leaves something usable: a map that grows, analogies that have been tested, procedures already tried, and details stored for later. The main warning sign is passive reading, reaching the bottom of a page with no memory of what it said. Rereading to memorize, piling up notes without digesting, and planning to practice later are others.

## Where this fits in the vault

A knowledge base or second brain is the storage step for evidence and reference information. Conceptual maps can live there too. The store holds the material, and the learner still does the digesting through practice, critique, mapping and rehearsal.

## What the research supports, and what it does not

Most of the method comes from one learning coach's teaching and has not been independently checked. The forgetting figure is presented as an upper estimate from some studies. The benefit of analogies for retention and understanding is described as repeatedly shown. The five-type sort and its matching processes are a practical system refined over years of personal use, not a tested model.

## Objections, cost, quit signals, and checks

The obvious objection is that the system feels forced. It is unnatural on purpose, because the brain's capacity to consume is limited and unstructured reading wastes it. The cost is slower consumption and extra time spent digesting. If there is no time pressure and depth does not matter, reading freely is fine. A quick check after any session asks whether you can explain the map you built and whether you used the procedure you read about.

## How to practice this

1. Before reading, set aside time to digest as well as to read.
2. While reading, label each piece of information with its PACER type.
3. Practice procedures right away, and critique each analogy.
4. Build and reorganize a map of the concepts as you go.
5. Store evidence and reference details without stopping to memorize.
6. Later, rehearse evidence in use and review reference flashcards.
7. If digestion falls behind, stop reading until it catches up.
