# Theme-First Text Analysis

Theme-first text analysis is a way to read and write about a text, often in a school English exam, by finding what it is trying to say before looking at how it says it. Students often list techniques first, such as repetition or rhetorical questions, and then try to attach them to a theme. That order produces a repetitive essay and misses the main point, while starting from themes produces a clear structure.

- Ask three questions in a fixed order: what, why, how.
- Themes first, then the techniques that carry them.
- The author's background explains why a theme matters.
- Technique names and effects still have to be memorised.
- Group many themes under a few umbrella words before writing.

## How it works

Picture the text as a tree, with the themes as the trunk and the techniques as the leaves. Starting from the leaves means memorising every branch they connect to, while starting from the trunk makes each technique easy to place. The three questions are always asked in the same order, because asking them out of order misses the main point.

- What: the point the author is trying to get across.
- Why: why it mattered to the author, given who they are.
- How: which techniques carry each theme.

```
     theme 1        theme 2        theme 3
     /     \           |           /     \
repetition  stats   imagery   question  authority
```

## How to find the themes

For a persuasive text, the question is what the author wants the reader to feel and why. A paid advertisement written by a store has a clear reason to present the store well. Treat every word as if the author had a strict character limit, so that each word was chosen on purpose. A strong word such as "abolished" in place of "got rid of" shows where the author is pushing.

- Sort emotionally charged words into positive and negative.
- Tag each technique with the theme it serves.
- Rhetorical questions pull the reader in and stress what follows.
- Statistics add credibility.
- Named officials add weight.

## How to write it up

Write out every theme, then group near-matches under one word each. In one advertisement a long list came down to three: positive change, customer first, and normal. The essay then states what the text wants the reader to think or feel, introduces the few grouped points, and backs each one with its techniques. Where information is missing, such as the author's background, mark the gap and move on instead of inventing an answer.

- Practise on many kinds of text before the exam.
- Drill the questions until they run without effort.
- Train essay writing as a separate skill.
