# Rules of Effective Memorization

Whether study time pays depends on the unit the learner memorises. Copying a Chinese character stroke by stroke while reciting its sound and meaning fills pages and stores almost nothing, because a stroke carries no information about the word. The units that store are the functional components. In a character, 糹 shows that the meaning has to do with thread and cloth, and 巠 shows roughly how the word sounds. Finding the right unit is encoding.

Seven rules decide whether deliberate memorisation works in any domain built on thousands of items that look arbitrary. Four of the rules build the memory trace: meaningfulness, organisation, association, and visualisation. Three of the rules keep the trace: attention, repetition, and interest.

## Core takeaways

- Material is stored in proportion to how much it means to the learner. Most of the other rules work by adding meaning to the material.
- The strongest unit for a Chinese character is the functional component, a piece that carries sound or meaning. Strokes, and pieces cut out by eye with no job, are weak units.
- Organisation, association, and visualisation each add meaning: regrouping the material, tying it to a known item, and consulting a picturable form.
- Meaningfulness, organisation, association, and visualisation decide what repetition has to work with, so they outweigh attention, repetition, and interest. This ranking is a claim, not a measured result.
- Attention decides whether encoding happens at all. Repetition, spaced out, consolidates the trace. Interest supplies the study time.
- The method transfers to any domain with a level at which items stop being arbitrary. The job is to find that level, then stop.

## Four levels of meaning in a character

Material encodes in proportion to how much it means to the learner. Nearly every other rule here works by manufacturing more of that meaning. Chinese characters show the proportion as a ladder with four rungs. Each rung up makes new items easier to learn, old ones easier to recall, and unstudied ones partly predictable.

Strokes are the weakest unit. Building 經 stroke by stroke offers no chunking and no connection to sound or meaning. The pieces are the wrong size.

Non-functional pieces are still weak. Splitting 經 into 幺 + 小 + 一 + 巛 + 工 cuts the load to five chunks, and none of those five meanings plays a role in the character. Many references stop at this level. The chunks do no work.

Functional components are strong. A functional component is a piece that does a job: it carries sound, or it carries meaning. A piece that is only easy to cut out by eye is not a functional component. In 經, 糹 carries meaning and 巠 carries sound. 輕 reuses the same sound component beside 車. Two meaningful chunks replace five chunks that do nothing.

Knowing how the jobs are done is the strongest level. Sound-component variation follows patterns: jīng and qīng share a place of articulation. Meaning components extend in patterned ways. The 金 series covers metals, then objects made of metal, then actions done with those objects. The pattern itself serves as the memory aid.

Once the components are functional, the sound and meaning of the spoken word cue the reconstruction of the written form, and an unknown item can be decomposed into a guess. Encoding and retrieval run over the same connections in opposite directions.

Copying from stroke-order diagrams while reciting pronunciation and meaning turns heavy effort into weak results. That is rote learning at a level that carries no meaning. The time is spent, and the character is not stored.

## Three ways to add meaning

Organisation. The brain retains organised information better than the same content left unorganised. Regrouping alone adds meaning. The classroom demonstration is one string of letters cut three ways. With no spaces, the letters are noise. BUS HAW OR THIS is still opaque, because the breaks are in the wrong places. Cut as the proverb the letters already spelled, the same string becomes one familiar unit, and the load drops because the pieces now mean something.

Characters come with two layers of organisation already built in: functional components inside a character, and series across characters. A 方-series and a 目-series are examples. A series is ordered by the writing system. The learner's need for a character comes from the spoken language, which follows a different order. Learning a whole series at once front-loads characters that have no spoken word to attach to. The better move is to compare each new character against the known members of its series, rather than learning the series as a block.

Association. Tying new material to known material transfers meaning across the link, and the link becomes a recall route. The right side of 嫩 would not stick until it was bound to the top of 整, a daily-use character that shares the component. After that, recall never failed. The repair works because it uses a character the learner already speaks every day, and not an invented story.

Visualisation. Picturable material encodes more easily because vision is a channel for dealing with the world. Anyone can count the doors in their own house by walking through the rooms mentally. Modern glyphs have lost their pictures. Ancient picture-like forms restore the connection. 及, 禾, 秉, and 兼 are the set. This is not a claim that modern 日 "is" a sun. It is a claim that a once-picturable form can be consulted once, at first encounter, so that the modern shape has an image attached to it.

## Why the first four rules outweigh the last three

Meaningfulness, organisation, association, and visualisation build the encoding. Attention gates whether encoding happens. Repetition consolidates what was encoded. Interest supplies the hours.

The first four carry more weight than the last three because they determine what any repetition has to work with. That ranking is a claim. It is consistent with depth-of-processing. It is not a measured result, and it is not someone else's finding presented as one. [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing|Deep Processing]] is the dimension those four rules apply to memorisation.

## Attention, repetition, and interest

Attention. Learning happens only while attention is engaged. Unattended exposure turns study time into nothing. Attention has to be checked while the work is on, because the failure where an item was never stored often starts here: a name heard at an introduction and not held.

Repetition. Repetition is necessary. On its own it is enough only for very simple material. Stacked on the encoding rules, retention climbs. Spaced repetition raises it further. [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval|Retrieval]] is where this rule opens out, and [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval/Spaced Interleaved Retrieval|Spaced Interleaved Retrieval]] is the house system it plugs into. Handwriting exercises belong in the same frame: they exercise the hand, and they are separate from the encoding work. "I wrote it fifty times" is evidence that the hand is tired. It is not evidence that the form was encoded.

Interest. Time follows interest. Making the material more interesting raises effort, and through time on task and depth of processing, raises results. Working out the reason behind a character's shape is itself engaging, and the engagement deposits meaning as a side effect. So the seventh rule, interest, feeds the first rule, meaningfulness. Attention, repetition, and interest rank below the four encoding rules and still decide outcomes. A perfectly analysed component that is never repeated, or never attended to, does not hold.

## How to apply the rules

The portable move is to find the functional level before memorising anything. Learn each item whole: form, sound, and meaning together. Anchor each item to live vocabulary, because the spoken word supplies the cues that reconstruction depends on. Build connections on purpose at encoding time; they double as recall routes. Take items in order of frequency, with one exception for families: 秉 completes 禾 and 兼, so this rare member is taken with its family rather than deferred.

The move generalises to any domain that has a level of analysis at which items stop being arbitrary. Anatomy, APIs, and terminology are examples. The job is to find that level, then stop. Whether such a level is always findable stays open. The instruction is to look for it.

Time spent working out why a form is the way it is produces the meaning the first rule requires.

## Ways the rules fail

The failure list is the ladder run in reverse. Rote learning at the meaningless level. Stopping at non-functional decomposition. Bulk study of a whole series. Expecting exact sound matches: variation is patterned, and treating it as noise breaks trust in the system. Expecting transparent meaning links: 漢 carries the water component because it named a river first, and opaque links have histories. Blaming storage when encoding failed: a name that vanished after an introduction was never encoded.

## What the evidence supports

Meaningfulness, organisation, association, visualisation, and the attention cluster are published. Depth of processing is established. The finding that an unencoded item leaves nothing to recall is established. Organisation aids memory. Dual coding is established. Spacing is established. Encoding specificity is established. Functional-component analysis is standard character pedagogy. The four-over-three ranking is a claim and has not been measured.

## Costs, limits, and checks

The case against. Already-meaningful material may not need the ladder; this is an open question. The character proof is one domain instance, and the functional level in another domain may look different. Treating the ranking as a measured result is a misuse. Studying the ancient forms as a paleography course, beyond the one look at first encounter, is a misuse.

The price is the time to find the functional level before any repetition starts. The benefit is that later items get cheaper and unstudied items become partly guessable.

Quit signals: still copying strokes after the components are known; learning a whole series with no spoken word to attach to it; "I wrote it fifty times" offered as evidence of encoding.

Checkable results: an unknown item can be decomposed into a guess; a spoken word cues the written form; a split into chunks that do nothing has been replaced by a split into chunks with jobs.

## How to practice this

1. Split the character into the pieces that carry sound or meaning, as 糹 and 巠 do in 經. Look once at its ancient picture-like form. Notice that two working pieces replace five with no job, and that the shape now has an image.
2. Learn the character whole: its form, sound, and meaning together, tied to a spoken word you use. Notice that the spoken word now cues the written form.
3. When a piece will not stick, bind it to a daily-use character that shares the piece. Notice that recall now runs through the character you already knew.
4. When a new character shares a component with characters you know, compare it against those known members. Do not learn the whole series as a block. Notice whether its sound and meaning follow the pattern of the series.
5. Check that your attention is on the work while you study, then put the character into spaced repetition. Notice that writing a form fifty times tires the hand and does not by itself store the form.
6. Later, split a character you have not studied into its components and guess its sound and meaning. Notice whether the guess is possible. If it is not, or if you are still copying strokes, go back to step 1.
