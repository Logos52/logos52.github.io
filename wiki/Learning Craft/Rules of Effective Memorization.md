# Rules of Effective Memorization

Four memory rules matter whenever you need to learn a large system of information and still be able to use it for real tasks. They were taught as the opening of a course on characters, and the examples below stay with that writing system. The same four rules keep returning. They are meaningfulness, organization, association, and visualization. Used together, they raise how much meaning the thing has for you, which is what makes it stay.

## Meaningfulness

A thing you are trying to memorize sticks better when it means more to you. The more you understand, the easier the work becomes. If only one rule survives, it should be this one.

Copying a character stroke by stroke, saying the sound and the meaning out loud, trains you to see a pile of strokes. Strokes generally do not carry meaning, so that view makes memory harder. Seeing the character as components is already better, because there are usually fewer components than strokes. Many resources still split characters into pieces that do no job inside that character. Functional components do a job, because they express sound or meaning. If you understand that, you can use the sound and meaning of the word you want to write to recall the form. You can also take a character apart while reading to recover a pronunciation or a meaning you have already learned, or to make a better guess about one you have not.

The strongest level is understanding how those functional components do their work. That includes how sound components express sound, including common variations, how meaning components express meaning, including common variations, and how sound and meaning connect across characters. For a standalone piece, or a character that is only that one piece, that means knowing its form, its meaning, and its sound.

## Organization

Brains hold organized material more readily than loose material. A string of letters that does not form words is hard to keep. The same letters with spaces that create real words become easier. Organizing those words into a meaningful sentence is easier still. Organization and meaningfulness travel together. You can organize into sequences or into categories.

Characters already offer two kinds of organization. Inside a character, notice the functional components, and across characters, notice how they relate as a system. A sound series groups characters that share one sound component. Their pronunciations are often not exactly the same. Meaning series group characters that share the same meaning component, and those meanings are not always identical to the component itself. The eye component can sit in characters about looking, seeing, appearance, sleep, and the eyes. A component for metal or gold can sit in characters about money, silver, iron, a bell, a clock, or melting. Not every meaning link is obvious in modern speech. One water component appears in a name that began as a river, then the people beside that river, then a dynasty.

It is usually a poor plan to learn a whole sound series at once. It works better to attach a new character to a word you already know well. Sound series and meaning series also do not line up with the order spoken language needs. When you learn a new character, check whether it has a sound component and try to match it with others you already know that share that piece, expecting the sounds to differ somewhat.

## Association and visualization

When you learn something new, attach it to something you already know. What you already know is usually more meaningful, and that added meaning makes the new item easier to keep. For a new character, that can mean other characters or components you already have. One learner could remember the familiar left side of a character and kept losing the right side, until that same right-hand piece turned up in another known character. After that match, the form stuck. You can also associate by sound series, meaning series, or similar form or meaning.

Things you can picture are easier to remember. You can usually walk through your house in your mind and count the doors without preparing. Many characters and components began as pictures of real objects and later lost that look. Comparing an older, more picture-like form with the modern form makes the object easier to see again, which makes the modern form easier to hold. The same component can also change differently in different surroundings, so two similar-looking pieces may not share an origin.

A less common character can be worth learning when it fits a group you are already studying, though as a rule you should start with the characters and pieces people meet most often.

## Using the rules together

Other memory rules exist and are less central. They were not covered in these videos.

While you learn, keep looking for ways to understand the object more clearly. For a single component, picture the real-world object when you can. For a character made of several components, understand the internal logic of those pieces, then look for system-level links that tie it to characters you already know. Use common characters to help you learn less common ones. Connect forms, meanings, and sounds on purpose, so later recall has more than one path. The rules work better in combination. Understanding, associating, picturing, and organizing at the same time is the strongest mix.

## Open questions

When I last tried to memorize a form, was I looking at strokes, at pieces that do no job, or at pieces that actually express sound or meaning?

Which character I already know well could this new form attach to, by sound, by meaning, or by a shared piece?
