# Rapid Skill Acquisition

Rapid skill acquisition is the work of taking a new method from the point where you can explain it to the point where you do it without thinking, in as little time as possible. Most of that time goes into practice. Most of the wasted time comes from too much theory, too many new methods at once, or rushing, and getting the proportions right early saves months of relearning later.

## Takeaways

- Being able to explain a method does not mean you can use it.
- Spend about five hours of practice for each hour of theory.
- Learn one or two new techniques at a time, never more.
- Practice replaces current study or work hours instead of adding hours.
- Old habits fire first and have to be replaced deliberately.
- A skill learned in a rush fails in ways you cannot see yourself.

## How it works

A new move starts in the front of the brain, which works out what matters and in what order. That work is slow and tiring, and it takes most of your spare attention. After enough practice the move settles into a stable pattern, and repeated use hands it to deeper brain areas that run habits quickly and cheaply. Fitts and Posner described the same path in three stages: thinking it through, cleaning it up, and doing it automatically.

- While a move needs attention, little mental room is left over.
- Only a stable, repeated pattern becomes a habit.
- In the same situation, the old habit fires first.
- Blocking the old habit tires you, so sessions have a natural end.

```
understand  ->  practise  ->  automatic
  (theory)     (effortful)     (habit)
  1 hour        5+ hours        free
```

## How to pace it

Start with one hour of theory for every five hours of practice, and cap new theory at about five hours a week. Then watch how fast each move becomes automatic, and take in more theory only when practice has caught up. Some learners do well at forty hours of practice for each hour of theory. The time a task takes usually rises at first and then falls below where it started.

- Pair techniques that do not overlap, such as scheduling with note-taking.
- Two note-taking methods at once hide which one caused a mistake.
- Practise each technique until it is correct without strain.
- Re-test the whole routine each time a technique is added.
- Improve from one technique to two to three, never straight to ten.

## Replacing an old habit

The old way of doing a task comes with its own measures of success, such as pages covered or notes written. The new way often scores worse on those measures while giving better results, so the first weeks feel like going backwards. Measure the outcome that matters instead, such as solving a real problem at the level you need. An if-then rule, written ahead of time, says what to do when the moment comes, for example summarising each paragraph whenever the text is dense.

- Write the if-then rules before practice starts.
- After each session, ask how the old habit showed up.
- Then ask what the new method made better.

## Where it fails

Rushing is the most common failure. A person who rushed rarely notices, because confidence rises as skill falls, and someone else usually has to point it out. Fixing it later costs two to three extra months, and by then four to six clear mistakes have grown into twelve to thirty possible causes. Hours of practice are what count, and months without practice add nothing.

- Too much theory: overload and more mistakes.
- Too little theory: nothing new to practise.
- Many techniques at once: errors cannot be traced.
