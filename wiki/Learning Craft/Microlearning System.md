# Microlearning System

Microlearning is a way to study a subject in many short sittings of five to thirty minutes each instead of one long session. It is built for people who rarely get a free hour, such as a university athlete who trains thirty or more hours a week or someone working two jobs. When the sittings follow a set order, a train ride, a lunch break and the wait for a kettle can add up to real understanding of the subject.

## Takeaways

- Each sitting does one step: prime, map, explore, dive or consolidate.
- Scope each topic to about one or two hours of normal study.
- Consolidate after every deep dive, before starting the next one.
- Plan a longer recall session, unless work already uses the subject.
- Long study sessions still work better where you can get them.

## How it works

The cycle has five steps: prime, map, explore, dive and consolidate. A step can be split across several sittings, and several steps can fit into one sitting when there is time. The order is fixed because sittings may be hours apart, and each one has to start from something already built. A deep dive on its own leaves a detail with nothing to attach to, so every dive ends by zooming back out.

- Prime: write the questions you need answered.
- Map: list the resources and what each one holds, with no studying yet.
- Explore: pull out three or four main ideas and how they relate.
- Dive: pick one idea and learn it in depth, in nonlinear notes.
- Consolidate: link what the dive found back to the main ideas.

```
prime -> map -> explore -> dive -> consolidate
                   ^        ^           |
                   |        +-----------+  next idea
                   +--------------------+  picture changed
```

After consolidating, go back to the dive for the next idea, or back to explore if the big picture has changed. If the questions turn out to be wrong or new resources appear, start again from prime.

## A day of sittings

The steps go wherever a gap appears in the day. One day might hold ten minutes of priming on the train, three minutes of mapping while waiting for a lift, twenty minutes of exploring at lunch and a dive while the water boils for dinner. Short bursts seem to sharpen focus, and they force each chunk to stay simple. When the questions from the prime are answered, the next topic gets a prime of its own.

- A tablet and stylus make notes easy to carry between sittings.
- Minor details go into a searchable notes app instead of memory.
- Facts that must be memorized go to flashcards during a dive.
- Keep the flashcard deck small, since true memorization is rare here.

## Retrieval

Knowledge picked up in scattered pieces is easy to lose. The fix is a longer retrieval session once a week, or several smaller ones, in which the learner recalls the material and uses it in mixed order. A weekend block of four to five hours works well. Someone whose daily work already uses the knowledge all the time, such as a person speaking a new language every day or a small business owner learning business, may not need the session.

- Teaching a colleague counts as a retrieval session.
- Presenting the material in a meeting also counts.
- Some managers will set aside a regular hour for this.
