# Fitness Mindsets

Fitness mindsets are the beliefs that decide whether a person keeps training and eating well for years. A person's plan of sets, meals and step counts is a separate thing.

Most people who want a leaner or stronger body already know what to do. The people who know the most about nutrition are not the leanest people. The body a person ends up with depends on whether the training and eating continue for years. Most people do not have the body they want because they have not been consistent. Training much less but never missing puts a person ahead, within six months, of training hard and then stopping.

Examples of these beliefs in practice are refusing the extra food a relative pushes at a family dinner, training three times a week instead of six or seven, and going back to the plan the day after a missed session. The same beliefs apply to how a person dresses: few clothes, kept for a long time, worn with no sign of effort.

## Core takeaways

- A person's training and eating routine lasts when they first change how they see themselves.
- Refusing food, drinks and plans that break a training and eating routine matters more for getting lean than the size of a calorie deficit.
- Do fewer things, such as a small set of exercises and a few meals you repeat, and keep doing them for years.
- Decide once, in advance, when and where you train and what you eat, so daily choices take no thought.
- Start with less than you want to do while you feel motivated, because motivation does not last.
- After a slip, such as a missed training session, go back to your plan the next day without guilt.

## Changing how you see yourself

Lasting change in training and eating comes from changing who you are, rather than only what you do. You start to see yourself as a person with the body and the way of eating you want. Behaviour does not last while the beliefs underneath it stay the same. Those beliefs come from the story you have told yourself about who you are.

Expect to feel resistance to this new identity. The old identity feels safer, because you already know how other people react to it. Find the beliefs that make it hard for you to keep going.

A habit does not change until you can see it. To see it, look at your upbringing, your beliefs, the food around you and your friends. Notice that you are doing the habit before you try to stop it. Then accept the habit, forgive it, and let it go.

Stop insulting your body, and be grateful for it while you change it. Anything done out of stress and self-hate turns out badly. Insulting yourself raises your stress hormones, blood sugar and insulin, and these work against fat loss.

Celebrate small wins, such as one day spent in a calorie deficit. Let the wish for more progress come from liking your body rather than from hating it.

Have an exact picture of the body or the weight you want. A vague goal such as "an attractive body" gives you nothing specific to work toward. Break the goal into monthly targets you can actually reach.

Stop comparing yourself with the bodies on your phone. Those pictures are taken in good lighting, with the muscles pumped up from a workout. Compare yourself with who you were yesterday.

Be the one who decides how your own story goes. In practice, this means saying no more often and setting firmer boundaries.

## Saying no

Learn to refuse food, drinks and plans that break your routine. For getting lean, this skill matters more than keeping a calorie deficit. If friends, family and circumstances set what you eat and when, you are not in control of your body.

Use one fixed answer instead of making a new decision each time. The answer is "I don't eat that." Say it with no apology and no reasons, in a steady tone. Practise it in small uncomfortable situations first, and move up to harder ones.

The people closest to you push food on you because they love you. The people who mock your training are comforting themselves about their own lack of progress.

Spend less time with acquaintances whose invitations, jokes and habits keep your body the way it is. Deepen one or two real friendships. Spend time around people who are doing better than you. Do not take advice about your body from anyone who does not care what is good for your body.

## Doing fewer things

Doing fewer things gives a better result. When attention is spread over many things, each of them is done worse. A person who cannot focus is usually trying to focus on too many things. Adding more things makes a person happier for a short time, and then their happiness goes back to its usual level.

Train three times a week on key exercises, instead of six or seven days a week. Do five or six exercises of three sets each, and leave the gym within an hour. Eighty percent of the result of training comes from twenty percent of the exercises. The rest of the exercises are what exhaust people.

Keep the same exercises instead of swapping them every couple of months. As you keep an exercise over time, you get better at it and can fix more of its small technique details.

Rotate three to five meals or restaurants you like. Work out their calories once, and repeat them. People who change their food every day spend energy on deciding, shopping and counting, until they quit.

Get your form good enough to work the muscle the exercise is meant for and to avoid injury. Then stop fussing over it. Form that is eighty percent correct is enough to start. Trying for perfect form costs strength and intensity, and those matter more.

Removing bad habits does more than adding fixes. Sleep well, exercise, keep a set routine, and stop smoking and drinking. That gets you eighty percent of the result without a single supplement.

Cut training, food, clothes and commitments down in the same way. Drop the burdens you could have dropped months ago.

## Planning over years

Give a change in your body a year or two, instead of a month. Plan on six to twelve months, losing about one kilogram a month. Fat that comes off slowly is more likely to stay off. People who rush the loss gain the fat back within a few months.

The biggest mistake is going fast with drastic measures. The body resists fast change through hormones and sudden spikes of hunger, and it reverses the change. Start months early instead of rushing before a trip. Make small changes to your routine instead of big ones.

Transformation programmes of thirty, sixty or ninety days do not work. The bodies shown in them took years to build. People want a big reward for little effort, and people who sell quick fixes make use of that.

## What optimizing costs

The fitness industry makes money by telling people to do more exercises, take more supplements and go faster.

Nobody forces a person to hit a protein target or to train five times a week. People set those rules for themselves. Once a person sees that, they can choose differently.

Optimizing one thing takes time and effort away from another. There is always a cost somewhere else. A person who misses an optimized schedule feels worse each time, and the guilt makes it harder to stay consistent.

Time with family, a walk, and a moment of not thinking about work are the parts of life that keep a person sane and happy. Optimizing makes those parts look like waste. A relaxed, balanced approach is slower. A person can keep it up, and over years it leads to more progress.

Decide what you value before you decide what to optimize. Keep your protein high, but stop trying to hit the optimal amount. Missing the protein target for one day does not matter. Trying to hit it costs you the enjoyment of your food and your attention to the people at the table.

Fitness matters, and it is not the only thing in life. Keep other hobbies and sports, and leave energy for everything else you do.

## Deciding once

Decision fatigue is the tiredness that comes from making many decisions. It is what exhausts people during a diet. One decision made properly makes every later decision easy.

Fix the times, the places and the meals in advance. Keep the number of things you must do before you start as small as possible. Use a gym and shops that are close to you.

Give training and your daily walk their own slot in the calendar, at the same time each day, and protect that slot. Things that have a set time get done. When training has no set time, other plans take its place. Show up the way you show up for work, whether or not you feel like it.

Arrange your home so the right choice takes no effort. Keep snacks far away and hard to reach, so eating them takes work. If the gym is far away, buy training equipment for home. Most homes are set up to make snacking easy. The food around a person is made to be craved.

## Starting small, and training when you do not feel like it

Motivation does not last. Each choice makes the same choice more likely next time. One diet soda makes the next diet soda more likely.

Start with a step so small that it causes no stress, such as a thousand steps a day. Doing the small things you said you would do is what makes you trust yourself.

Start with less than you want to do while you feel motivated. That means one to three sessions a week, and one to three exercises on a bad day. Set a goal that seems too small. You can add more later. A start that was too big cannot be taken back. People with a lot of motivation commit to five sessions a week and quit by February.

One, two or three times a week is the frequency most people can keep up for anything they want to keep doing. That includes filming, writing and eating out, as well as training. A person who holds back at the start still looks forward to training most of the time.

A person can be present in what they are doing in three states: enthusiasm, enjoyment and acceptance. When enthusiasm and enjoyment are gone, accept the moment as it is and focus on showing up.

Stay in the present instead of thinking all the time about the body you will have later. Obsessing over that future body takes away from the progress already made.

On a day you do not want to train, do not argue with yourself. Pack your gym bag and leave the house. Once you are out of the house, it is too late to talk yourself out of training. People often have good sessions on days they did not want to go.

There is no perfect time to start. Everybody has three hours a week available, so saying you are too busy shows what your priorities are.

## Help from people ahead of you

Almost nobody gets good at anything by studying alone. Ten years spent on something that could have taken three is seven years lost.

Go to a person who is two, three or five steps ahead of you, not to the best expert in the world. That person has made the mistakes you are about to make. Pay them. Paying saves your own time, and you give more attention to something you have paid for.

A hard, military-style talk to yourself gets you started, but the energy from it is gone by the afternoon. A mentor or a group keeps a person going after that.

Saving and watching fitness content is different from acting on it. There is more fitness information than anyone can use. Pick the few things that have lasted, and do them. They are simple, and doing them is still hard.

## After a slip

When you slip off your plan, forgive yourself, forget it, and go back to the plan the very next day. Do not wait two or three days. People who feel guilt or shame eat more. Guilt is the worst feeling to have while trying to get lean. Take today's weight as the new starting point, instead of measuring it against where you were before.

Two or three days of skipping make the next skip easier. Judge consistency over a year rather than over weeks. A year of training with three skipped weeks is better than two perfect months, then two months of nothing, then two perfect months again.

Weight goes up and down while it falls overall. Look at the trend over weeks instead of today's number. A plateau happens when the body slows down weight loss because the loss has been too fast. Most people who quit do so because they cannot handle the ups and downs in their weight. The training itself did not fail.

Know before you start that plateaus will come. When one comes, eat a little more on purpose.

## Stress, hunger and cravings

Stress is what makes people binge. It raises insulin and weakens the body's signal that it is full, so a person keeps eating to escape whatever they are avoiding. Find what is causing your stress, and remove the causes you can. Walk, meditate, practise gratitude, and choose slow yoga over a hot yoga class. Notice the moment when you are eating to get away from something.

When people break a diet because they feel hungry, the feeling is usually not a need for food. Ask what you are trying to fill or cover with food. It may be a lack of affection, of self-esteem, of confidence or of purpose.

A craving for processed sugary food is a sign of an emotional need. Real hunger means the day's calories were too low, and then any nourishing food will do. Being hungry is a normal, healthy state. It is not a problem that has to be solved with food right away. While you are hungry, your body is taking the energy it needs from stored fat.

Wait five or ten minutes before a second helping. After training, wait thirty to forty minutes before eating, because real hunger has not arrived yet. Do not push the wait so far that you get angry.

## Tracking for a while, then stopping

Write down your weight, your calories, your steps and your workouts. Track nothing else. Count calories accurately for two weeks. After that, you can estimate them for the rest of your life.

Weigh yourself every morning. Watch the week rather than the day, and learn which changes are fat and which are only water. Write down the sets, reps and weight of every exercise, so you arrive at the gym knowing what you have to beat.

People mostly quit when they stop seeing progress, and the mirror is unreliable from day to day. Photos and written numbers show progress that the mirror does not show. Tracking everything for the rest of your life takes the fun out of eating.

## Fitness habits applied to clothes

Own a small number of basic pieces, and get their fit right before you add anything. The basics are jeans, t-shirts, trainers, chinos and plain trousers. Timeless basics like these make up about eighty percent of dressing well.

Buy the fundamental colours: white, black, beige, khaki, brown, navy, grey and military green. Make eighty percent of what you buy and wear these colours, and keep patterns and bright colours to the rest. When everything matches, getting dressed takes no thought.

Wear fewer things, and leave space in the outfit. Every extra item is another chance to make a mistake. Before you leave the house, take one item off, because in front of the mirror you probably put on too much.

When you see a look you like, work out why it works, such as the fit, the material, the shape or the colour. Copy that instead of buying the same items.

Wear a garment until it softens and takes the shape of your body. Worn clothes look better than new ones. Give a new piece a couple of weeks. Buy good fabric, because it ages better with every wash.

Put effort into dressing well, but do not let the effort show. An outfit that looks planned to impress looks worse than the same outfit when it looks unplanned. Never show off what you have, whether logos, a new purchase or your physique. Wear your most expensive piece as casually as you wear slippers.

Wear normal or slightly loose fits, so your body shows only in glimpses when you move. Tight rash guards and tank tops worn to show off muscle make a man look worse.

Being lean is what makes clothes fit. At a low body fat, clothes hang properly whatever you wear. Fat on the face comes off only when total body fat comes off, and losing it is what shows the jawline.

Clothes look good on a person whose attention is on something else. Skaters look good in ordinary clothes because skating is what they care about, and the clothes matter less to them. The person matters more than the clothes. A mannequin in the best clothes does not look good, because there is no person inside them.

Dressing well is a skill, and it is learned by getting it wrong. Expect about three seasons of buying and selling before a wardrobe is even decent.

Several of these dressing habits match a fitness habit. Owning a few basics matches using as few exercises as possible. Buying the fundamental colours matches eating the same few meals. Copying why a look works matches learning why a training or eating method works and building your own plan. Wearing a garment for years matches giving a change in your body a year or two.

## How other people see you

A person who is deeply absorbed in a craft they love looks their most attractive and charismatic. Charisma comes from being genuinely good at something and treating that as normal instead of showing it off. It cannot be faked. Serious focus on a craft, backed by skill, is itself charisma.

The fastest way to lose charisma is to panic when something goes wrong. React slowly and stay relaxed. Use everyday mishaps to practise this.

Your facial expression matters more than your face. Let your face and voice show emotion, including negative emotion, instead of holding a flat, pleasant expression. Showing emotion lets people see that you are comfortable and not afraid of being judged.

Posture is one of the biggest things that decide how people see you. An open posture makes a person feel open and confident, and a hunched posture makes them anxious. People rarely see themselves walk, so the first step is to notice that your own posture is poor.

## How to practice this

1. When someone offers food that breaks your eating plan, say "I don't eat that," with no apology or reasons. Practise it first in small uncomfortable situations, then in harder ones. Notice that you no longer make a new decision each time.
2. Give training and your daily walk the same time slot in your calendar each day, and protect it. Show up at that time the way you show up for work. Notice that training with a set time gets done, and other plans stop taking its place.
3. Start a new habit at one to three times a week, even if you feel like doing more. Notice that you still look forward to it most of the time. Notice that doing the small things you said you would do makes you trust yourself.
4. Wait five or ten minutes before taking a second helping. While you wait, ask what you are trying to fill or cover with food. Notice whether only processed sugary food will do, since that is a sign of an emotional need.
5. After a slip, go back to your plan the very next day. Take today's weight as your new starting point. Notice any guilt, because guilt and shame make people eat more.
6. Before you leave the house, take one item off your outfit. Notice how often you had put on too much in front of the mirror.

## Open questions

1. Should a person who is restarting after a break put most of their effort into diet or into training? Muscle does not show until a person is lean, so one approach puts eighty percent of the effort into diet. Another approach gets the person back into the gym first and sorts out the diet afterwards.
2. Some people have quit tracking apps before. Would two weeks of calorie counting with a daily weigh-in keep them going, or would keeping the body untracked, as The Accretion Frame does?
