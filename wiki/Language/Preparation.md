# Preparation

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Preparation is studying a language on purpose so that time spent immersed in it is easier to understand. It is one of three pillars of language learning, alongside interactive immersion and freeflow, and progress depends on keeping the pillars in balance. Its main forms are learning the writing system, priming vocabulary with a spaced repetition tool, and reading grammar primers. Each aims at comprehension, not at memorizing rules for speaking. Preparation works when its words and patterns start turning up and making sense during immersion. It fails when it crowds out immersion, starts too early, or turns into drilling. Small daily limits keep it in proportion.

## Core takeaways

- Preparation is learning about the language so that immersion yields more understanding.
- It is one of three pillars, and all three need balance.
- Vocabulary priming, writing-system study and grammar primers are its main forms.
- The test is whether prepared words and patterns show up and make sense in real content.
- Keep it to short daily sessions, and spend extra time on immersion instead.

## The forms preparation can take

Preparation takes a few common forms. Learning the writing system comes first, and once a phonetic script feels comfortable, that time moves to the language itself. Vocabulary priming uses a spaced repetition app to learn the most common words first, since a handful of words turn up far more often than the rest, and learning those first lets a learner understand the language comfortably. Any spaced repetition software works, as do some pen-and-paper methods. Grammar primers come later. They introduce the patterns of a language for the first time and explain what is going on, without asking the learner to produce those patterns.

## How to tell whether preparation worked

Preparation shows its value inside immersion. A useful check is a noticing game, where the learner watches or reads real content and looks out for the words or grammar points they just studied. Spotting a new word in the wild, or finally understanding a sentence that used a primed pattern, is the sign that preparation did its job. Primers are also a reference to return to. When something in immersion is confusing, looking it up in the primer turns the confusion into understanding.

## Three ways preparation fails

1. It crowds out immersion. Vocabulary review is easy to overdo, and preparation that eats into immersion time defeats its purpose, since acquisition happens through immersion.
2. It starts too early. Grammar primers do not help a learner who knows almost nothing yet. They work after roughly 10 to 15 hours of immersion and a couple of hundred beginner words, with a quick way to look up unknown words.
3. It becomes drilling. Languages hold far too many dense patterns to memorize efficiently, so grammar primers are read for understanding, not drilled. The patterns are learned through time in the language.

## How much preparation is enough

Short, steady sessions are enough. A common setup is about 10 to 20 minutes of vocabulary review a day, with about 10 new words. If reviews keep running past 20 minutes, the learner adds fewer new words, and extra time goes to immersion, not to more new cards. Reviews are finished every day even when that takes longer. Grammar priming runs at about 5 to 10 minutes a day, one point at a time.

## How to practice this

1. Learn the writing system until the phonetic script is comfortable.
2. Add about 10 new frequent words a day in a spaced repetition app and finish reviews daily.
3. After 10 to 15 hours of immersion, start one grammar point a day from a primer.
4. Play the noticing game in immersion with what you studied that day.
5. If preparation passes 20 minutes or so, cut new items and immerse more.
