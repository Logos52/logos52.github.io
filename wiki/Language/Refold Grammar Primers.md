# Grammar primers

A grammar primer is a short written tour of the most common patterns in one language, read once so that those patterns stand out later in real speech and text. For a learner who studies mainly by watching, listening and reading, it takes the place of the grammar textbook. Grammar then takes five to ten minutes a day, and no rule is drilled.

## Takeaways

- The grammar itself is learned from many hours of real input.
- A primer shows a pattern once, so it can be noticed later.
- Start after 10 to 15 hours of input and a few hundred words.
- Read without memorising, and skip what does not make sense yet.
- Re-read the whole primer after about every 50 hours of input.
- A primer is for understanding sentences, and correct speech comes later.

## How it works

Every language has thousands of patterns, and too many of them are too dense to memorise efficiently. A learner who builds sentences from memorised rules is translating from the native language, and that is slow. It also sounds unnatural, because native speakers use set phrases that no rule predicts. A primer does a smaller job. It gives a first look at a pattern and a rough idea of what the pattern means, and the hours of listening and reading that follow do the learning.

- Words carry most of the meaning, and grammar joins them.
- So vocabulary comes before grammar study.
- A pattern seen in the primer is easier to notice in input.
- Each sighting in input adds to the learner's instinct for it.
- Knowing a rule helps a learner check a sentence after saying it.

```
primer ---> input ---> confusion ---> primer
first       see it     look it        read
look        used       up             again
       <------ 2 or 3 rounds ------>
```

## How to use one

A primer is ordered by what a speaker of English is likely to meet first and find hardest, so the order differs from language to language. A Spanish primer opens with verbs, and a German one with nouns. A Japanese one opens with the predicate, the part of a sentence that says what happens or what is so. When something in input is confusing, the learner can jump straight to the section that covers it. Once the primer feels comfortable, a full grammar reference takes over, and the learner searches it for each new point met in input.

- Before starting, have a quick way to look up words.
- Study one new point a day, for five to ten minutes.
- Then look for that point in the day's input.
- When a sentence confuses you, find its pattern in the primer.
- Leave a hard section and come back after more input.
- Accept a pattern as it is, without asking why.

## Examples

Two learners show how the primer and the input take turns. A Japanese learner with about 15 hours of input keeps meeting sentences that seem to be missing a part, looks it up, and finds that Japanese usually drops the subject. A Spanish learner keeps hearing the ending -aba in people's stories about their past, looks it up, and finds a past form. In both cases the pattern made sense only after two or three rounds of input and re-reading.

- Japanese: the subject is usually left out and guessed from context.
- Spanish: one verb has about 150 possible forms.
- None of them need to be known at the start.
