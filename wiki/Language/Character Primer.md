# Character Primer

Chinese and Japanese are written with characters instead of an alphabet. Each character stands for a word or for a part of a word. A beginner who knows how characters join into words, why one character can have many meanings, and which parts of a character give hints can start on real text sooner.

## Takeaways

- A character stands for a word or a word part.
- Most words are one or two characters long.
- A common character can have ten or more meanings.
- Context decides which meaning and which reading applies.
- Most characters hold a meaning hint and a sound hint.
- Breaking a character into parts makes it easier to remember.

## Characters and words

The earliest characters were simple drawings. A few still look like what they mean, such as 山 for mountain and 火 for fire, and most do not. Many characters are words on their own, and others only appear inside longer words. Most modern Mandarin words are two characters long, and the meaning of a two-character word cannot always be guessed from the characters in it.

- 火, fire, and 山, mountain, make 火山, volcano.
- 牛, cow, and 肉, meat, make 牛肉, beef.
- Some characters mostly do grammar work: 了, 到, 上, 下, 来.

## Context

A character used in many words collects many meanings, and some characters have more than one pronunciation. Japanese characters have more readings than Chinese ones, because Japanese uses fewer characters for more words. The reader picks the right meaning and reading from the words around it, the same way an English reader handles a word with several meanings. The two characters 马上 are a common example: 马 is horse and 上 is on or above, and together they mean a different thing in each of three sentences.

- "Riding 马上 all day" means on horseback.
- "Get up 马上" means immediately.
- "Birds flew 马上" means above the horse.
- When context does not settle it, guess and move on.

## Parts of a character

Almost every character can be split into smaller parts. The parts that give hints are a meaning part and a sound part. The meaning part, usually on the left or the top, hints at what the character is about, and the sound part, usually on the right, hints at how it is said. Neither hint holds every time, and a learner who can name the parts still remembers a few known pieces rather than every stroke.

- 氵, water, sits in 河 river, 湖 lake, 波 wave and 海 sea.
- 口, mouth, sits in 吃 eat, 叫 shout and 洞 hole.
- 明, bright, is 日 sun plus 月 moon.
- The 214 dictionary headings called radicals are not worth studying alone.

## How to learn them

Meet a new character first in a deck of vocabulary cards, then see it many times while reading and listening to real material. Each time you see the character and try to recall its meaning and sound, it gets easier to remember. Break every new character into parts from the first day, before you know which parts give meaning and which give sound. Watch for a sound part that repeats, since a known sound part helps you guess the reading of a new character.

- Learn each character inside a word.
- Split each new character into known parts.
- Notice sound parts shared between characters.
