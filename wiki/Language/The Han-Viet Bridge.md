# The Hán-Việt Bridge

The Hán-Việt bridge is the head start a Vietnamese speaker has when learning Mandarin. Over many centuries Vietnamese borrowed a large share of its words from Chinese, and those words, called Sino-Vietnamese or Hán-Việt, are still everyday Vietnamese, with sounds that match Mandarin in regular ways. A learner who knows the regular sound changes can guess the meaning of many Mandarin words on first contact, and knows where the guess will fail.

## Takeaways

- A Vietnamese speaker already knows about 3,000 syllables borrowed from Chinese.
- 學生 is học sinh in Vietnamese and xuéshēng in Mandarin.
- Meaning carries over well.
- Sound carries over less reliably.
- A few sound rules turn a Vietnamese reading into a Mandarin guess.
- Syllables ending in -p, -t or -c are where tone guesses fail.
- Some shared words have drifted to very different meanings.

## How it works

Both the Vietnamese and the Mandarin readings of a character come down from Middle Chinese, the Chinese spoken in the Tang period and recorded in rhyme dictionaries such as the Qieyun. Each language then changed its sounds on its own schedule, so the two readings of one character differ, in patterns that repeat. Vietnamese has been written in the Latin alphabet since the early twentieth century, so a Vietnamese speaker holds the sound and the meaning of these words without knowing the characters. The bridge lets the learner attach a new character to a meaning and a sound family already known.

```
        Middle Chinese  學生
         /            \
  Vietnamese          Mandarin
  học sinh            xuéshēng
  (known)             (guessed from the left)
```

- Recognising a shared word is fast and takes little effort.
- The meaning link is the strongest part of the bridge.
- The sound link is a clue that still needs checking.
- A Hán-Việt sound can narrow down which character is meant.

## The sound rules

A handful of regular changes cover a large number of words. Vietnamese kept the final consonants -p, -t and -c from Middle Chinese, and Mandarin lost them, so those syllables end in a vowel in Mandarin. The same syllables belonged to a tone class called the entering tone, and Mandarin spread that class across all four of its tones with no reliable pattern. Any Hán-Việt syllable ending in -p, -t or -c is therefore a signal to look the tone up instead of guessing it.

- Final -p, -t or -c: the Mandarin syllable ends in a vowel.
- Final -m becomes -n in Mandarin.
- Initial nh- becomes r- in Mandarin, as in nhân and rén, 人.
- Entering-tone syllables: the Mandarin tone cannot be predicted.

## Where it fails

The Chinese words in Vietnamese came in at three different times, and a word from an earlier time can have a sound that breaks the regular rules. Those older borrowings often look like native Vietnamese words, so a learner who applies the rules to them guesses wrong. Meanings also drift apart over centuries. The best known trap is a word that means an ordinary difficulty in Mandarin and is a strong insult in Vietnamese.

- Older borrowings follow different sound rules.
- 困難 kùnnán means difficulty in Mandarin.
- Its Vietnamese reading, khốn nạn, means despicable.
- Check the Mandarin meaning of a shared word before using it.
