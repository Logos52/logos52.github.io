# Refold Language Learning System

The lessons that teach this method cover the first two phases. Those phases are fundamentals, and reading plus listening. The two phases are similar. During fundamentals, the learner is still working on the earliest pieces of the language and making it stop sounding like noise. The reading-plus-listening phase is about gaining comprehension and picking up the first 5,000 words. That work is done by reading text, including books, captions, transcripts, and similar material, paired with audio whenever possible.

Those two phases still have to be spent in a useful mix of activities. Inside the same lessons, progress is a balance of three pillars. The pillars are preparation, interactive immersion, and freeflow. Preparation is learning about the language and priming for acquisition, meaning studying ahead of time so later immersion is easier to understand. Interactive immersion expands understanding through interactive experiences and tools. Freeflow turns conscious knowledge into subconscious abilities.

## How the course is meant to be used

Homework assignments are a baseline. If more time is available, do more than the assignment asks. The core of making progress is to spend time with the language. Extra time is best spent on activities where the learner is actively understanding the language, even if only a little.

The lessons give specific instructions, which removes as many decisions as possible. Some things will not work for everyone. The learner still has to make the choice or adaptation that fits their circumstances and learning style.

Someone who is not a total beginner should move through the first week quickly. A recap of the material within a day or two is still recommended. A whole week of review would be boring if the basics have already been learned elsewhere. The lessons were built as the first day through the thirtieth day, in sequence, and going slower is fine. Staying on the same day twice and repeating the homework is allowed. Some people go through the lessons twice. The first pass absorbs what it can. The second pass focuses on what did not make sense the first time.

## Aim-Shoot-Skin for Language Learning

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Aim-Shoot-Skin applies a three-step study method to languages, and its core is learning from material just beyond the learner's current level. In the aim step, the learner picks dialogue or text they genuinely care about that they half understand, then spins out variations of what they would want to say. In the shoot step, they look up and learn whatever they need to produce those variations, and they use each new word often alongside flashcards. Most of the learning happens in this loop, and a learner can become fluent on it alone. The optional skin step hunts for patterns, such as shared roots and character parts, so there is less to memorize. It pays off most for scripts like Chinese.

## Aiming

Aiming means finding the edge of comprehension. Language input falls into three bands: what the learner already understands comfortably, a band just above that, and material that is too hard to follow. The middle band is the target. There the learner might catch about half the words but still get the gist from context. The method depends on not memorizing the new words in advance. The learner pieces meaning together from words they know, tone, gestures and context, and words worked out that way stick far better than words drilled on flashcards.

Interest matters more than topic. The best material is whatever the learner genuinely cares about, such as a favorite scene they want to follow without subtitles. One passage can then be multiplied into many questions. If one person said this and got that reply, the learner can ask what they would have said instead and how the other person might answer. The branches can be drafted in the learner's own language first. The aim step's job is to create curiosity, because curiosity puts the learner in a problem-solving frame.

## Shooting

Shooting is simple once the aim is clear. When the learner hits a word or phrase they cannot yet say, they look it up and work out how to say it. Because the aim has already set out what the learner wants to say, the job is only to fill gaps.

New words need flashcards and constant use together. Flashcards alone fade. A word drilled in isolation gains no new meaning and stays cut off from other words and sentence patterns, so it needs many repeats. Use alone fails too, because without a record the learner believes they know a word until they meet it again and find it gone. Using each new word as often as possible, in speaking, listening, reading and writing, keeps the cycle fast. The more a word is used, the less the flashcard is needed. The shooting can only be as good as the aim behind it, since a weak aim gives it no direction.

## What the loop is for

The loop works because learning has a purpose. Relevant input and a problem the learner cares about make retention much higher. Immersion abroad and training that teaches only the language needed for real conversations both work for this reason. As the loop runs, material that was just out of reach becomes comfortable and the comprehension edge moves outward, so the learner re-aims at new material and repeats. The method scales to advanced levels. A fluent learner can use technical material where they know about 97% of words instead of nearly all of them, and the technique stays the same.

## The optional pass

The skin step is optional for languages but speeds up reading and writing. Its job is to find connections and patterns so the learner memorizes less. Where 100 words would otherwise be learned one by one, a learner who finds shared roots might really learn about 42 and combine them for the rest. Etymology helps, as with a Greek root for light that runs through photograph and photometry. For Chinese, where each character has to be learned on its own, structure inside the script cuts the load. Characters share components, and memorable links help, as when the country character is pictured as a king inside a boundary.

A practical routine is a periodic flashcard review. Every few weeks or once a month, scanning the whole deck for patterns not noticed before takes about half an hour and usually surfaces a few useful links. Mind maps can hold the connections but quickly get crowded. When time is short, running the aim and shoot loop well comes first, and pattern hunting is a bonus for spare time.

## What is actually known

The evidence behind this method is mixed in quality. The comprehensible input idea, that people acquire language from input just above their level, is the hypothesis the course builds on, and the method leans on how young children pick up languages without flashcards. The specific figures, such as catching half the words at the edge or the 100-to-42 saving from roots, are illustrations from a learning-skills course, not measured results. They have not been independently checked.

## Attention is Important

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Attention is Important

Building an instinct for a new language takes a great deal of time spent immersing in it. The hard part, early on, is how to pay attention and learn when the language is not yet understood. The way through that stretch is to pay attention even before the language makes sense, and to have fun doing it.

That is why time spent in the language is treated as a high-attention activity. Background listening or passive listening does not count.

## What to look for

The practice that makes that attention concrete is the noticing game. While spending time in the language, look for cognates, words already known, or words from a word list or bingo card. If those lists run out, notice things from priming, meaning earlier study done to raise comprehension during later immersion, or use additional word lists.

The first session that introduces this work is short on purpose, so the habit can start before the language is understood. It is 20 minutes in total. Ten minutes go to learning the writing system. Once the phonetic writing system is comfortable, that same block becomes more time with the language. The other ten minutes are time in the language spent on the noticing work above.

## Freeflow Immersion

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Freeflow Immersion

Freeflow immersion is a way to spend time in the language that lets the learner unwind and enjoy the material while remaining attentive. It builds self-sufficiency by relying on subconscious understanding. It also prepares the learner for real-world situations where language tools will not be at hand.

That mix of ease and attention is also why it can give so much more exposure. One advantage is up to five times more contact with the language. If the learner is not engaged with the content, nothing is gained from it. The content has to match both interest and skill level.

Freeflow does not take the place of intensive immersion, and it remains high-attention work. Without attention it has no value.

## How a session looks

Because the point is to rely on understanding that is already forming, the session reuses content rather than hunting for something new. Reuse content that has already been used for interactive immersion. Watch it without lookups or rewinding. Afterward, note how much was understood, whether it was interesting, whether it was fun, and whether focus held.

If the gist comes through and some parts stay unclear, keep immersing. Understanding comes with time. Re-watching content that was already done intensively, or that was already seen in a first language, makes freeflow easier.

A session around this work is 45 minutes in total. Twenty minutes of that session are time in the language spent this way.

## Hacking Comprehension Menu

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Hacking Comprehension Menu

A menu of tools, techniques, and content choices can raise comprehension while immersing. The items fall into tools that sit on the media, techniques the learner applies, and choices about what to watch or read. In the list below, the language being learned is the target language, and the learner's first language is the native language.

## Tools on the media

Matching target-language subtitles make it easier to parse the words people are saying. A popup dictionary helps the learner look up words while immersing. Automatic pausing gives the time needed to read and understand. Blurred native-language translations let the learner check understanding and fill in gaps.

## Techniques

Priming is studying the language to gain extra information that raises comprehension during later immersion. Reducing speed gives more time to process the language. The speed should not go below 80 percent, or things get weird. Rewinding, in reading or in listening, gives more than one chance to understand something. Lookups of words, sentences, or grammar help explain unknown or confusing things during the session. Spoiling the plot means reading about the thing before consuming it, so the learner knows what will happen and can infer meaning from context.

## Content choices

Reuse known content, meaning things already consumed in the native language, or already used for interactive immersion or for freeflow, a later watch without lookups or rewinding. Content about topics already known well also helps, such as cooking, business, science, or fashion. Simple and simplified material is another option, including learner content, native content with simple plots or dialogue, or content aimed at children. Material with a lot of non-verbal information helps too, such as overdramatic acting, pictures, graphics, or visual diagrams.

## Immersion Metalayers

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Immersion Metalayers

A metalayer is a tool that sits on top of immersion and makes the session easier to work with. It can let the learner look up words in a show, pause automatically after each line of dialogue, and jump between subtitle lines.

Those functions show up together in one working setup that uses a browser tool, and that tool behaves the same way on YouTube and on Netflix. After it is added and the page is reloaded, the learner sets a translation language, sets the subtitle language to the language being learned, and hides the translations so they are not visible yet. Autopause is turned on. Playback then stops after each line on its own. The learner can hover a word to look it up, click a line in the side list to jump back to it, and hover the hidden translation when a check of the meaning is needed.

The same tool also has catalogs of YouTube and Netflix titles by language, which can be used to see what exists. Once the layer is in place, the content to use is dual-channel input with matching subtitles, meaning the learner reads and hears the language at the same time. One search mentioned for finding that kind of content on Netflix is a global title search. After setup, the noticing game is played on that read-and-listen content.

Learners of Chinese or Japanese are pointed at ready-made sources for that content on the tenth-day page of the resource sheet. Everyone else is pointed at the ninth-day page of the sheet. A session around this work is 35 minutes in total, including ten minutes of time in the language on that read-and-listen content.

## Interactive Immersion

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Interactive Immersion

Interactive immersion names a family of activities, not one exercise. It expands understanding through hands-on experiences and tools. The activities that belong in the category are the noticing game, in more than one named version, plus sentence mining, intensive listening, and transcription. The last three sit outside the lessons that list them.

A session of this work includes 20 minutes in the language. It uses dual-channel input, meaning content the learner reads and hears at the same time. On that content the learner keeps playing the noticing game, and watches for two-target sentences when they show up. A two-target sentence, sometimes written 2T, is a sentence with two things the learner needs to look up to piece it through. One check used in the session is to pick an example sentence from the immersion and count the known and unknown words.

Intensive reading is related, and it is not the same as the noticing game alone. It also looks for two-target sentences and does lookups to get the meaning of the sentence. If no two-target content can be found, the noticing game is the fallback. The two-target threshold is a reminder not to look up every single word. Ten minutes of the activity may cover only a few minutes of content, because the work is the looking-up, not the runtime of the video.

Sentence mining, listed above as one of the interactive activities, means taking sentences and making priming material out of them. Priming here means turning those sentences into study material, such as cards, that will later raise comprehension during immersion. The lessons that name sentence mining do not teach how to do it.

## Language Isn't Math

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Language Isn't Math

Most language programs rest on a skill-building model that makes no distinction between learning and acquisition, and treats the two as the same thing. The learner first studies vocabulary and grammar rules on purpose, then practices using those rules and words to speak. With enough practice, speaking will become intuitive and natural, the way a first language already is. In practice the model does not get people to high levels in a foreign language.

The first problem is an assumption inside the model. The model treats a human language as vocabulary plus grammar rules. If that were true, memorizing the relevant words and rules would let a person translate any thought into the target language. That cannot work, because human speech is highly specific in ways no one can predict.

## The same ideas, said differently

Languages give the same basic ideas different shapes, each language in its own way. The examples here are English and Japanese, though the same principles apply to other languages as well.

English speakers say they "play the piano." Japanese speakers say *piano o hiku*, which means they "pluck the piano," because a piano has strings. There is no deeper reason for the difference. English speakers say "I'm hungry." Japanese speakers say *onaka suita*, "my stomach has become empty." An English speaker who cannot find a phone asks "Have you seen my phone?" A Japanese speaker in the same situation asks *keitai shiranai*, "Do you know my phone?" That wording sounds strange in English, and in Japanese it is understood as asking whether the other person knows where the phone is right now.

## Set phrases

Native speakers also express common ideas in arbitrary set phrases. An English speaker says "I need to go to the bathroom," those exact words, many times over a life. A Japanese speaker says *toire ikitai*, "I want to go to the bathroom." It is possible, in Japanese, to say the wording that matches "I need to go to the bathroom," and it sounds unnatural. The reverse is also true. English speakers can say "I want to go to the bathroom," yet that is not how they usually say it.

Asking for a phone number works the same way. English has the set phrase "Can I get your number?" Japanese has *denwa bango oshiete*, "Can you teach me your phone number?" There is no deeper reason for either wording. Native speakers almost always express these ideas the same way, using those same words in that same order every time. It is not a creative process. It is set in stone.

## What translation produces

One speaking strategy is to think in a first language, then use memorized grammar rules and vocabulary to translate the thought. The best case is that the speaker is still understood and sounds odd or funny. The worst case is that the speaker is not understood at all.

A Japanese speaker thinking in Japanese and translating into English with memorized rules might say "Can you teach me your phone number?" A listener would probably still understand, even though it sounds a little funny. The same speaker might say "Do you know my phone?" when the English phrase is "Have you seen my phone?" A listener may not be able to tell what is being asked, because English does not express the idea that way.

## Ideas one language has and another does not

Different wording for one shared idea is not the whole problem. Different languages also express different ideas in the first place. Japanese has many words with no English equivalent. They name things Japanese speakers say often, and that English speakers do not say.

*Sokudou* means "instant response." If a person offers a date and hears no at once, a Japanese speaker might say *sokudou*. The word marks that the other person did not even think about it. The refusal came so quickly that it showed complete confidence.

*Gyakugire* means "reverse anger." It names the moment when the person at fault becomes angry anyway, as a way to defend themselves. If someone is told not to eat a yogurt in the fridge, eats it anyway, and then gets angry at the owner for having put the yogurt there, that anger is *gyakugire*. The other person can name it directly. Japanese speakers simply say different things than English speakers tend to say.

## Imitate or guess

Native speech has two properties that matter together. It is set in stone, meaning native speakers usually repeat the same ideas in the same forms. It is also highly specific in unpredictable ways. Abstraction does not get a learner very far. To express an idea in a way that will sound natural to native speakers, the speaker has to already know the precise form natives usually choose for that idea. To speak like a native, the speaker also has to know which kinds of ideas natives usually voice to begin with.

Either the speaker already knows how natives tend to speak and can imitate that, or the speaker does not and is making blind guesses based on a first language. Those guesses fail in a large majority of cases.

Language is not like math, where a formula can deduce the answer. Either the speaker already knows the answer ahead of time, or the speaker does not. There is no space for guessing or for inventing a wording on the fly.

Plenty of room remains to be clever and inventive in language use, or poetry would not exist. The idea is to first get a firm grip on how the language is supposed to work, then later bend those patterns the way an artist would. Getting creative with language before the speaker has mastered the target language sounds weird and unnatural at best, and is not understood at all at worst.

## Noticing Game

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Noticing Game

The noticing game is a high-attention way to spend time in a language that may not yet be understood. Background listening or passive listening does not count, because without attention the time does no work.

What the learner looks for are handholds inside that barely understood stream. Those handholds are cognates, words already known, or words from a word list or bingo card. If those lists run out, the next place to look is priming, meaning earlier study done to raise comprehension during later immersion, or additional word lists.

The same search can follow the learner's other study. Once new words have been learned that day, the game can be pointed at those words. After a short stretch of grammar study done for the same reason, it can be pointed at a grammar pattern, looking for that pattern in the wild.

The game belongs to interactive immersion, the wider set of activities that use tools to go deeper into input. After a metalayer is set up, meaning a lookup-and-pause layer on top of a show, the game is played on dual-channel input. Dual-channel input is content the learner reads and hears at the same time.

Intensive reading goes further than that search alone. It also looks for two-target sentences and does lookups to get the meaning of each sentence. A two-target sentence, sometimes written 2T, is a sentence with two things the learner needs to look up to piece it through. If no such content can be found, the noticing game is the fallback. The two-target threshold is a reminder not to look up every single word.

## Preparation

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Preparation is studying a language on purpose so that time spent immersed in it is easier to understand. It is one of three pillars of language learning, alongside interactive immersion and freeflow, and progress depends on keeping the pillars in balance. Its main forms are learning the writing system, priming vocabulary with a spaced repetition tool, and reading grammar primers. Each aims at comprehension, not at memorizing rules for speaking. Preparation works when its words and patterns start turning up and making sense during immersion. It fails when it crowds out immersion, starts too early, or turns into drilling. Small daily limits keep it in proportion.

## Core takeaways

- Preparation is learning about the language so that immersion yields more understanding.
- It is one of three pillars, and all three need balance.
- Vocabulary priming, writing-system study and grammar primers are its main forms.
- The test is whether prepared words and patterns show up and make sense in real content.
- Keep it to short daily sessions, and spend extra time on immersion instead.

## The forms preparation can take

Preparation takes a few common forms. Learning the writing system comes first, and once a phonetic script feels comfortable, that time moves to the language itself. Vocabulary priming uses a spaced repetition app to learn the most common words first, since a handful of words turn up far more often than the rest, and learning those first lets a learner understand the language comfortably. Any spaced repetition software works, as do some pen-and-paper methods. Grammar primers come later. They introduce the patterns of a language for the first time and explain what is going on, without asking the learner to produce those patterns.

## How to tell whether preparation worked

Preparation shows its value inside immersion. A useful check is a noticing game, where the learner watches or reads real content and looks out for the words or grammar points they just studied. Spotting a new word in the wild, or finally understanding a sentence that used a primed pattern, is the sign that preparation did its job. Primers are also a reference to return to. When something in immersion is confusing, looking it up in the primer turns the confusion into understanding.

## Three ways preparation fails

1. It crowds out immersion. Vocabulary review is easy to overdo, and preparation that eats into immersion time defeats its purpose, since acquisition happens through immersion.
2. It starts too early. Grammar primers do not help a learner who knows almost nothing yet. They work after roughly 10 to 15 hours of immersion and a couple of hundred beginner words, with a quick way to look up unknown words.
3. It becomes drilling. Languages hold far too many dense patterns to memorize efficiently, so grammar primers are read for understanding, not drilled. The patterns are learned through time in the language.

## How much preparation is enough

Short, steady sessions are enough. A common setup is about 10 to 20 minutes of vocabulary review a day, with about 10 new words. If reviews keep running past 20 minutes, the learner adds fewer new words, and extra time goes to immersion, not to more new cards. Reviews are finished every day even when that takes longer. Grammar priming runs at about 5 to 10 minutes a day, one point at a time.

## How to practice this

1. Learn the writing system until the phonetic script is comfortable.
2. Add about 10 new frequent words a day in a spaced repetition app and finish reviews daily.
3. After 10 to 15 hours of immersion, start one grammar point a day from a primer.
4. Play the noticing game in immersion with what you studied that day.
5. If preparation passes 20 minutes or so, cut new items and immerse more.

## Reading stories pitched at your vocabulary

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

A language model can write a short daily story that uses only the words a learner knows, adds a few new ones and repeats the ones due for review. The story itself then drives the learner's word schedule. Models stray from the list, so code checks every story and retries the failures. The idea breaks at the very start of learning. With only 10 to 100 known words, one builder found the model could not write anything that read like a story. The builder judged the design not yet useful until a learner knows roughly 1,000 words. The builder's workaround was to have the model write stories from a fixed beginner word list until those words became automatic. Two other projects avoid the problem from different sides. One retells a real story at five fixed levels, each held to its own list of allowed characters and audited, and the other measures each story's reading level and rewrites the ones that miss. Measuring and rewriting raised that second project's on-target stories from 42% to 71%, once the model wrote each page as separate sentences with word counts set.

## A daily story that drives the word schedule

A Mandarin reading tool built this way sends the model three groups of words each day. The first group holds 60 candidate new words, from which the model picks 5 to 10 that fit the story. The words due for review form the second group, and the 300 most frequent words in the pool are always available as glue.

Code then checks the result. Every sentence has to split cleanly into its listed words, and every word has to come from the allowed pool or be tagged as outside it. The story also has to land within 20% of its target length. A failed structure check triggers a retry, up to 15 attempts. New and due words that the model leaves out simply stay due for the next day. Making those two checks soft cut the retries and raised the quality, and the review schedule still worked because skipped words came back. Words the model used from outside the pool go onto a wishlist, which shows which words the list is missing.

## Where it breaks at low vocabulary

The trouble starts with how few words a beginner has. With 10 to 100 words introduced, the stories came out as chains of unrelated clauses with no tension and no ending, and sometimes as nonsense. The builder judged this a structural limit that better prompts alone could not fix, because the model could not write a natural 200-character story from 30 words.

The word list made it worse. It came from podcast speech, so it lacked words a story needs, such as 了, 吗, 家, 水, 听 and 打, and even at 300 introduced words the usable vocabulary was much thinner than the count suggested. The pool size also pulls the other way, since a model kept to a list of 60 words reliably, to 300 poorly and to 1,000 badly. A tiny known vocabulary therefore gives bad stories, and a large allowed list gives a model that strays from it.

## Retell a real story at fixed levels

A Japanese reading project starts from finished stories, so the plot already exists before any rewriting. It takes five ghost stories by Lafcadio Hearn and has a model retell each at all five levels of the JLPT, the Japanese proficiency test. Each level has its own cumulative list of allowed kanji, the Chinese characters used in Japanese, running from 79 kanji at the lowest level to 2,211 at the highest. Any word needing kanji beyond the level appears in the phonetic kana script instead. The project checks every retelling against open word lists for each level and compares it with the original for meaning. At every level the audit found no kanji outside that level's list. The share of kanji in the text rises from 9.0% at the lowest level to 31.2% at the highest, and the average sentence grows from 11.5 to 37.9 characters.

## Measure each story and rewrite it

A Spanish storybook project for children measures every story. It scores every story with a Spanish readability index plus a count of words per sentence for each age band, and it rewrites the stories that miss with specific feedback. Across 24 test requests, putting the child's age in the prompt hit the target 42% of the time, keeping the best of three drafts reached 58%, and measuring and rewriting reached 71%.

## Refold Grammar Primers

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Refold Grammar Primers

Grammar is learned by immersing and spending time with the language. The brain needs a great deal of time to process the patterns. A primer's goal is to expose those patterns for the first time and help the learner understand what is going on.

The learner should not treat anything in the primer as material to memorize or drill. Languages are large, and the patterns are too many and too dense to commit to memory in an efficient way. The patterns are learned by spending time with them in immersion and understanding the meaning behind them. Traditional study often treats grammar as rote memorization, like math. The approach here is to learn about grammar so comprehension rises, not to memorize rules for the sake of speaking.

## When to start

A recommended-knowledge section sits at the front of each primer. The main idea is to spend about 10 to 15 hours in the language, at least, before grammar study begins, and longer is fine. The learner should also know a good chunk of beginner words, maybe a couple hundred words, and have a quick way to look up words that appear in the primer. Vocabulary should not stand in the way, because the words matter more than the grammar. Grammar puts the words together.

At the time of the demonstration, there were primers for Korean, Japanese, Spanish, and German. More may appear later, and they all work the same way. Priming grammar, meaning working through a primer so later immersion is easier to understand, is the same kind of process no matter which language is being learned.

## How to read one

The primers are long for everyday reading and short for grammar primers. They do not need to be read in one sitting, and not everything in them needs to be understood on the first pass. If a section makes no sense, move on and come back later. Coming back every 50 hours of immersion, more or less, is a good interval. Another option is to read a little every day and cycle through the primer until the material feels solid. A daily stretch of 5 to 10 minutes is the size used once grammar priming has started. A first session studies one new grammar point and then looks for that point in the wild.

Each primer is ordered around what a learner is most likely to meet and have trouble with. The Spanish primer starts with verbs, because those cause many learners problems. The Japanese primer also starts with verbs, and specifically with the predicate, since that is among the largest differences from English. The German primer starts with nouns. The order is not perfect, and skipping around is allowed. The material is grouped in sections, so the learner can bounce between them based on what needs to be understood right then.

The primer should help the learner understand the language, not teach a pile of disconnected facts. When something in immersion does not make sense, that is a good time to look for it in the primer. After about 15 hours of Japanese immersion, sentences may feel as if something is missing, or as if they are random fragments. The primer explains that sentence fragments, which are treated as incorrect in English, are a normal way to talk in Japanese. The first reading will not make the point fully clear. Further immersion, and one or two later passes through the same section, will.

Spanish verbs have about 150 possible forms each. Those forms do not need to be memorized, or even known, at the beginning. A form that ends in "aba" may sound odd at first. It shows up more often when people tell stories about their lives, and the primer identifies it as another way to talk about the past, as in "I was speaking."

## What a primer is not for

After using a primer, the learner does not need to be able to produce the patterns or create correct grammar. That is not what the primers are for. They also do not cover everything, and that is on purpose. They get the learner in the door so that grammar can start to be acquired naturally during time with the language. The whole goal, for now, is to understand the language.

When the primer feels comfortable, the next step named in one of the guides is a full grammar reference. Search that reference whenever a grammar point in immersion is not yet understood. Each structure can have dozens of uses. The primer teaches the most common ones to get started. If an explanation does not cover a usage found in immersion, that is something to leave for later.

# YouTube Immersion Account

Immersing in content the learner finds interesting and compelling makes attention easier, because paying attention is easier when the content is enjoyable. That is why one setup step is to make a YouTube immersion account, then explore videos in the new language to find content that holds that attention.

Dialect choice comes next, because the account is only useful if the videos match the variety being learned. Choose content from the dialect being focused on. If no specific variety is the focus, choose the variety that offers the largest supply of videos. Keep playing the noticing game while watching, so the exploration is still high-attention work. The same session is also a time to try to figure out a learner type.

The time box keeps the search from turning into an endless setup task. Put in at least ten minutes of this exploration. If more time is available, do as much as feels right, without overdoing it. A session around this work is 20 minutes in total, with the other ten minutes going to the writing system, or to more time with the language once that system is comfortable.
