# The three-mode immersion system

The three-mode immersion system is a way to learn a language mainly from hours spent watching, listening to and reading real material, with study used only to make that material easier to follow. Its three modes are the kinds of time in a study day: preparation, input with lookup tools, and input with no tools. It sets the order of work over months and the shape of a single day, so a learner does not have to decide each morning what to study.

- Progress comes from time spent understanding the language, even partly.
- Rules and word lists crammed for a test fade within weeks.
- The order is reading, then listening, then speaking.
- Each day mixes preparation, input with tools and input without tools.
- Attention is required, and background listening does not count.
- A small daily minimum, tracked, keeps the habit through bad weeks.

## The phases

The work is split into stages, and each stage has one aim. At the start the aim is only to make the language stop sounding like noise, which means learning the sounds, the writing system and the commonest words. Then comes reading with audio, which aims at the first 5,000 words and the common grammar. Listening at natural speed comes after that, and speaking after listening, once the learner can follow most of what they read.

- Fundamentals: sounds, script, and the first few hundred words.
- Reading plus listening: vocabulary and grammar from text with audio.
- Listening: follow native speech at full speed without effort.
- Output: speaking and writing, built on a large store of input.

## A day in the system

A day has a few fixed blocks, and any extra time goes to input. Early days are about 20 minutes long, and after a month they are about 45 to 50. The blocks are kept small on purpose, so that the habit survives a bad week. The time splits into preparation, input with lookup tools and input with no tools.

- Writing system: about 10 minutes, until the script is comfortable.
- New words: about 10 a day, with 10 to 20 minutes of review.
- Review over 20 minutes a day means cut the new words.
- Grammar: 5 minutes with a grammar primer, one point a day.
- Input: 10 to 20 minutes or more, with full attention.

## Input that works

Input helps only when the learner understands part of it and is paying attention. Early on, when almost nothing is understood, the task is to catch any known word. Reading and listening at the same time helps while the sounds are still hard to separate. Tools that sit on top of a video let the learner look up a word, pause after each line or jump between subtitle lines.

- Pick content the learner actually wants to watch.
- Use text with audio while the sounds are hard to hear.
- Practise sitting with content that is mostly not understood.
- Raise comprehension with tools, techniques or easier content.
- Vary the speakers, topics and formats.

## Keeping it going

A language takes years, and motivation rises and falls over that time. The system relies on habits. A daily minimum is set small enough to meet on a bad day, a tracker records whether it was met, and a log of hours stands as proof of progress. A weekly review looks at what worked and what is limiting progress now.

- Set a daily minimum and track it.
- Log hours, and set goals as minutes spent each day.
- Estimate the total hours honestly before starting.
- Review weekly and change one thing.
