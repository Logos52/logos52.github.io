# The three-mode immersion system

The three-mode immersion system is a way of learning a language by spending many attentive hours with language the learner can partly follow. Sounds, words, grammar, and phrasing become automatic from that experience. Study about the language does not replace time spent in it.

The system has three moves that run together: large amounts of attentive time with the language; input made easier when a passage is still too hard to follow; and a small amount of preparation so the next session contains something the learner has already seen.

The learner is not only logging hours. The learner manages attention, difficulty, friction, and feedback. That management is what separates this system from the instruction "just immerse." It is also why the system fits with [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing|Deep Processing]] (noticing and comparing examples), [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation|Self-Regulation]] (diagnosing the current limiter), and [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management|Self-Management]] (daily minimums, friction, tracking).

## Core takeaways

- Language learning is mainly an acquisition problem. Implicit knowledge, built from attentive time with partly understood language, is what produces speech. Explicit study has a place as noticing, and it is not the main engine.
- A session counts only when the learner is paying attention. Background audio may build familiarity, but it is not counted as study time.
- Comprehension can be adjusted. Subtitles, transcripts, dictionaries, slower playback, familiar topics, and repeated content make the same material easier to understand.
- Three modes share the time. Preparation primes the learner, interactive immersion inspects input with tools, and freeflow immersion builds volume. Overusing any one mode creates a bottleneck.
- The system runs as a seven-step loop. Each week the learner checks which mode is getting too little time and changes the mix.
- The measure of progress is whether the same kind of content yields more gist this month than last month. Flashcard deck statistics are not the measure.

## Why time in the language comes before study about it

Beginners often collect vocabulary lists, memorize grammar, and translate from English. That sequence treats a language as a rule system to be completed before listening begins. [[wiki/Language/Language Isn't Math|Language Isn't Math]] is the mindset that rejects that sequence. Language learning is mainly an acquisition problem: implicit knowledge is what produces speech. Explicit work still has a place, as noticing. It is not the engine.

Five things happen once the method has started to work. Spoken sound that was unclear becomes recognizable speech. Marks on the page become recognizable writing. High-frequency words are understood without looking them up. Patterns are noticed inside real sentences. Understanding becomes direct instead of a word-by-word decoding. These five are things the learner experiences. They are not a rubric for scoring a week.

## Attention, noticing, and adjusting the input

A session counts only when the learner is paying attention. Background audio may increase familiarity, but it should not be counted as the session. Unattended speech is not worthless, because implicit statistical learning is not zero. It is still the wrong unit of study. [[wiki/Language/Attention is Important|Attention is Important]] covers that requirement in full.

The first problem for a beginner is staying engaged with material that is barely understood. [[wiki/Language/Noticing Game|Noticing Game]] gives the beginner a concrete thing to look for in the input, so attention has a target.

Comprehension can be adjusted. Subtitles, transcripts, pop-up dictionaries, prior viewing, slower playback, familiar topics, repeated content, and bilingual support can make the same material easier to understand. [[wiki/Language/Hacking Comprehension Menu|Hacking Comprehension Menu]] lists those adjustments.

[[wiki/Language/Preparation|Preparation]] is study done in advance so the next session is possible to enter. It supports immersion and does not replace it. Vocabulary, writing-system study, pronunciation, character awareness, and grammar primers are valuable because they make future input easier to understand. A small amount of preparation that opens up a passage is useful. Study that starts from lists and delays input is the problem. The main activity is meeting the language in use.

## The three modes and how they are balanced

[[wiki/Language/Three Pillars of Language Learning|Three Pillars of Language Learning]] is the central model, with three modes. Preparation primes the learner for input. Interactive immersion works through harder input with tools. Freeflow immersion builds volume and automatic understanding. Overusing any one mode creates a bottleneck. The learner balances the modes by giving time to whichever one is getting too little. That balancing rule is vault doctrine, not a published standard. The three modes are one problem of balance, not three separate problems.

Grammar study serves comprehension. It is not used as a formula for building sentences to speak. A pattern is studied briefly, then noticed in real input. A [[wiki/Language/Refold Grammar Primers|grammar primer]] is a short introduction to patterns, used so the learner can notice those patterns in input.

Language practice should use [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Interleaving for Complex Problem Solving|interleaving]]: varied speakers, topics, formats, comprehension supports, and small production attempts. The goal of that variety is the ability to work out meaning across changing contexts, rather than fluency with isolated vocabulary.

## The order of reading, listening, and speaking

Reading, listening, and output have a default order. First, make written and spoken language familiar. Then build comprehension. Then develop output from a large base of understood input. That order is a default, not the only path. A language where sound carries most of the difficulty may be learned by listening before reading. Vietnamese is the example given for that order. Early speech is allowed. Comprehension is still the base that speech stands on. Output can only be as good as the internal model that input has built. A learner with a small model produces output that is still translation from English. The order matters for that reason, and not because speech is forbidden.

The working rule is to rely on systems rather than on motivation. This is a rule of practice, not a research finding. Daily minimums, habit tracking, time tracking, friction reduction, and realistic expectations keep going during low-energy weeks. Research on motivation is mixed. A daily minimum is the part of the routine a low-energy week can still complete.

The default sequence has four stages. It is a description of the domain, not a syllabus. (1) Fundamentals: sounds, script, first words, attention. The goal of this stage is orientation, not mastery. (2) Reading: vocabulary, grammar comprehension, script familiarity. (3) Listening: understanding at native speed. (4) Output: speaking and writing from a comprehension base. Text paired with audio is preferred whenever it is available, and it is not a requirement. The tool class for paired text and audio is a dual-channel or subtitle player.

## The seven-step loop

In detail, the system is metacognitive control: choosing better input, sustaining attention, increasing comprehension, noticing patterns, and accumulating hours. That control runs as seven steps. The steps run as a loop. They are not a linear checklist.

1. Pick content that can hold attention.
2. Adjust it until some meaning is reachable.
3. Prepare: words, sounds, script, characters, grammar. Do this only so the next session is more comprehensible or more likely to happen.
4. Inspect the input closely with tools (interactive immersion).
5. Accumulate relaxed, attentive exposure (freeflow immersion).
6. Track time and daily minimums.
7. Reflect on bottlenecks and change the mix.

Common problems have set responses. When attention is lost, switch to content that holds attention, or to easier content. When a passage is still not understandable, add one comprehension aid. When a session is spent mostly on dictionary lookups, use simpler material, or repeat the same clip. When a week has been all flashcards and no input, the balance has gone wrong, and time in the language has to be restored.

Preparation, by this test, is any deliberate study that makes future input easier. One question decides whether an hour of preparation counted: does it make the next session more comprehensible or more likely to happen? Preparation that fails that test is a way of avoiding input.

[[wiki/Language/Interactive Immersion|Interactive Immersion]] is the mode where input is inspected with tools. [[wiki/Language/Freeflow Immersion|Freeflow Immersion]] is the mode that builds volume and automatic understanding. Both are chosen from inside this loop. They are not fixed stages that follow one another.

## What a session looks like

Interactive immersion uses real input, worked through closely. The learner pauses, looks words up, checks subtitles, compares lines, counts known and unknown words, confirms guesses, and works out meaning. Examples of interactive techniques: the Noticing Game; noticing with confirmation; intensive reading; sentence mining; intensive listening; transcription. [[wiki/Language/Immersion Metalayers|Immersion Metalayers]] covers subtitle and transcript tooling.

Freeflow immersion is attentive exposure without constant tool use. It is not passive listening. The content must hold the learner's focus and must give at least the gist.

A beginner who cannot yet follow much can still look for seven things: known words; cognates; proper names; repeated phrases; sounds that stand out; subtitles that match the audio; a grammar point studied recently. The emotional problem early on is feeling helpless. Having something to notice gives the beginner a way to make progress. Noticing is the language-learning form of [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Aim|Aim]]: write down what the material must answer before opening it.

The learner can adjust tools, techniques, or content. The practical question is: what one change would make this input more understandable while keeping the experience in the target language? [[wiki/Dimensions/Mindset/Marginal Gains|Marginal Gains]] is the page about making the smallest useful adjustment. No percentage of comprehension is set as a target. Any number given would be a house estimate by feel, not a research finding.

The tools are described as classes, not as products: an SRS; a popup dictionary or subtitle inspector; a dual-channel player. No specific brand is required.

## Scripts and characters

For phonetic writing systems, learning the script early gives access to subtitles, transcripts, dictionaries, and search. For Vietnamese, the Latin script is an advantage. Tone marks, sound distinctions, spelling, and the mapping from writing to sound still have to be trained. The early bottleneck in Vietnamese is likely hearing the sound distinctions and recognizing words by their tones. That bottleneck is why Vietnamese is learned listening-first. The [[wiki/Language/Vietnamese Grammar Primer|Vietnamese Grammar Primer]] is the language-specific primer for the noticing part of that work.

For character languages, characters become readable once the learner sees them as recurring structures with components, meanings, sounds, and uses that depend on context. [[wiki/Language/Character Primer|Character Primer]] is the introduction. The learner does not need to become an etymology specialist. Five recognitions are enough: characters are units that build words; context decides meaning and pronunciation; semantic components hint at meaning; phonetic components hint at sound; character recognition is built by vocabulary priming plus repeated reading.

## Grammar, flashcards, and tracking

The grammar workflow: study one small pattern briefly; do not try to master it in the abstract; look for it in immersion; let repeated examples train recognition; return to explanations when input raises a question.

The best role for an SRS: high-frequency items in the first weeks; forms studied ahead of a session; review of words already met in a video or a page; support for character recognition; reviews kept short enough that they do not take time from the immersion block. Deck statistics are not the measure of progress. The measure is whether the same kind of content yields more gist this month than last month.

Useful tracking questions:

- How much time actually ran today?
- Was attention on?
- Which of the three modes took the hour?
- Which change made meaning easier to reach?
- Where did the session snag?
- Which clip or page made continuing feel cheap?
- What is the smallest change for tomorrow?

Those questions follow [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management/Kolbs Experiential Cycle|Kolbs Experiential Cycle]]: after an attempt, record what happened, how it felt and why, what rule that suggests, and what changes next time.

Two worked cases use the same standard. Vietnamese: audio, tones, phrases, a [[wiki/Language/YouTube Immersion Account|YouTube Immersion Account]] as the content supply, and a lightweight primer. Mandarin: characters, reading with audio, script conversion, subtitle tooling, and character components. The standard in both is compelling content, made a little more comprehensible each week.

## How this fits the wider learning system

[[wiki/Syntheses/ICS System|ICS System]] is the general learning system: a five-part diagnosis of the learner plus a loop for fixing the current limiter. The acronym names no provider. That page explains how to learn deliberately. The three-mode immersion system is the version of that system for language.

The dimensions from that general system, with the part of language learning each one covers: Deep Processing (noticing); [[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval|Retrieval]] (vocabulary, characters, matching audio to words); Self-Regulation (the current limiter); Self-Management (minimums); [[wiki/Dimensions/Mindset|Mindset]] (ambiguity and partial understanding).

Five mistaken assumptions to check a week against:

- Every word has to land or the session failed.
- A finished grammar is the gate to listening.
- Unattended audio logs as study.
- Harder is always the upgrade.
- Speaking should start before a large input base exists.

Content sources are listed at [[wiki/Resources/Vietnamese Language Learning Resources|Vietnamese Language Learning Resources]] and [[wiki/Resources/Mandarin Chinese Language Learning Resources|Mandarin Chinese Language Learning Resources]].

The division into three modes is vault doctrine, not a published standard. The four-stage sequence is a default, and listening-first is an active variant. The method costs many attentive hours. A learner who wants a speaking course from week one will not stay with it. The cost is daily attentive time plus a minimum that a low-energy week can still meet. Signs that the method is not being followed: a week of all preparation and no input; all background audio logged as study; output that is still translation because a large input base was never built. The expectation that can be checked: the same kind of content yields more gist this month than last, and at least one mode is getting enough time.

The measure of all those hours is increasing comprehension of real content. The mix of modes keeps serving that measure because the learner runs a loop and changes the mix, instead of working through a fixed checklist.

## How to practice this

1. Pick content that can hold your attention. Notice whether you stay engaged or your attention drifts. If it drifts, switch to something that holds, or something easier.
2. Adjust the input until some meaning is reachable. Add one aid, such as subtitles, a transcript, slower playback, or a repeated clip. Notice whether you can now get at least the gist.
3. Prepare words, sounds, script, characters, or grammar only for the next session. Notice whether the next session is more comprehensible or more likely to happen. If it is not, the preparation was avoidance.
4. Inspect part of the input closely: pause, look words up, compare lines, confirm guesses. Notice how many words in the clip you know and do not know. If lookups take most of the session, use simpler material or repeat the same clip.
5. Spend the rest of the block on relaxed, attentive exposure without constant tool use. Notice whether the content holds your focus and yields at least the gist. If it does not, pick easier content or add one aid.
6. Track your time and daily minimum, then note where the session snagged and which mode took the hour. Notice whether the same kind of content gives more gist this month than last. Change one thing in the mix for tomorrow.
