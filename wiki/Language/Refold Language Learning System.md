# Refold Language Learning System

The Refold method treats time spent attentively in the target language as the core of progress. The course that teaches it covers the first two phases—fundamentals, then reading plus listening—while you balance the three pillars named on [[wiki/Language/Three Pillars of Language Learning|Three Pillars of Language Learning]]: preparation, interactive immersion, and freeflow.

During fundamentals you are still making the language stop sounding like noise. In the reading-plus-listening phase you gain comprehension and pick up the first several thousand words by reading text paired with audio whenever possible. Homework assignments are a baseline; extra time belongs in activities where you actively understand the language, even a little. The lessons give specific instructions to remove decisions, but you still adapt what does not fit your circumstances.

Someone who is not a total beginner should move through the first week quickly, with a recap within a day or two instead of a boring full week on basics already known elsewhere. Staying on the same day twice is allowed. Some people run the course twice: the first pass absorbs what it can, the second focuses on what did not make sense before.

## Attention and the noticing game

Building instinct for a new language takes a great deal of high-attention immersion. Background listening does not count. The practice that makes attention concrete is the **noticing game**: while in the language, look for cognates, words already known, or words from a word list or bingo card. If those lists run out, notice items from preparation that day, or use additional word lists. After grammar study, point the search at a pattern you primed and look for it in the wild.

A first session may be short on purpose—about twenty minutes total, often split between writing-system work and time in the language on noticing—so the habit starts before the language is understood.

## Preparation

Preparation is studying on purpose so immersion yields more understanding. Main forms are learning the writing system, priming frequent vocabulary with spaced repetition, and reading grammar primers. Each aims at comprehension, not memorizing rules for speaking. Preparation works when its words and patterns start turning up in real content. It fails when it crowds out immersion, starts too early, or turns into drilling.

Learn the writing system until a phonetic script feels comfortable, then shift that block to more time in the language. Vocabulary priming adds about ten new frequent words a day; if reviews exceed about twenty minutes, add fewer new words and give the saved time to immersion. Grammar primers fit after roughly ten to fifteen hours of immersion and a few hundred beginner words, with a quick way to look up unknowns. Read primers for understanding, about five to ten minutes a day, one point at a time, then look for that point during noticing.

**Grammar primers** expose patterns for the first time; they are not drill books. Languages hold too many dense patterns to memorize efficiently. Do not expect to produce grammar correctly after reading a primer. When something in immersion confuses you, look it up in the primer. If a section makes no sense, move on and return after more immersion—every fifty hours is a workable interval.

## Interactive immersion

Interactive immersion expands understanding through tools and hands-on work on **dual-channel input**: content you read and hear at the same time. A session often includes about twenty minutes in the language on that content.

**Intensive reading** looks for two-target sentences: sentences where you need to look up two pieces, not every word. If no such content appears, fall back to the noticing game. Ten minutes of work may cover only a few minutes of video runtime because the work is looking up, not watching.

**Immersion metalayers** add lookup, pause, and subtitle tools on top of a show. A common setup uses a browser layer on video sites: target-language subtitles, translations hidden until you hover, autopause after each line, and line-by-line navigation. After setup, play the noticing game on read-and-listen content.

**Hacking comprehension** combines tools on the media, techniques you apply, and content choices. Tools include matching target-language subtitles, popup dictionaries, automatic pausing, and blurred native translations for checking meaning. Techniques include priming before a session, slowing playback (not below about eighty percent), rewinding, lookups, and spoiling the plot so context carries more load. Content choices include reusing material you know from your first language or from earlier immersion, topics you already understand, simpler plots, child-oriented material, and rich non-verbal cues.

Set up a **YouTube immersion account** in the target dialect, explore until content holds attention, keep playing the noticing game, and time-box setup so it does not become endless configuration—at least ten minutes of exploration per session is a floor.

## Freeflow immersion

Freeflow reuses content already used interactively. Watch or read without lookups or rewinding, stay attentive, and note gist, interest, fun, and focus. If the gist comes through and some parts stay unclear, keep immersing. Re-watching after intensive work or after seeing something in your first language makes freeflow easier. A typical freeflow block might be about twenty minutes inside a longer session. Freeflow does not replace intensive work; without attention it has no value.

## Language is not math

Most language programs treat vocabulary plus grammar rules as enough to translate any thought. Human speech is highly specific in ways no one can predict. English says "play the piano"; Japanese says you pluck it. Set phrases are arbitrary: "Can I get your number?" does not map word-for-word into natural Japanese. Translating from a first language with rules produces speech that ranges from funny to unintelligible.

Languages also express different ideas. Japanese has words such as instant refusal without hesitation, or anger from the person at fault, that English rarely names in one phrase. To sound natural you either already know the form natives use or you are guessing—and guesses fail most of the time. Plenty of room remains for poetry and style after you know the defaults; creativity before that sounds odd or fails entirely. Primers and immersion exist so you hear and notice real forms, not so you generate them from tables.

## Aim–Shoot–Skin for languages

The general study method Aim–Shoot–Skin adapts cleanly to languages. **Aim** means material just above your edge—about half the words unknown but gist still reachable—and genuine interest, spun into variations of what you would want to say. **Shoot** fills gaps with lookups and heavy use of new words in all modes, with flashcards as support, not as the whole job. **Skin**, optional, hunts patterns such as shared roots or character components to memorize less; it pays off most for scripts like Chinese and links to [[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|How Chinese Characters Work]] and [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Bear Hunter System|Bear Hunter System]].

## Graded input and tooling

AI-generated stories locked to a learner's word list are an experiment some builders tried for Mandarin and other languages. Models stray from vocabulary ceilings, so useful designs use code to validate or retell existing stories at fixed levels. Early vocabularies too small to support plot are a structural limit; see [[wiki/Story Craft/Story Under a Vocabulary Ceiling|Story Under a Vocabulary Ceiling]] for the writer's side of the same problem. [[wiki/Learning Craft/Don't Outsource the Learning|Don't Outsource the Learning]] applies here too: the model may draft, but attention and noticing still do the acquisition work.
