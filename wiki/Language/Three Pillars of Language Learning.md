# Three Pillars of Language Learning

Language study time splits three ways, and each session picks one of the three by how much of its content already lands. Two of the ways are immersion: time spent with material made for native speakers rather than material made for learners. The word excludes audio playing in another room while attention sits elsewhere. It has also never meant living abroad. The third way is preparation, and it is the only one of the three that happens away from real content.

Three names do not mean three problems, though. Preparation stands apart because it is the one activity producing no exposure at all. The other two run on identical material at different tool settings, which the standard freeflow move makes plain: content already worked through with subtitles and lookups gets rerun later with neither of them on. So a session carries two decisions rather than three. How much time goes to study away from content is the first. Where the tool dial sits on the content itself is the second, and that one gets decided by counting.

## Counting the Unknowns

**The dial is set from a count of the words that do not resolve, and the count takes about half a minute.**

One dense stretch of roughly a hundred words is enough: a screen of subtitles or a paragraph of a novel, read through with a tally of everything that fails to come through. Reading research supplies the edges of the scale. Comfortable unassisted comprehension sits near 98% known words, and around 95% is the floor where comprehension still holds up with support. Below that floor the aids stop paying their way: subtitles, a popup dictionary, slowed playback and a plot known in advance all raise how much of a piece lands, and none of them rescues material running ten unknowns deep in every hundred words.

| Unknown words per hundred | What the session is | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–2 | followed at full speed | tools come off, the session runs freeflow |
| around 5 | followable with support | subtitles, a small lookup budget, one more pass |
| 10 or more | under the comprehension floor | different content, or groundwork before this content reopens |

```text
Paragraph of a novel, hundred words counted off: eleven do not resolve.
Eleven sits under the floor, so this is not a session — it is a lookup exercise.
Same author, a chapter already read in translation: four unknowns per hundred.
Four routes to interactive — audiobook subtitles on, four lookups, one pass.
```

The count reports on one stretch of one piece of content, so it gets rerun whenever the content changes. A denser chapter of the same book can land two rows lower than the one before it.

## What Priming Actually Buys

**Priming pays over months and is unreliable tonight.**

Loading a small set of words, sounds, characters or a single grammar pattern shortly before input, aimed at recognition rather than at recall, is what priming means here. Over hundreds of hours its payoff is not in dispute, and the coverage figures above are the shape of it, since a larger known vocabulary means more of any given page arriving intact. Over one evening the effect thins out badly. Pre-teaching a word list before a listening session reliably raises knowledge of those words and raises learner confidence, and it often leaves comprehension of that session exactly where it was. In a direct comparison, knowing the topic of the passage beforehand plus a second listen beat the vocabulary support.

That result reorders the levers for a session already going wrong. The content dial is the cheaper one: a plot summary read first, or a rewatch of something already known in a first language, moves comprehension further than an hour spent building a deck for it.

Preparation is also the mode that feels most like progress while it is happening, which is why it runs under a cap rather than under judgment. The field's standard allocation splits study four ways, roughly a quarter each to language-focused study, meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output and fluency work. The daily shape this model inherited is smaller and harder-edged: ten minutes on the writing system, fifteen on vocabulary priming, twenty inside the language, forty-five in total, with priming capped so any spare time falls through to input. Either anchor turns yesterday into something checkable against a number.

## Where the Hours Come From

**Freeflow is the mode that carries volume.**

A session halting at every unknown word contains fewer minutes of language than one running straight through, and the hours have to come from somewhere. The measured version of the argument comes from extensive reading, the same low-friction shape applied to text: across 34 studies and 3,942 participants, d = 0.71 from before to after and d = 0.46 against controls, covering reading speed, comprehension, vocabulary and grammar together. What accumulates over that volume is what this model calls instinct, meaning arriving with no translation step in between.

The failure named against freeflow is content playing while attention sits elsewhere, then logged afterwards as immersion. Stated as an absolute the rule overshoots. Attention behaves as a multiplier on what exposure is worth rather than as a switch. The claim that acquisition requires it has been contested for decades. Adults who heard non-native tone contrasts for eight hours across four days, attention elsewhere throughout, afterwards showed faster and larger neural responses to those contrasts. Sound-level familiarity is close to all that unattended exposure buys, enough to earn background audio a small slot labelled as phonology and to keep it out of the immersion total.

## Choosing What Changes Next

Balancing by bottleneck is this vault's own working rule and carries no research behind it. Whichever single thing is limiting sessions now takes the next change: comprehension, attention, or the raw hours going in. No field construct matching it was located, so its whole support is the general case for adjusting a method while the method is still running.

It also disagrees with the quarter-each allocation. A week of preparation and nothing else is legal under a bottleneck rule when opacity is killing every session, and illegal under fixed proportions. The reconciliation used here lets the bottleneck choose what changes next, under a cap that stops the exception from becoming the schedule.

Where attention is the bottleneck, the first move is a content change rather than a harder technique: a shorter session, or content already seen once and carrying its own visual support. That ordering is untested, and its nearest evidence is the comparison already cited, where knowing the content beforehand beat the vocabulary work.

The old worry about looking things up too much belongs in the same category of unproven house rules. No study locates a lookup density at which the flow of a language breaks. The nearest evidence runs the other way, since glossed reading is lookup made frictionless, and it produces roughly 45% word learning against 27% for unglossed reading immediately afterwards while improving comprehension of the text rather than degrading it. What survives is a rule about how a session ends: the lookup budget worth setting is the one that keeps the session running to its last line.

## The Case Against

The lineage here is comprehensible-input pedagogy, whose central formulation — content pitched one step above the current level — has never been given an operational definition. That absence is the standing reason for calling it untestable, and a recent critique presses further, arguing the absorption framing sits badly against evidence that acquisition is active, interactive and multimodal. Nothing above rests on that hypothesis. The measured results are what hold the model up.

The reader it fails is the one who has to hold a conversation in three months, because every hour this model routes goes into input. The price is a decision before each session, the half-minute count, and a record of where the hours actually went, without which the balancing half cannot run at all. The larger cost is that labelling becomes an activity in itself: an hour of subtitled video with four lookups is interactive and freeflow at once, and forcing it into one box can take more than the routing gives back.

Those costs are worth paying only while the routing changes something. Two weeks of counting that leaves every session looking the same means the model has turned into decoration, and the time goes back to plain input. The promise that can fail is re-entry. Content worked through interactively should be followable at full speed within a session or two of that first pass, and a second pass still needing the tools means the first pass was tool operation rather than comprehension.

## Nothing Here Produces a Sentence

The model is input only and never admits it. No mode in it produces a spoken or written sentence, while the standard four-way allocation gives meaning-focused output a full quarter of study time. The omission is inherited rather than chosen, since the material these three were compressed from defers production past its own horizon. A year can pass under this model with nothing produced and nothing in it raising a flag, which leaves a date on the calendar as the only guard.

So the split that looked like three separate practices is one routing rule with a number at each edge. An evening's content gets counted, and the count names the tool setting for that evening. The study half stays under a ceiling, where it cannot quietly take the hours the content needs. Production is the one thing the count never routes to, so its start date has to be set from outside this model.
