# Language Isn't Math

Most language programs rest on a skill-building model that makes no distinction between learning and acquisition, and treats the two as the same thing. The learner first studies vocabulary and grammar rules on purpose, then practices using those rules and words to speak. With enough practice, speaking will become intuitive and natural, the way a first language already is. In practice the model does not get people to high levels in a foreign language.

The first problem is an assumption inside the model. The model treats a human language as vocabulary plus grammar rules. If that were true, memorizing the relevant words and rules would let a person translate any thought into the target language. That cannot work, because human speech is highly specific in ways no one can predict.

## The same ideas, said differently

Languages give the same basic ideas different shapes, each language in its own way. The examples here are English and Japanese, though the same principles apply to other languages as well.

English speakers say they "play the piano." Japanese speakers say *piano o hiku*, which means they "pluck the piano," because a piano has strings. There is no deeper reason for the difference. English speakers say "I'm hungry." Japanese speakers say *onaka suita*, "my stomach has become empty." An English speaker who cannot find a phone asks "Have you seen my phone?" A Japanese speaker in the same situation asks *keitai shiranai*, "Do you know my phone?" That wording sounds strange in English, and in Japanese it is understood as asking whether the other person knows where the phone is right now.

## Set phrases

Native speakers also express common ideas in arbitrary set phrases. An English speaker says "I need to go to the bathroom," those exact words, many times over a life. A Japanese speaker says *toire ikitai*, "I want to go to the bathroom." It is possible, in Japanese, to say the wording that matches "I need to go to the bathroom," and it sounds unnatural. The reverse is also true. English speakers can say "I want to go to the bathroom," yet that is not how they usually say it.

Asking for a phone number works the same way. English has the set phrase "Can I get your number?" Japanese has *denwa bango oshiete*, "Can you teach me your phone number?" There is no deeper reason for either wording. Native speakers almost always express these ideas the same way, using those same words in that same order every time. It is not a creative process. It is set in stone.

## What translation produces

One speaking strategy is to think in a first language, then use memorized grammar rules and vocabulary to translate the thought. The best case is that the speaker is still understood and sounds odd or funny. The worst case is that the speaker is not understood at all.

A Japanese speaker thinking in Japanese and translating into English with memorized rules might say "Can you teach me your phone number?" A listener would probably still understand, even though it sounds a little funny. The same speaker might say "Do you know my phone?" when the English phrase is "Have you seen my phone?" A listener may not be able to tell what is being asked, because English does not express the idea that way.

## Ideas one language has and another does not

Different wording for one shared idea is not the whole problem. Different languages also express different ideas in the first place. Japanese has many words with no English equivalent. They name things Japanese speakers say often, and that English speakers do not say.

*Sokudou* means "instant response." If a person offers a date and hears no at once, a Japanese speaker might say *sokudou*. The word marks that the other person did not even think about it. The refusal came so quickly that it showed complete confidence.

*Gyakugire* means "reverse anger." It names the moment when the person at fault becomes angry anyway, as a way to defend themselves. If someone is told not to eat a yogurt in the fridge, eats it anyway, and then gets angry at the owner for having put the yogurt there, that anger is *gyakugire*. The other person can name it directly. Japanese speakers simply say different things than English speakers tend to say.

## Imitate or guess

Native speech has two properties that matter together. It is set in stone, meaning native speakers usually repeat the same ideas in the same forms. It is also highly specific in unpredictable ways. Abstraction does not get a learner very far. To express an idea in a way that will sound natural to native speakers, the speaker has to already know the precise form natives usually choose for that idea. To speak like a native, the speaker also has to know which kinds of ideas natives usually voice to begin with.

Either the speaker already knows how natives tend to speak and can imitate that, or the speaker does not and is making blind guesses based on a first language. Those guesses fail in a large majority of cases.

Language is not like math, where a formula can deduce the answer. Either the speaker already knows the answer ahead of time, or the speaker does not. There is no space for guessing or for inventing a wording on the fly.

Plenty of room remains to be clever and inventive in language use, or poetry would not exist. The idea is to first get a firm grip on how the language is supposed to work, then later bend those patterns the way an artist would. Getting creative with language before the speaker has mastered the target language sounds weird and unnatural at best, and is not understood at all at worst.
