# Language Isn't Math

Many language courses teach vocabulary and grammar rules and expect the learner to build sentences from them, the way a student works a math problem with a formula. Sentences built that way are often correct by the rules and still sound wrong, or are not understood at all, because native speakers say most things in fixed ways that no rule predicts. A learner who wants to sound natural has to hear how native speakers say a thing before trying to say it.

- Vocabulary plus grammar rules does not produce natural speech.
- Languages say the same idea in different ways that no rule predicts.
- Native speakers repeat fixed phrases word for word, every time.
- Some ideas are common in one language and missing in another.
- Translating from your own language sounds odd or fails.
- Study grammar to understand what you hear and read.

## Why rules fall short

The usual course follows a skill-building model: memorise words and rules, practise building sentences, and expect speaking to become natural with enough practice. The model treats learning about a language and acquiring it as the same thing. It assumes a language reduces to words and rules. Each language picks its own way to say even simple things, those choices follow no rule, and they have to be known in advance.

- English says "play the piano" and Japanese says "pluck the piano".
- English says "I'm hungry" and Japanese says "my stomach has become empty".
- English asks "have you seen my phone?", Japanese "do you know my phone?"

## Fixed phrases

Native speakers express common ideas in the same words, in the same order, almost every time. English speakers say "I need to go to the bathroom", and Japanese speakers say the equivalent of "I want to go to the bathroom". Each language can say the other version, and it sounds unnatural. A learner translating word by word will often pick the version nobody says.

- Japanese asks for a phone number as "teach me your phone number".
- Translated word for word into English, that sounds odd.
- "Do you know my phone?" in English is not understood at all.

## Ideas one language has

Languages also differ in what people talk about at all. Japanese has a common word, sokudou, for an answer given instantly and without thought, often said when someone refuses at once. It has another, gyakugire, for getting angry when you are the one in the wrong. English speakers rarely express either idea, so a learner cannot translate their way to these words and has to meet them in use.

## What to do instead

Build a large store of how native speakers say things by spending many hours with content in the language, and treat grammar study as help for understanding that content. Grammar notes help you notice patterns in what you hear and check your own output. Creative use of the language, changing set phrases on purpose, works once you know the normal phrases well.

- Listen and read a lot before trying to speak much.
- Start grammar after roughly 10 to 15 hours of listening and reading.
- Read grammar notes to understand, without drills or memorising.
- When a sentence confuses you, look up its pattern.
