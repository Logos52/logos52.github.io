# Sound Components

A sound component is a prediction tool. Early writing was largely picture-like, and only so many words can be drawn. The more abstract a meaning is, the harder it is to depict. One answer was to add a mark, such as a mark at the bottom of a tree to point at the root. Another answer was the rebus principle. Take an existing character and use it for its sound to write another word.

A rebus does not always match the target word exactly. Context fills the gaps. An English picture of an ear can stand in "Oh, here you are," even though "ear" has no H. Writing systems are built for people who already know the spoken language.

The same thing happened with these characters. 北 first meant a person's back. Back and north sounded alike, and north is hard to draw, so the back character was borrowed to write north. Later a body component was added to write 背, "back," and 北 was kept for north. The two words have different tones today. The older language most likely did not have tones, so there is usually little or no tonal match between a character and its sound component. Tone comes from audio.

Adding a semantic piece to 北 to make 背 signals that the word sounds like north and has to do with the body. An English parallel is adding a letter to show that the word is "here" rather than "ear" or "year." In 背, 北 does two jobs at once. It supplies both meaning and sound.

## What a sound component can predict

Characters that share a sound component do not necessarily share a pronunciation. They may not even share an obvious modern connection. 尚, read shàng, appears in 黨 dǎng, 堂 táng, and 躺 tǎng. 占 zhān pairs with 店 diàn. Those patterns are themselves something to learn.

A common claim is that sound components are not helpful because they cannot guess a pronunciation with complete accuracy. That looks at the problem from the wrong direction. Once the way sound can vary is understood, the components become useful. The working drill is to guess a range, not an exact hit. Coming across 樘, a learner who knows that 木 is often semantic and 尚 is often a sound component can predict two things. The sense is likely tied to trees or wood. The pronunciation is probably one of shang, chang, zhang, tang, or dang. Lookup then gives the exact reading and sense. The tone would not have been knowable from the component alone.

English spelling has the same limit. A person cannot look at an unfamiliar English word and be sure of its pronunciation from the letters alone.

The habit to build is simple. On any unknown character, predict meaning and pronunciation before the dictionary opens.

## Open questions

- How does guessing a range of readings, then looking the character up, change what the next shared sound component feels like?
- How does expecting the tone of 北 to carry into 背 change whether the sound system still looks trustworthy?
