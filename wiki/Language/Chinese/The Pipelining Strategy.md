# The Pipelining Strategy

Do not learn form, meaning, and sound on the same day. A cohort of three to five characters moves through a three-day cycle. On the first day, recall the words in one direction. On the second day, recall them in the reverse direction, which is much easier. On the third day, attach the written form to a word that is now overlearned.

Cohorts overlap, the way loads of laundry overlap in a wash cycle. Each day then pairs one new task with two already-known ones. The point of the stagger is to create the advantage a native writer already has. Writing the form is easier once the spoken word is fully known.

Each pronunciation-meaning pair gets its own card. 道 read dào and 道 read dǎo are separate. 那 read nà and 那 read nèi are separate. Merging those cards later is allowed as an experiment.

A day's intake is three to five new characters plus common carrier words. Those carrier words combine the new characters with known ones, up to about 20 items a day. That mix is how the bulk of a vocabulary was built in the account that teaches this method. A delayed-form variant is also allowed. Hold off for a month or two after the spoken word is learned, then learn the written form.

Pipelining is the layer for new items, placed on top of spaced repetition. It does not replace daily reviews. Those reviews continue.

## Open questions

- How does saving the written form until the third day, when the word is already overlearned, change how the form sticks?
- How does introducing new characters without carrier words that reuse known ones change what a day can hold?
