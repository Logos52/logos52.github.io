# The IME Method

The IME method is a way to bring back a Chinese character you have forgotten by doing in your head what a Chinese keyboard does. On a computer, the input method editor, or IME, takes a typed sound and shows a short list of characters with that sound, and the typist picks the right one by sight. The method builds the same list from memory, out of the word's sound and meaning, so a learner can write or read the character without a dictionary.

- Chinese characters are built from parts that hint at sound or meaning.
- Start from the sound and meaning of the word you want.
- List the parts that could give that sound.
- List the parts that could give that meaning.
- Pair them until one pairing looks familiar.
- Picking a character by sight is easier than writing it cold.

## How it works

Most Chinese characters are made of smaller parts. In a given character a part usually does one job, either hinting at the sound or hinting at the meaning. A learner who knows which parts tend to give which sounds, and which parts tend to give which meanings, holds two lists of candidates. Crossing the two lists leaves a few possible characters, and recognition picks the right one, the same way a typist picks from the keyboard's list. The method works with as few as about fifty known characters.

- Step 1: say the word and list the sound parts that fit.
- Step 2: list the meaning parts that fit what it means.
- Step 3: pair them until one character looks right.
- Unsure where a part goes: write both versions and choose by sight.

```
the word's sound          the word's meaning
       |                          |
       v                          v
  sound parts                meaning parts
  that fit                   that fit
          \                  /
           v                v
           a few candidates
                  |
                  v
         recognition picks one
```

## Building the sound list

The sound list takes the most practice. Modern Mandarin has moved away from the sounds the characters were built on, so a sound part gives a range of related syllables. Consonants that start a syllable and are made in the same place in the mouth, such as b, p and f, often swap within one family. Tones rarely carry over from the part to the character. Tone changes in speech, on a third tone, on 一 and on 不, also hide the dictionary tone, so a heard syllable is searched across every tone.

- Keep a list from each syllable to the parts that give it.
- Widen the search to related consonants, such as b, p and f.
- No part comes to mind: recall other characters with that sound.

## Running it backwards to read

The same two lists help while reading, when a character is new or forgotten. Decide which part probably gives the sound and which gives the meaning, and let the two narrow the answer. In 臉盆, washbasin, the meaning part 皿 and the words around it point to a container, and the sound part 分 points to fen, ben or pen. A guess of pén can then be checked, and making the guess before checking is the moment the character gets stored.

- Some parts nearly always give the sound, such as 各.
- Some nearly always give the meaning, such as 穴.
- Some switch: 立 is meaning in 站, sound in 拉, both in 位.
- Guess first, then look it up.
- Look a word up only after meeting it about three times.
