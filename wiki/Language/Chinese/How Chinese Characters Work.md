# How Chinese Characters Work

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

A Chinese character exists to record speech, so each one ties a written form to a spoken word's sound and meaning. The link from form to sound and the link from form to meaning each have to be learned and tested on their own. The form divides into functional components, and each component does one of four jobs in a given character. Form components use a picture to express meaning, meaning components carry an abstract extension of an original sense, sound components point at a range of pronunciations, and empty components do neither. Each character fixes its components' jobs for itself, so the same shape can give meaning in one character and sound in another. Reading a character by the jobs of its parts lets a learner predict its meaning and its sound range and rebuild a half-forgotten form. New characters are then learned in a staggered cycle anchored to words already known by ear.

## What the parts do

A character is one memory item with three equal sides, which are form, meaning and sound. [[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|Three Attributes of a Character]] rejects the view of a character as an ideograph that runs from form straight to meaning, because what a character does is bring the spoken word to mind. The form-to-sound edge and the form-to-meaning edge each need their own encoding and their own testing. A new character is best tied to a word you can already recognise when you hear it.

The form then divides into functional components, meaning parts that do real work in that character, most often by supplying a pronunciation or a meaning. The breakdown stops at that level. 部 breaks into 咅 plus 阝, and cutting deeper produces noise, because a memory story built on parts that explain nothing does not help.

A component's job belongs to the character it sits in, not to its shape. In 端 the shape 立 carries meaning, in 拉 it carries sound, and in 音 it is empty. 大 behaves the same way, picturing its sense in 美, lending meaning in 尖 and lending sound in 达. In 达 it does two jobs at once. The core skill is naming the job a component does in the character at hand.

The four jobs split into two semantic types and two others. Form components express meaning through what they picture, and meaning components through a meaning they have acquired, so the two together go by the name semantic components. Sound components supply a sound, while empty components supply no sound and no meaning. Radicals are a separate matter, since a radical tells a reader where to find a character in a dictionary and does not reliably say what the part is doing.

## Character Primer

# Character Primer

Chinese and Japanese do not build words by combining letters from an alphabet. They use characters. Characters are like pictures that represent a meaning. Drawing a realistic picture of everything would take too long, so the old forms are picture-like symbols rather than detailed drawings. Some characters resemble the meaning they stand for. Most do not.

Examples include 火 for fire, 山 for mountain, 牛 for cow, and 肉 for meat or flesh. Each of those characters is also a word by itself. Not every character is a word. Some never appear alone and only form a word when combined with other characters.

Words are usually one or two characters long, and sometimes three or four characters long. Two-character examples that use the characters above are 火山, fire plus mountain, meaning volcano, and 牛肉, cow plus meat, meaning beef. The meaning of a multi-character word is not always predictable in that way. It is common to meet a new word made of familiar characters and still need to look it up.

## Several meanings, several readings

A single character can appear in many words, so it can carry many meanings. A very common character may show more than ten meanings in a popup dictionary. Characters can also have more than one pronunciation. That is especially common in Japanese, which uses fewer characters than Chinese and so uses the ones it has in more words. Context decides which meaning and which pronunciation apply.

The Chinese pair 马上 is a compact case. 马 means horse. 上 means up, on, or above. Together, the two characters can mean "immediately," as one word. They can also be two words meaning that something is located above a horse. They can also be one word meaning "on horseback." Which of the three meanings applies depends on the surrounding sentence.

Sometimes even context is not enough. The learner may know every character in a sentence and still not understand the sentence. It is best not to dwell too long. Give the stretch a serious attempt, then let it go if it stays unclear. More reading experience makes these calls easier.

In Chinese, some characters are also used for grammar. The most common example is 了, which has many uses learned over time. Other common examples are 到, 上, 下, and 来. In Japanese, words are often formed by combining kanji with kana. The kana portion can show which meaning of the character is intended, and it can make the boundaries between words clearer.

## Components, not radicals

All characters are formed from what English calls radicals. There are 214 radicals, and almost every character can be broken into those pieces. Studying the radicals themselves is not particularly helpful.

Components are the useful pieces. They make up most of the characters people meet in Chinese, and about 60 percent of the ones people meet in Japanese. A component can hint at a character's pronunciation or its meaning. Some components are also radicals, or are characters on their own.

A semantic component hints at meaning. It usually sits on the left or the top of the character, though not always. Recognizing it can help the learner guess a new character's meaning, or remember a meaning already met. It does not always give a useful hint.

The character 水, water, often changes to 氵 when it appears as a semantic component. That kind of shape change is common. Characters that contain it include 河 for river, 湖 for lake, 波 for wave, 池 for pond, and 海 for ocean or sea. An unfamiliar character with the water component has a good chance of having something to do with water. The hint is sometimes metaphorical. 口 means mouth. As a component it often has to do with mouths, as in 吃, to eat. It can also have to do with speaking or with openings, as in 叫, to shout, and 洞, hole.

A phonetic component hints at pronunciation. It usually sits on the right-hand side, though not always. Recognizing it can help the learner guess or remember how a character is said. Specific phonetic components are not something to study the way semantic components are. The useful habit is to stay on the lookout for pronunciation patterns.

## How characters are remembered

The recommended way to learn characters is to prime them with a vocabulary deck and then get a great deal of immersion, so the same characters appear again and again in the real world. The more often a character is seen and its meaning and pronunciation are recalled, the easier it becomes to remember.

Visualizing the parts that make up a character also helps. Every detail does not have to be memorized. Treating a character as smaller pieces put together is a way to stop seeing characters as squiggles. Semantic and phonetic components are the most useful parts for that. Even before those categories are comfortable, characters can be broken into smaller pieces from the first day. Easy splits include 真 as 具 plus 十, and 明 as 日 plus 月. A rare, extremely complex character, one that will not show up in ordinary reading, can still be seen as a pile of familiar pieces such as horse, words, walking, heart, moon, chief, and roof.

A character's meaning does not always come from its semantic component.

## Empty Components

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Empty Components

An empty component does not relate to the sound or the meaning of the word being written. It gives neither a sound nor a meaning. In this character, it is empty. That status is not a fixed property of the shape. The same piece can be empty here and functional somewhere else.

There are two main kinds. Distinguishing marks were added to keep two words apart. Corrupted components are what remain after a character's original internal structure has been distorted. A piece can also be empty because it was added as decoration, to regularize a shape.

## Distinguishing marks

In early writing, one character was often used for more than one word, and context usually told them apart. One later fix was to add a semantic component, a meaning-bearing piece, as with 背. Another fix was to add a mark whose only job was to say that this writing is word A, while the original character stays with word B.

夫 shows a standing person from the front, just as 大 does. A mark is added so that the word being written is not 大. 少 takes 小. Here 小 works as a meaning component and as a sound component at once. A mark is added to keep the two characters apart. 否 and 不 are another such pair.

京 depicts a tall building and, by extension, a capital city, because such buildings appeared in cities. 高 is the same depiction, meaning "tall," with 口 added to distinguish it from 京. The two resembled each other more closely in the old script. After the mark was added they evolved differently, so the modern shapes look less alike.

## Corruption

Sometimes a character's form changes until the original internal structure is no longer intact. That is called corruption. 射 began as a drawing of a bow pulled with an arrow. A hand was later added to emphasize the drawing of the bow. Over time the bow was distorted until it resembled 身, "body," and was reread as that component. The traditional story, that body is there because a person draws the string toward the body, is not the historical structure. The body-shaped piece is empty, a leftover of the bow.

春 originally had vegetation and sun as semantic pieces, which fit spring. It also had 屯 as a sound component. 屯 sits in a sound group that can interact with zh, ch, and sh, so it fits 春. Over time 屯 and the vegetation piece merged into the top of 春. That top shape shows up in other characters too, always as a corruption, and not always from the same original pieces. In 泰, the top is a corruption of two hands plus the original sound piece.

外 has a 夕 that was once 月, yuè. The top of 春, written 𡗗, was 屯, tún.

## What to do with an empty piece

Empty components are hard to remember because they do not point at sound or meaning. A memory story can help, and it should rest on the character's functional components, not on a breakdown into pieces that do no work. The story of 偷 as someone slipping into a building after dark, knife in hand, is the kind to avoid.

Two workable sources for a story are the traditional explanation, used as a memorable account even when it is not historical, and the learner's own imagination. Folk explanations are fine when they are labeled as memorable rather than historical. The learner should invent a story before reading someone else's. In the image itself, an empty piece can be pictured as glass or transparent, so that its empty status is part of the picture, as with the glass "body" in 射.

Corruption is a graduate-level topic in paleography. It is not necessary to know every detail before learning a character that contains a corrupted piece. Sometimes the whole story helps. Often it is enough to know that the piece is empty and to make a memory story for it.

## Form Components

# Form Components

A form component uses its pictorial quality to express meaning. "Depicts" does not mean "still resembles." 光 once depicted a person holding a torch, and the modern shape no longer looks like that scene. The old form should be looked at the first time the character is met, before a mistaken visual reading takes hold. Direct corrections overwrite folk readings. The top of 美 is a headdress, not 羊. 儿 is not "legs."

When a component is talked about as depicting something, the claim is about the original form, not about the modern printed shape still looking like that thing.

## One identity, several printed shapes

Some components keep one identity across several printed shapes. 犬 and 犭 are the same component. 心, 忄, and 㣺 are the same component as well. So are 手 and 扌, and 刀 and 刂. 肉 and the 月-shaped form of flesh are also one component. Each set is one memory, with a position rule for which shape appears where.

A bound component, one that does not appear as its own character, is shown inside a host character while the rest of that character is dimmed. Components added later in history are timeline beats, as when 糸 was added to 羅.

Same-looking families should not be treated as one origin. 𡗗 in 奏, 泰, 奉, 秦, and 舂 has a different origin in each character.

Components also have spoken names, such as 儿字底, or in Taiwan the phrase that points at the bottom of 儿 in 儿. A pronounceable name is a handle for remembering the piece and a way to talk about characters aloud.

A character and the same shape used as a component can keep two lists of meanings. That split is taken up with meaning trees.

## Meaning Components

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Meaning Components

A meaning component carries an abstract extension of a concrete original sense. 力 began as a plow and extends to effort and strength. Form components, by contrast, carry concrete pictures. Very few characters are built only from meaning components.

Components are learned through tables of siblings that share the same piece. Each table lists the member characters and the role the component plays in each one. 木 can mean "part of a tree" in 根 and 標, "a type of tree" in 机, and "made of wood" in 架 and 機.

## The consistency test

A memory story holds up only if it still works for every sibling that shares the component. Reading 偷 as "roof, night, and knife" fails against 輸, 愈, 愉, and 喻. Reading 亼 as a roof breaks 合, 命, and 令. Stories anchored in the component's real function reinforce the whole family. Folk decompositions damage it.

One true component meaning can open a whole cluster. 亼 as "a mouth speaking downward from above" holds 合, 命, and 令 together. The component should be taught just before its members.

Transparent characters such as 想 encode almost for free. Explanation time should go to opaque ones such as 藍, where stylization, corruption, and sound drift hide the logic. The older language is a tool for explaining those links, not material to memorize. 監, 籃, and 藍 belonged to one sound family.

Empty pieces inside a meaning analysis still get a one-line reason. The 冖 in 勞 was once 衣, a corruption. The extra 口 in 命 was added to keep 命 apart from 令, and that reason also records the pair that learners mix up.

An English phrase such as "plowed through his work" sits close to the way 力 extends from a plow to effort. 合 originally meant "to answer" and is now written 答. That link turns an old sense into a live connection between two characters.

## Meaning Trees and Original Meanings

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Meaning Trees and Original Meanings

The original meaning of a character is the trunk of its meaning tree. It is the sole sense bound to the form, and it is the root that explains every later extension. A simple test for the basic modern sense is to ask what the character would mean if it were written on a whiteboard with no extra context.

Extensions can sit close to that trunk or farther away. Direct extensions are marked with a single arrow. Distant ones are marked with a double arrow. Sound-loan senses are marked so that no false bridge gets built from the form to that sense. About 18 percent of characters are sound loans. 我 pictured a weapon. 找 once meant "to paddle." 錢 in the sense of "money" is a loan path. Knowing the loan ahead of time helps the mind accept that the form and that sense do not match. Some old senses later moved to another character: 必 to 柲, 其 to 箕, 常 to 裳, and 文 to 紋.

## What to store

Do not memorize a sense that can already be derived from a more basic one. Rare senses can wait until they are actually met. The character 長 is the worked example of that pruning.

Two readings get two trees, with separate original-sense branches, as with 長 read cháng and 長 read zhǎng, or 看 read kàn and kān.

A character and the same shape used as a component keep two different trees. The senses of 又 as a word are not the same as its senses as a component, where it points at hand-actions. 日 as a component points at light or time. Using the component tree when the character tree is needed, or the other way around, means the analysis has already gone wrong. 游 is understood through 氵 as "actions involving liquids," not through "a body of water."

## Sound Components

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Sound Components

A sound component is a prediction tool. Early writing was largely picture-like, and only so many words can be drawn. The more abstract a meaning is, the harder it is to depict. One answer was to add a mark, such as a mark at the bottom of a tree to point at the root. Another answer was the rebus principle. Take an existing character and use it for its sound to write another word.

A rebus does not always match the target word exactly. Context fills the gaps. An English picture of an ear can stand in "Oh, here you are," even though "ear" has no H. Writing systems are built for people who already know the spoken language.

The same thing happened with these characters. 北 first meant a person's back. Back and north sounded alike, and north is hard to draw, so the back character was borrowed to write north. Later a body component was added to write 背, "back," and 北 was kept for north. The two words have different tones today. The older language most likely did not have tones, so there is usually little or no tonal match between a character and its sound component. Tone comes from audio.

Adding a semantic piece to 北 to make 背 signals that the word sounds like north and has to do with the body. An English parallel is adding a letter to show that the word is "here" rather than "ear" or "year." In 背, 北 does two jobs at once. It supplies both meaning and sound.

## What a sound component can predict

Characters that share a sound component do not necessarily share a pronunciation. They may not even share an obvious modern connection. 尚, read shàng, appears in 黨 dǎng, 堂 táng, and 躺 tǎng. 占 zhān pairs with 店 diàn. Those patterns are themselves something to learn.

A common claim is that sound components are not helpful because they cannot guess a pronunciation with complete accuracy. That looks at the problem from the wrong direction. Once the way sound can vary is understood, the components become useful. The working drill is to guess a range, not an exact hit. Coming across 樘, a learner who knows that 木 is often semantic and 尚 is often a sound component can predict two things. The sense is likely tied to trees or wood. The pronunciation is probably one of shang, chang, zhang, tang, or dang. Lookup then gives the exact reading and sense. The tone would not have been knowable from the component alone.

English spelling has the same limit. A person cannot look at an unfamiliar English word and be sure of its pronunciation from the letters alone.

The habit to build is simple. On any unknown character, predict meaning and pronunciation before the dictionary opens.

## Sound Series

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Sound Series

A sound series is the set of characters that share a sound component. Thousands of years of sound change can make those links look baffling. The point of studying a series, at first, is the sound. Meanings and whether a given character belongs in a current vocabulary list can wait.

Very few series look as tidy as a learner might expect, with every member sharing one reading and one tone. It is unusual for tones to be uniform across a series. Tones most likely did not exist in the older language, which is why they do not count for membership in a series. They matter completely when speaking.

Learners often reject a valid link when the surface pinyin differs, such as 比 bǐ and 批 pī. An authoritative note that those two readings are related acts like a fence. Once that fence is up, the brain can take in the sound connection.

If two sounds seem unrelated, say them aloud. Notice whether they are made in the same part of the mouth. g, k, and h come from the back of the mouth. d comes from the front. The same self-test works for the other groups.

Learn the basic groups first, then notice the crossings, then revisit. The crossings should not be memorized cold. They come from sound change over time. The English name George keeps a soft G, while an older German form keeps a hard G. The English ending -tion is spoken as "shun," a change in the same family as t or d meeting zh, ch, or sh.

Mark exactly what drifts. It may be the initial, the final, or the tone. A true irregular, such as the -n on 勁 jìn, should be labeled as an exception. A marked exception can stay inside the fence. An unmarked one knocks the fence down.

Multiple readings of one character are usually related branches of one tree. Examples include 盛 as chéng and shèng, 彈 as dàn and tán, and 說 as shuō and shuì. The puzzling cases are two old glyphs that merged into one modern shape, such as the two histories behind 舌. Those are separate characters and separate series.

## What the groups are for

The rules exist so the learner can remember characters, call them back, and make smart predictions about ones not yet studied. Out-of-level members of a series still teach the pattern. They do not have to be enrolled as vocabulary. 夬 functions as a sound-bearing piece only in modern Mandarin, and it belongs on series pages without being added to a word list.

A short verse that strings known characters of one group together gives the group something the ear can hold. One verse used in the lesson joins 張, 尚, and 陳 with 湯 and 鄧.

## Surface vs Deep Structure

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Surface vs Deep Structure

Deep structure is the hidden logic of the writing system. It includes functional components, internal logic, and the connections of sound and meaning. It shows how the characters actually work. Surface structure is how a character is supposed to look at a given moment in history, or how it looks today. It includes radicals, strokes, stroke order, and non-functional components. It shows how to write a character, and what the character is supposed to look like in a given setting. The People's Republic of China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong each have their own standards. Those standards overlap a great deal and are not exactly the same.

Both layers matter, and they apply at different times. Deep structure explains why a character looks the way it does. Surface structure explains how to write it.

## Functional components

To understand a given character, see how it divides into functional components, what job each one is doing, and how those jobs connect to the sound and the original meaning. A functional component is a part that is serving a direct function in that character, usually by giving a pronunciation or a meaning. Functional components belong to deep structure.

Decompose only to the functional level. 部 breaks into 咅 plus 阝. Cutting deeper produces noise. Over-splitting, and parsing by radical, build memory stories on parts that explain nothing. The radical of 錦 tells the reader where to find the character in a dictionary. It does not say what the character is.

The same component can take on different jobs in different characters. 立 is a meaning component in 端, a sound component in 拉, and an empty component in 音. In 部 it is a non-functional fragment. 口 as a component means sound or speech. 耳 as a component means perception. 而 as a component means beard.

A radical is a role a component can play, not part of the component's nature. Appearing on the list of radicals does not make a shape a radical in every character that contains it, and knowing which part is the radical does not reliably say what that part is doing. Radicals belong to surface structure.

There are four types of functional component. Form components use their pictorial quality to express meaning. Meaning components use an acquired meaning to express the character's meaning. Those two types together are called semantic components. Sound components give a sound, and empty components give neither a sound nor a meaning.

Some sound loans need to be marked honestly, including 而, 需, 成, and 要. Some sound links are not obvious in modern Mandarin, including 位, 成, and 定. The semantic part alone can separate a pair such as 問 and 聞, both built on 門.

## The IME Method

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# The IME Method

On a computer, an input method lets a person type the sound of a word and then pick the right character from a list. The IME method is that same move, done from memory, when no computer is offering the list.

To recall a forgotten written form, list the sound components that can carry the syllable, list the semantic components related to the meaning, and cross-match those two lists until a pairing feels familiar. The method can work with as few as about 50 known characters. If the arrangement of the pieces will not come, write both candidate layouts and let recognition choose. Recognition outlasts recall. That choice is the mental version of picking from an input-method window.

Keep a reverse index from syllables to sound components, loosened by the related-initial groups such as b, p, and f. If that index does not help, recall other characters with the same sound and mine their components.

The method also runs backwards for reading. Classify which piece is likely giving the sound and which is likely giving the meaning. Then let the combination narrow the answer. Some pieces have stable habits. 各 is almost always sound. 穴 is almost always meaning. 立 swings. It gives meaning in 站, 端, and 豎, and sound in 拉, 粒, and 垃. In 位 it does both.

## Guessing in context, then looking up

In running text, a semantic component plus the context can give the category of meaning, and a sound component plus group knowledge can give a pronunciation range. Commit to a guess, then check it. The moment of committing and then checking is the encoding. 臉盆 is the worked example of that guided guess.

Lookups have a threshold. Circle an unknown character at most once per page, and look it up only after about three circles. Write the definition in the margin, and keep an index at the back of the book. Read slightly above the present level.

Memory stories are scaffolding. In the end they fall away. What remains is the sound and the meaning of the words the learner wants to read and write.

Heard tones sometimes differ from dictionary tones. Map the surface tone that was heard back to the citation tone in the dictionary, so the audio cue and the dictionary cue stay connected.

# The Pipelining Strategy

Do not learn form, meaning, and sound on the same day. A cohort of three to five characters moves through a three-day cycle. On the first day, recall the words in one direction. On the second day, recall them in the reverse direction, which is much easier. On the third day, attach the written form to a word that is now overlearned.

Cohorts overlap, the way loads of laundry overlap in a wash cycle. Each day then pairs one new task with two already-known ones. The point of the stagger is to create the advantage a native writer already has. Writing the form is easier once the spoken word is fully known.

Each pronunciation-meaning pair gets its own card. 道 read dào and 道 read dǎo are separate. 那 read nà and 那 read nèi are separate. Merging those cards later is allowed as an experiment.

A day's intake is three to five new characters plus common carrier words. Those carrier words combine the new characters with known ones, up to about 20 items a day. That mix is how the bulk of a vocabulary was built in the account that teaches this method. A delayed-form variant is also allowed. Hold off for a month or two after the spoken word is learned, then learn the written form.

Pipelining is the layer for new items, placed on top of spaced repetition. It does not replace daily reviews. Those reviews continue.

## Three Attributes of a Character

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# Three Attributes of a Character

A written character exists to record speech. Its job is to call up the spoken word. The idea that a character is an ideograph, a path from form to meaning alone, does not hold. Form, meaning, and sound are three equal sides of one memory item. Each edge, from form to sound and from form to meaning, has to be encoded and tested on its own.

Speech came first. A new character should be anchored to a word the learner already knows by ear, because hearing first brings in the stronger trace.

Form and sense often come apart. 大 depicts a person and means "big." The learner should generate the bridge between the picture and the meaning before reading someone else's explanation, asking, for example, why small over big means "sharp."

## What a component is doing here

The core skill is to name which attribute a component is contributing in this character. 大 is a form component in 美, a meaning component in 尖, and a sound component in 达. It can do two jobs at once. In 达 it is both sound and form.

Popular analyses often read form components as if they were meaning components. A story grounded in the scene the form depicts works better than combining abstract glosses.

A character and the same shape used as a component can keep two different lists of senses. 游 makes sense through 氵 as "actions involving liquids," not through "a body of water." Where a piece sits can change what it is entirely. 阝 on the left is 阜, hills. 阝 on the right is 邑, city.

Sound is its own encoding channel. Cultures without writing invented rhyme, alliteration, and meter as memory tools, which is one reason sound pattern can carry a character on its own.

## How Foreign Words Become Chinese

A foreign word enters Chinese by being rewritten one syllable at a time. Each syllable is written with a character taken from a small pool of characters used for their sound. Each of those characters already has a meaning and a tone, and both stay attached to it in the new word. The sound of the foreign word fixes which syllables are needed. The only decision left to the translator is which character writes each syllable.

The features of Chinese loanwords that people find strange all follow from that one decision: the extra syllables, the pitch pattern the original word did not have, and the different spellings of the same city in Taipei and Beijing.

## Core takeaways

- Chinese takes in foreign names in two ways. 音譯 rewrites the sound with characters that mean nothing as a string. 意譯 translates the meaning. A third way, 音義兼譯, does both, and it is rare because it needs a string of characters that fits the sound and also has a good meaning.
- The characters used for sound come from a small, nearly fixed pool. A final -l or -r is written 爾, an /s/ is written 斯 or 絲, a final /k/ is written 克.
- The mainland has one office, 新華社譯名室, and published tables that fix one character per sound. Taiwan has reference books and no compulsory standard. That is why Obama is 歐巴馬 in Taiwan and 奧巴馬 on the mainland.
- There is no rule that transliterations take first tone. In a corpus built from 1,372 English loanwords, first tone was 28.8% of syllables, against 26.9% in general vocabulary. The tone of each syllable is the tone of the character chosen for it.
- The rewritten word is longer than the original, carries tones the original did not have, and has every sound rounded to a legal Mandarin syllable. An English speaker not recognizing 波拉勒斯 as Boralus is the normal result.
- The Coca-Cola name 可口可樂 developed out of Shanghai names such as 可口汽水 and 可口露 in the 1920s. The story of a £350 contest in *The Times* won by 蔣彝 is false, and 蝌蝌啃蠟 was never an official name.

## Sound translation, meaning translation, and the rare mix of both

Chinese has two ways to take in a foreign name. 音譯 (sound translation) rewrites the sound. Kul Tiras becomes 庫爾提拉斯: four English syllables become five Chinese ones, and the five characters mean nothing as a string. 意譯 (meaning translation) drops the sound and translates the sense. Stormwind becomes 暴風城, storm-wind-city, and a Chinese speaker reads it as a description, not as a name. Blizzard's Chinese localizations use both and split them by category. City names mostly get meaning translation (暴風城 Stormwind, 鐵爐堡 Ironforge). Race and region names mostly get sound translation (庫爾提拉斯, 贊達拉).

The third way, 音義兼譯 (sound-and-sense translation), does both at once: the characters approximate the sound and also read as something that fits the thing named. It is used mostly in branding, because it needs a string of characters that both sounds right and means something good, and such a string usually does not exist. Coca-Cola as 可口可樂 is the textbook case. Taiwan's word for hacker, 駭客, is another: it sounds like the English word, and 駭 carries a sense of alarm. The mainland's 黑客 (black-guest) matches only the sound. Sonar became 聲納, sound-take-in. UFO became 幽浮, ghostly-floating. Viagra became 威而剛, roughly power-and-firmness.

Two more examples appear constantly in translation teaching, and each comes with an origin story that has not been confirmed. The usual claim about Revlon's 露華濃 is that it takes a line from Li Bai, 春風拂檻露華濃, which would make it both a sound fit and a literary allusion. No first-hand source for that origin story has been located. The usual claim about Carrefour's 家樂福 is that the French name means "crossroads" and the Chinese was built to read "every household happy and blessed". Taiwanese official and school material tells it that way. The Taiwan chain was renamed 萬家福 in June 2026 after 統一 took over, so in Taiwan the example is now historical.

The third way is rare because the needed coincidence is rarely found. Most of the time nobody finds a string of characters that fits both sound and meaning, the meaning is not used, and the word is written for sound only.

## The characters used for sound

Characters used for sound are bleached: their meaning has faded through repeated use in this role until readers stop noticing it. The standard teaching example is 麥克風, microphone, which has nothing to do with 麥子 (wheat) or 風 (wind). A transliterated word means something only as a whole string. Taken apart it produces nonsense, and Chinese readers do not take it apart.

The pool is small, and the assignments are nearly fixed. A word-final -l or -r (the /l/ in Bill, the /r/ in beer) is written 爾. An /s/ is written 斯 or 絲. A final /k/ is written 克, /m/ is written 姆, /t/ is written 特, /d/ is written 德. Syllables that start with a vowel use 阿, 埃, 伊, 奧, 烏. The mainland's tables add fixed rules on top: the letter *s* is written 斯 whether it is pronounced [s] or [z], which is why Jones is 瓊斯 and not 瓊茲. Cantonese uses a separate pool. Jack's /k/ takes the entering-tone character 積, and Rose's /s/ takes 士. That is why Hong Kong renderings can be identified as Hong Kong renderings on sight.

The usual explanation for why these characters and not others is that they are high-frequency, semantically empty, and together cover the syllable inventory. That is a reasonable account. No formal argument for it has been located, so it counts as folk consensus and not as a finding.

## Who fixes the standard rendering on the mainland and in Taiwan

The mainland has a single office for this. 新華社譯名室, the Xinhua transliteration room, was founded on 24 July 1956 after an instruction from Zhou Enlai: 譯名要統一，要歸口於新華社, renderings must be unified, and they answer to Xinhua. It is the only official body on the mainland that approves Chinese renderings of foreign names. It works by two principles, stated in eight characters. 名從主人 means the name follows its owner: translate from the pronunciation in the person's own language. 約定俗成 means an established rendering stays established, however wrong it looks now.

The reference works behind the office are large. 《英語姓名譯名手冊》 first appeared in 1965 and reached its fifth edition in 2018, with roughly 74,000 English-language names. 《世界人名翻譯大辭典》 came out in 1993 and was revised in 2007, with about 650,000 entries. Each source language has its own 譯音表, a syllable-to-character table with one character per sound. The tables for personal names and for place names differ slightly. Place names also have a national standard, GB/T 17693, covering English, French, German, Russian, Spanish, Arabic, Portuguese, Mongolian and (since 2019) Japanese. Its stated principle is sound translation first, with deference to established forms.

Taiwan has no compulsory standard. Publishers and public bodies issue reference wordbooks for anyone who wants them, and long usage settles the rest. The nearest thing to an authority is the 國家教育研究院 (formerly 國立編譯館). It publishes 《外國地名譯名》 (fourth edition 2014; the third edition carried 66,632 place names, each with English name, Chinese rendering and coordinates) and 《外國學者人名譯名》 (8,912 names across ten source languages). Both are posted publicly on 樂詞網. Its stated principle is the same 名從主人. The de facto press standard is 中央社, which compiled 《標準譯名錄》 in 1980 and later turned it into a database of roughly 122,000 entries. Media do not follow one source. Some outlets use CNA's renderings, some invent their own, and the same outlet can print one person's name two ways.

Neither the mainland arrangement nor the Taiwan arrangement is new. A 1924 Republican-era formulation already reads 即西文每一音素，均擇一發音相當之漢字，作為標準譯字: for each Western phoneme, pick one Chinese character of matching sound and make it the standard rendering character.

## Names that differ between Taipei and Beijing

The difference shows on well-known names. Obama is 歐巴馬 in Taiwan and 奧巴馬 on the mainland and in Hong Kong. Trump is 川普 in Taiwan and 特朗普 on the mainland. Bush is 布希 in Taiwan and 布什 on the mainland (Hong Kong: 布殊), for both presidents. Reagan is 雷根 in Taiwan and 里根 on the mainland (Hong Kong: 列根). Kennedy is 甘迺迪 in Taiwan and Hong Kong and 肯尼迪 on the mainland. Clinton is 柯林頓 in Taiwan and 克林頓 on the mainland. Sydney is 雪梨 in Taiwan and Hong Kong and 悉尼 on the mainland. A mainland reader who meets 雪梨奧運會 in Hong Kong or Taiwanese news can fail to identify which Olympics is meant.

China Daily gives a structural explanation for the personal-name differences, and the explanation is not sourced further than that article. Taiwan kept a Republican-era habit of making the first character of a foreign personal name a real Chinese surname. That habit gives 甘迺迪, 雷根 and 柯林頓, and it uses rare surnames too: 胡笙 for Hussein, 佘契爾 for Thatcher. The mainland has no such convention and produces 肯尼迪, 里根, 克林頓, 侯賽因, 撒切爾.

The Obama case is the one time the difference was argued in public. In November 2009 the US embassy in Beijing announced that the US government's standard Chinese rendering was 歐巴馬, on the grounds that it is closer to the English pronunciation. Xinhua did not change its rendering. Its reasons: Obama is a common Kenyan surname with an established rendering, 奧巴馬 had been in use for years, and head-of-state renderings are not changed lightly. By February 2015 the embassy's own press releases had gone back to 奧巴馬. A local story says Taiwan avoids 奧 because in Taiwanese the character carries a sense of underhandedness, as in 奧步, a dirty trick. That story circulates in Hong Kong press with no documentation behind it and should be treated as folklore.

## Where the tones come from

There is no flat-tone rule. Transliterations are not systematically first tone. The tone on a syllable is the tone the chosen character already had.

The numbers show this. Eleanor Glewwe's 2015 UCLA master's thesis built a corpus of 3,660 syllables drawn from 1,372 English loanwords in Mandarin. Baseline tone frequency across general Mandarin vocabulary is T1 26.9%, T2 24.7%, T3 18.3%, T4 30.1%. The commonest tone in general vocabulary is fourth tone. In her loanword subset (N=3,047) the figures are T1 28.8%, T2 29.5%, T3 16.0%, T4 25.7%. First tone in loanwords is therefore about two points above its baseline. That two-point difference is the whole of the "flat tone" effect, and it is close enough to noise that no rule can be built on it.

What did condition tone in her corpus was the voicing of the English consonant at the start of the syllable. Voiceless obstruents (p, t, k, s, f, the consonants made by blocking or squeezing airflow with the vocal cords switched off) took first tone 38.4% of the time (N=1,957). Sonorants (m, n, l, r, w, y, made with the vocal cords running throughout) took first tone 11.6% of the time and second tone 37.2% of the time (N=1,090). Glewwe's explanation is F0 perturbation: a voiceless consonant raises the pitch of the vowel after it slightly, a voiced consonant lowers it slightly, and the person borrowing the word hears the raised pitch as high tone and the lowered pitch as rising tone.

Her experiment gave a different result from her corpus. Live speakers adapting invented words keyed on English *stress* instead: stressed syllables took first tone most often, word-final stressed syllables took fourth tone, and the voicing effect did not appear. Glewwe's conclusion is that the two studies conflict and that tonal adaptation "depends on overall English intonation rather than small F0 perturbations." The field has no settled phonological rule.

Zhuting Chang's 2020 CUNY dissertation explains the data by not looking for a phonological rule at all. In Chang's account, tone assignment operates "beyond any usual phonological concerns." What adapters use is implicit knowledge of tone probability and character frequency in the existing lexicon, plus "seeking of appropriate characters based on their meanings, either to avoid unintended readings of loanwords or to form desired interpretations." Chang credits the writing system itself, which earlier work in this literature had underrated: one character is one syllable, homophones are everywhere, and compounding is free. The adapter picks the character for its meaning, and the tone is whatever tone that character has.

One constraint limits the choice. Mandarin has tonotactic gaps: syllable-and-tone combinations that have no character. There is no *dán*. Loanword adaptation does not fill those gaps. Only combinations already in the native lexicon are available. So a share of the tones in any transliteration were never chosen at all: that syllable exists in one tone, and that is the tone the word gets.

Three examples show the range of tone shapes.

- **可口可樂** kě-kǒu-kě-lè, tones 3-3-3-4. The most famous transliteration in the language contains no first tone.
- **德魯伊** dé-lǔ-yī, tones 2-3-1. It contains a third tone, the rarest tone in the language.
- **波拉勒斯** bō-lā-lè-sī, tones 1-1-4-1. Three first tones out of four. This is the shape people have in mind when they say transliterations come out flat.

Three words, three different tone shapes, all three verified against 教育部重編國語辭典. A fourth case settles the question. Boralus is written differently on the two sides of the strait, and the difference moves the tone. Blizzard's Taiwanese localization writes 波拉勒斯, starting on 波 (bō, first tone). The mainland writes 伯拉勒斯, starting on 伯 (bó, second tone). One English source word, two teams, a different first character, a different tone. If the tone were computed from the English, that could not happen.

One narrow sub-rule does hold. An earlier claim in the literature (Wu 2006) that inserted syllables beginning with /s/ take first tone holds in Glewwe's corpus: of 251 first-tone inserted syllables, 195 are the single syllable sī. That syllable is 斯, the -s at the end of 波拉勒斯 and 庫爾提拉斯.

A note for anyone saying 可口可樂 out loud: 3-3-3-4 is the citation form, not the spoken one. Two third tones in a row turn the first into a rising tone, so 可口 is said ké-kǒu and the whole word is said ké-kǒu-kě-lè.

## Why the original speaker does not recognize the word

Glewwe's thesis starts from one fact: English words borrowed into Mandarin must be assigned tones. There is no toneless option. Mandarin has no un-toned syllables, so the borrowing process adds pitch information that was not in the source word.

Three changes then happen to the word, and a fourth runs alongside them.

The word gets longer. Mandarin does not allow consonant clusters, so English *dr-* cannot stay as one unit. It splits across two syllables, 德 + 魯, and two English syllables become three. Mandarin also allows only -n, -ng and -r at the end of a syllable. Every other final consonant is either dropped or given a syllable of its own. The final -s of Boralus becomes 斯, and three syllables become four. Glewwe's term for the added syllables is epenthetic syllables. Her clearest case is *lace* arriving as lěisī: one English syllable, two Chinese ones.

The word gains tones. Every syllable now carries a pitch contour the original did not have, and those contours come from the characters, not from the English.

Every sound is rounded to the nearest legal Mandarin sound, because a syllable that does not exist in Mandarin cannot be written.

The fourth change, per Chang, is that the adapter is looking for characters whose meanings will not produce an unwanted reading, so the sounds are bent further to reach a character that reads acceptably. Where a tonotactic gap applies, there was no choice to make.

By the time Boralus comes out as 波拉勒斯 it is one syllable longer, it carries tones, every sound in it has been rounded, and at least one of its syllables was chosen for reasons unrelated to English. An English speaker not recognizing it is the expected outcome of these changes.

## What the characters mean when read one at a time

Reading the characters one at a time shows how little meaning they carry. 德魯伊 is 德 (moral norm, virtue), 魯 (dull-witted, clumsy; also the ancient state of Lu, Confucius's home), 伊 (a literary third-person pronoun, today used mostly as a transliteration syllable: 伊朗 Iran, 伊拉克 Iraq). Read literally it is virtue-dull-he. 德 is a high-prestige character and carries no meaning here. It is present because it sounds like d-, as it does in 德國 Germany and 德州 Texas. The English word means "oak-knower", from Proto-Celtic *dru-wid-s, a reading Pliny the Elder endorsed. A meaning translation exists on record, 橡木賢者, oak sage. Nobody uses it.

波拉勒斯 reads the same way: 波 (wave), 拉 (to pull, drag), 勒 (a bridle or bit), 斯 (to split; as a literary word, "this"). Wave-pull-bridle-this. That 波 lands on a harbour city is coincidence. 波 is the default character for foreign bo-/po- syllables, as in 波士頓 Boston, 波蘭 Poland, 波音 Boeing.

## Where 德魯伊 came from

Blizzard did not coin 德魯伊. A druid is a priest of the pre-Christian Celtic religion, and the older Chinese rendering is 督伊德 (dū-yī-dé, tones 1-1-2, the same source word with a different tone shape). The word was in games before World of Warcraft. Diablo II: Lord of Destruction added the Druid class on 29 June 2001, and the Taiwanese release wrote 德魯依. Both spellings are still in use in the community. Before that it was a standard Dungeons & Dragons class, written 德魯伊 throughout Taiwan's Chinese D&D 5E material. Blizzard is the company that made the word part of general vocabulary.

A widely repeated claim says 德魯伊 came into Chinese through Japanese ドルイド rather than straight from English, which would explain why it sounds so unlike the English word. Chinese Wikipedia's article does not say this, and no primary source has been found. The syllable mapping fits ドルイド more neatly than it fits /ˈdruːɪd/. That fit is a reason to keep looking. It is not evidence.

## The Coca-Cola name: what the record shows

The documented history has no contest and no prize. The name developed in the Shanghai market across the 1920s.

祝淳翔's research traces the lineage back to 可口汽水 in 《時報圖畫周刊》 no. 160, dated 6 August 1923. Watsons (屈臣氏) ran 可口露 advertisements in 《申報》 on 1 May 1924 and in 《新聞報》 on 2 May 1924, and 可口水 appears in 《工商新聞》 on 10 October 1924. The first issue of 《商業雜誌》 in November 1926 states the mechanism directly: 可口係 coca 之譯音, 可口 is the transliteration of *coca*. So the sound mapping was explicit at the time. 葉新 found a May 1930 advertisement in 《商業雜誌》 reading 屈臣氏汽水可口可樂. The Coca-Cola Company applied for the trademark on 26 March 1930, received approval on 24 June 1931, and had it published in 商標公報 no. 54 on 15 August 1931. The agent of record was the American lawyer Norwood F. Allman (阿樂滿), who practised in Shanghai from 1916 to 1950 and served as Coca-Cola's China counsel.

## The two stories about the Coca-Cola name

The famous story is false. It says that in the mid-1930s Coca-Cola advertised in *The Times* offering £350 for a Chinese name, and that the London-based scholar 蔣彝 (Chiang Yee, the "Silent Traveller") entered 可口可樂 and won. Taiwanese press including CNA still prints it. The dates rule it out. 蔣彝 did not reach London until 1933, and 可口可樂 was in print in 1930. In 1930 he was county magistrate of 九江 in Jiangxi, in China, and could not have entered a London contest. Two more objections stand: *The Times* is a British newspaper with no reason to run a Chinese-naming competition, and no such advertisement has ever been found.

The second story, also false, says that Coca-Cola's earlier Chinese name was 蝌蝌啃蠟, "tadpoles bite wax." It was never an official name. Its documented origin is Allman himself. He wrote it up in a 1957 issue of *Coca-Cola Overseas* as a negative example, a 荒誕字眼, an absurd rendering, to illustrate merchants who chase sound and ignore meaning. There is a real event under the story. Coke reached China around 1927–28 before the company had registered a Chinese name, so individual shopkeepers wrote their own characters on in-store placards, and some of those were nonsense. So "bite the wax tadpole" signs plausibly existed on shop walls. The claim that 蝌蝌啃蠟 was the official earlier brand name comes from that 1957 anecdote. It does not come from any 1927 label. Chinese Wikipedia files the whole set, the tadpoles, the £350, 蔣彝, as 傳聞, hearsay, and 備受質疑, heavily doubted.

## What the Coca-Cola name means

The false stories do not change what the name does. 可 means to affirm or to be suitable. 口 is the mouth. 樂 read as lè is joy. 可口 kěkǒu is a real pre-existing Chinese word meaning tasty or palatable, and was not invented for the brand. 可樂 reads as "can be enjoyed", and the brand has taken the word over so completely that in Taiwan 可樂 now means cola. 樂 has three readings, lè happy, yuè music, yào to be fond of, and learners often get it wrong; here it is lè. The name follows *ko-ka-ko-la* in sound and reads "tasty + joy" in meaning, both at once. Its ranking as the best brand translation ever made into Chinese is advertising-industry consensus, and the ranking has not been measured. The double fit is real, and it is why the example keeps being used.

## The one decision, used for meaning or not

The sound of the source word fixes the syllables. The characters are the translator's only decision. Using that decision for meaning gives 可口可樂: close to the sound, tasty and joyful in meaning, and in print in 1930, before any of the stories about its origin appeared. Leaving the decision unused gives 德魯伊 and 波拉勒斯, virtue-dull-he and wave-pull-bridle-this, whose characters were picked for sound alone and whose tones are the tones those characters had. The case where meaning is not used is the ordinary one. That is why the tones look random, why two localization teams can return the same city with different pitch, and why the person who owns the original word rarely recognizes it in the result.

## How to practice this

1. Take a transliterated name you know and write out its characters. Look up each character's tone. Notice that each tone is the tone of that character, with nothing taken from the English.
2. Count the syllables in the English word and in the Chinese one. Notice where a consonant cluster was split into two syllables. Notice where a final consonant became a syllable of its own.
3. Read the characters of 德魯伊 or 波拉勒斯 one at a time for their meanings. Notice that the string of meanings tells you nothing about the word.
4. Find one foreign name in a Taiwanese report and in a mainland report. Notice whether the first character differs. Notice whether the Taiwan form begins with a Chinese surname.
5. Say 可口可樂 aloud. Notice that the first 可 comes out rising, because two third tones in a row change the first one.
6. Find a transliteration that ends in -s. Notice whether the last syllable is 斯, read sī in first tone.

## Open questions

No Chinese-language study of 音譯詞聲調 using Taiwan-specific data has been located. The findings on loanword tone assignment from Glewwe and Chang are English-language and based on mainland or general Mandarin. The tone values for 可口可樂, 德魯伊 and 波拉勒斯 are identical in Taiwan and on the mainland, so the three worked examples are unaffected, but the general claims rest on sources that never looked at Taiwan.

The claim that 德魯伊 arrived through Japanese ドルイド is unsourced past aggregator sites. The syllable mapping fits, which is a reason to keep looking. It is not a reason to believe the claim.

## Language Research

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Language Research describes how a language works, while the learning pages describe what a learner should do about it. Its research so far covers 音譯, the way a foreign word enters Chinese by being rewritten one syllable at a time with characters chosen for their sound. Each of those characters keeps its own meaning and tone in the new word, so the translator's only real choice is which character writes each syllable. The learning pages are gathered here by job, from immersion method through grammar and Chinese characters to related memory pages. They share one model, in which a language is acquired through large amounts of attentive time in it, with preparation and tools making that time easier to understand.

## What is in it

The research covers one process from two directions. The Transliteration Into Chinese Bank is a research bank on 音譯 that stays off the public site. It covers who sets naming standards in the PRC and in Taiwan, the pool of characters a translator draws from and what decides the tones. It works through 可口可樂, 德魯伊 and 波拉勒斯 character by character. ## The learning wing, by job

### Method and immersion

Immersion is the core activity, and it rests on three pillars, with tools that make the time usable.

- [[wiki/Language/Refold Language Learning System|Refold Language Learning System]]: a course in two phases, first fundamentals and then reading plus listening, which balances three pillars and treats time in the language as the core of progress. [[wiki/Syntheses/ICS System|ICS System]] is the general learning system this wiki is organised around.
- [[wiki/Language/Three Pillars of Language Learning|Three Pillars of Language Learning]]: preparation, interactive immersion and freeflow, which have to be balanced for progress.
- [[wiki/Language/Refold-Language-Learning-System|Preparation]]: a short orientation to the sounds, words, characters, grammar and tools of a language, done so the next stretch of immersion becomes noticeable.
- [[wiki/Language/Refold Language Learning System|Interactive Immersion]]: a family of activities that use tools to go deeper into input, including the noticing game, sentence mining, intensive listening and transcription.
- [[wiki/Language/Refold Language Learning System|Freeflow Immersion]]: watching content without lookups or rewinding while attention stays high, which builds self-sufficiency.
- [[wiki/Language/Refold Language Learning System|Attention is Important]]: time in a new language counts only when attention is high, even before the language is understood.
- [[wiki/Language/Refold Language Learning System|Noticing Game]]: high-attention looking for handholds such as cognates, known words or list words inside a barely understood stream.
- [[wiki/Language/Refold Language Learning System|Hacking Comprehension Menu]]: a menu of tools, techniques and content choices that make immersion easier to understand.
- [[wiki/Language/Refold Language Learning System|Immersion Metalayers]]: a tool layer on top of a show or video that lets you look up words, pause after each line and jump between subtitles.
- [[wiki/Language/Refold Language Learning System|YouTube Immersion Account]]: a separate YouTube account used to find target-language videos compelling enough to hold attention.
- [[wiki/Language/Refold Language Learning System|Aim-Shoot-Skin for Language Learning]]: a loop that starts from a passage you can almost follow, turns each missing word or pattern into a flashcard and uses it in new sentences the same day.

### Grammar, script, characters

Grammar and script both rest on noticing patterns during immersion over learning rules.

- [[wiki/Language/Refold Language Learning System|Language Isn't Math]]: a language cannot be reduced to vocabulary and grammar rules that translate any thought, because native speech is set and specific in unpredictable ways.
- [[wiki/Language/Refold Language Learning System|Refold Grammar Primers]]: a grammar primer exposes patterns so they can be noticed in immersion, and it is not something to memorize or drill.
- [[wiki/Resources/Vietnamese Language Learning Resources|Vietnamese Grammar Primer]]: a short catalog of the separate words and word order that carry meaning in Vietnamese, where the verb does not change form.
- [[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|Character Primer]]: Chinese and Japanese characters are picture-like symbols that are easier to remember once broken into meaningful components.
- [[wiki/Language/The Han-Viet Bridge|The Hán-Việt Bridge]]: a method for Vietnamese speakers learning Mandarin, built on the Sino-Vietnamese readings already inside everyday Vietnamese words.
- [[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|How Chinese Characters Work]]: the hub for the character pages, where naming the job of each component makes the script predictable.
- [[wiki/Language/Chinese/Chinese Characters, Condensed|Chinese Characters, Condensed]]: the character pages stated as rules, one rule per line.
- [[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|Three Attributes of a Character]]: a character binds form, meaning and sound, and each link has to be encoded and tested on its own.
- [[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|Surface vs Deep Structure]]: deep structure is the sound-and-meaning logic of a character, and surface structure is how it is supposed to look when written.
- [[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|Form Components]]: a form component uses a picture to express meaning, so the ancient form comes first when the modern shape no longer resembles it.
- [[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|Meaning Components]]: a meaning component carries an abstract extension of a concrete original sense, and it is learned through tables of sibling characters.
- [[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|Sound Components]]: a sound component is a prediction tool that points at a range of pronunciations, not at one guaranteed reading.
- [[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|Sound Series]]: the set of characters that share a sound component, grouped by seven related-initial groups.
- [[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|Empty Components]]: a component that does no sound or meaning work in a given character, such as a distinguishing mark, leftover corruption or decoration.
- [[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|Meaning Trees and Original Meanings]]: a character's meanings grow from the original sense tied to its form into extensions, loans and branches for each reading.
- [[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|The IME Method]]: recalling a forgotten character by listing possible sound and meaning components and cross-matching them, the way an input method offers candidates.
- [[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|The Pipelining Strategy]]: a cohort of three to five characters moves through a three-day cycle, so form, meaning and sound are never learned on the same day.
