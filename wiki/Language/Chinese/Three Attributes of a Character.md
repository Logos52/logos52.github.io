# Three Attributes of a Character

A written character exists to record speech. Its job is to call up the spoken word. The idea that a character is an ideograph, a path from form to meaning alone, does not hold. Form, meaning, and sound are three equal sides of one memory item. Each edge, from form to sound and from form to meaning, has to be encoded and tested on its own.

Speech came first. A new character should be anchored to a word the learner already knows by ear, because hearing first brings in the stronger trace.

Form and sense often come apart. 大 depicts a person and means "big." The learner should generate the bridge between the picture and the meaning before reading someone else's explanation, asking, for example, why small over big means "sharp."

## What a component is doing here

The core skill is to name which attribute a component is contributing in this character. 大 is a form component in 美, a meaning component in 尖, and a sound component in 达. It can do two jobs at once. In 达 it is both sound and form.

Popular analyses often read form components as if they were meaning components. A story grounded in the scene the form depicts works better than combining abstract glosses.

A character and the same shape used as a component can keep two different lists of senses. 游 makes sense through 氵 as "actions involving liquids," not through "a body of water." Where a piece sits can change what it is entirely. 阝 on the left is 阜, hills. 阝 on the right is 邑, city.

Sound is its own encoding channel. Cultures without writing invented rhyme, alliteration, and meter as memory tools, which is one reason sound pattern can carry a character on its own.

## Open questions

- How does generating the bridge from a picture to a meaning, before reading an explanation, change what sticks?
- How does treating 大 as the same kind of component in 美, 尖, and 达 change what those characters become?
