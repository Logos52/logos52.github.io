# Three Attributes of a Character

For memory, a character is one stored item with three links: form to sound, form to meaning, and sound to meaning. Each link can fail on its own. Speech already carries the sound and the meaning. Writing adds the form. Any two of the links can hold while the third is gone.

Chinese script follows the same rule as alphabetic writing: it records spoken words. The old Western habit of reading the characters as symbols for ideas is called the **ideographic myth**. That habit does not fit how the script works.

## Core takeaways

- One character is one stored item with three links. Each link gets stronger or weaker on its own. That independence is vault doctrine, and no field study has established it.
- A yes-or-no test of a character hides which link is broken. Test form to sound and form to meaning separately.
- Attach a new character to a word already known by ear. [[wiki/Language/Chinese/The Pipelining Strategy|The Pipelining Strategy]] applies this at the scale of a session: spoken word first, then form.
- A character reused inside another brings all three attributes and can contribute through any one of them. Decide which one it contributes before explaining the host. 大 does all three jobs.
- A component can do two jobs at once. Check every component for a second function.
- Three common mistakes: explaining a character as ideas only, treating most parts as meaning parts, and treating the written form as the real word.

## How the three links work

For memory, the character is one stored item with three bound attributes. The form-to-sound link, the form-to-meaning link, and the sound-to-meaning link each strengthen or decay on their own. That independence is vault doctrine. It is not a finding from research. Any two links can hold while the third is gone. So a yes-or-no question, "do I know this character?", hides which link has broken. The learner stores one item and trains three separate links.

When the picture and the meaning differ, work out the bridge between them. That bridge is the content of the form-to-meaning link. In 艸 the picture and the meaning are the same thing: the drawing is the plant. 大 draws a standing adult. Adults are big compared with children, so the meaning follows from the picture instead of repeating it.

Learn the modern Mandarin reading, even though the character is older than that reading. 艸 had a different pronunciation when it was first carved. That reconstructed sound is background only. It does not go on a review card.

## Two study habits

Two scheduling habits go with the model. They are also vault doctrine, not research findings.

**Ear-first anchoring** attaches a new character to a word the learner already knows by ear. [[wiki/Language/Chinese/The Pipelining Strategy|The Pipelining Strategy]] is the same order applied across a session: spoken word before written form.

**Per-link testing** runs separate checks for form to sound and for form to meaning. Whether the extra card volume is worth the diagnostic value is still open. If the split cards do not change which link gets repaired, stop splitting them.

## 大 in three jobs

A character reused inside another character brings all three attributes with it. It can contribute through any one of them. Classify which one it contributes before explaining the host character. 大 plays all three roles.

As a **form component** it contributes its picture. In 美, 大 supplies a person. The headdress has worn down over time into a shape that looks like 羊. The meaning "big" and the sound dà are not used. The parse is a person plus a headdress. [[wiki/Language/Chinese/Form Components|Form Components]] holds the full headdress analysis and treats the sheep reading as a folk error.

As a **meaning component** it contributes its sense. In 尖, 小 over 大 describes tapering to a point. Characters built only from meaning components came late and never became common. [[wiki/Language/Chinese/Meaning Components|Meaning Components]] holds the sibling-falsifies test, which checks stories built on those parts.

As a **sound component**, the original 大 in 達 signals the reading dá. [[wiki/Language/Chinese/Sound Components|Sound Components]] describes the range-then-lookup cycle and works through 棠 as its example.

A component often does two jobs. The same 大 in 達 also depicts a person crossing 辶. That gives "go through" and then "reach." Check every component for more than one function.

## Parts that carry meaning and parts that carry nothing

Form components and meaning components together are **semantic components**: any part that feeds the host character's meaning. **Empty components** contribute nothing. The top of 言 is what remains of a mark above a tongue. 口 does the form work. [[wiki/Language/Chinese/Empty Components|Empty Components]] covers the two routes into that leftover and the glass repair.

Early 達 showed a person walking a sheep across an intersection. 大 later degraded into 土. So the modern right side reads as 幸, and the original structure is hidden. Form Components and Empty Components hold the general rule on how forms corrupt.

## Component meanings and word meanings

A component's meaning is separate from the same character's meaning as a word. 水 as a word is a body of water. As 氵 it marks actions on liquids. 火 as a word extends to anger and firepower. As 火 or 灬 it marks light, heat, and actions involving fire. [[wiki/Language/Chinese/Meaning Trees and Original Meanings|Meaning Trees and Original Meanings]] holds those two inventories.

Position can decide what a component is. 阝 on the left is 阜, hills. 阝 on the right is 邑, city.

Variant forms keep their identity: 氵=水, 灬=火, 訁=言, 艹=艸, and 人/亻/儿. 儿 has two histories. As a component it is a person. As a standalone simplified character it is 兒 with the top deleted. Both histories belong on Form Components.

## Three mistakes the model rules out

Three failure modes go against the rule that the script records spoken words.

The first is the ideograph reflex. It explains a character purely as a set of ideas put together, and it drops the sound attribute.

The second is meaning-component inflation. It treats most semantic parts as meaning components by default, or it cuts out fragments that have no function and attaches stories to them. Meaning Components holds the test that shows those stories to be false.

The third is glyph-as-canonical-word. It treats the written form as the real word and speech as secondary. Writing is lasting storage for speech, which disappears as soon as it is spoken. That is why cultures without writing built their spoken lines to be easy to remember.

## What the model costs and how to check it

The three-link model is vault doctrine. It has not been tested as a named method. The cost is three checks in place of one recognition card. The rule for stopping is the same: if the split does not change the repair, stop splitting. The expected sign is a character the learner can read aloud but not write, or write but not read aloud. That is one link broken while another holds.

[[wiki/Language/Chinese/How Chinese Characters Work|How Chinese Characters Work]] is the hub and sets the reading order. [[wiki/Language/Chinese/Surface vs Deep Structure|Surface vs Deep Structure]] covers functional decomposition: where to stop cutting a character into parts.

The question "do I know this character?" is no longer the test. The three links are what get trained. Ear-first anchoring is how the form joins a word the learner already holds.

## How to practice this

1. Take a character you think you know and test it twice: form to sound, then form to meaning. Notice which test fails. A single yes-or-no answer would have hidden that.
2. Before you learn a new character's form, make sure you already know the spoken word by ear. Then attach the form to that word. Notice that the form now joins a word you already hold.
3. Keep separate review cards for form to sound and for form to meaning. Notice which card fails, because that is the link to repair. If the split never changes which link you repair, stop splitting.
4. When a character's picture and its meaning differ, work out the bridge between them. 大 draws a standing adult, and adults are big next to children. Notice that the bridge is the content of the form-to-meaning link.
5. When a component appears inside a larger character, decide whether it gives its picture, its meaning, or its sound. Do this before you explain the host character. Then check for a second job, as 大 in 達 gives both sound and picture.
6. Look for a character you can read aloud but not write, or write but not read aloud. Notice that one link is broken while another holds. That is the sign the model predicts.
