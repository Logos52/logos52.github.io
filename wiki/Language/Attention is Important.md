# Attention is Important

A barely-understood language hour counts as a session when a word, sound, subtitle, or pattern is registered as present. Early language content is often hard to understand, and exposure is still required. The hour counts through that noticing.

## The job, and the rule

Attention gets a job. The job is to notice something — a word, a sound, a subtitle, a pattern — registered as present, on purpose. [[wiki/Language/Noticing Game|Noticing Game]] is that job as a procedure.

The concrete target-set does not need a student-only sheet. Cognates, words already known, words primed yesterday: ten items, ten visual minutes, a mark on what appeared. That is the page. When comprehension is low, the target is the repair. Immersion here means time with real language — shows, videos, talk, text — rather than only with lessons about the language.

Attention keeps that time from becoming passive listening. It builds familiarity with sounds, words, subtitles, visual cues, and recurring patterns. It makes difficult content less emotionally flat because there is a game to play. It creates data for self-regulation: what was easy to notice, what stayed invisible, and what made focus collapse. Active attention turns immersion from vague exposure into a stream of small targets. That is [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing|Deep Processing]] at the grain of a word or sound, and the same split as [[wiki/Concepts/Are You Thinking, or Just Consuming|Are You Thinking, or Just Consuming?]], applied to language input.

If attention drops to zero, the session is no longer a useful language-learning session. Background or passive listening does not count. [[wiki/Language/Freeflow Immersion|Freeflow Immersion]] is the lower-tool mode that still requires this rule. That does not mean everything has to be understood. It means engagement enough to look, listen, predict, compare, or confirm.

The barely-understood hour now has a job — notice something — and a fail condition. Zero attention still does not count. Not-understanding-everything still does.

## Open Questions

Whether a session with partial attention — some noticing, some drift — should count as a fraction of the hour or as a failed hour.

## Sources

- Schmidt, R. (1990). The role of consciousness in second language learning. *Applied Linguistics*, 11(2), 129–158. Conscious registration of a surface element as the condition for that element to become intake.
- Schmidt, R. (1995). Consciousness and foreign language learning: a tutorial on the role of attention and awareness in learning. In R. Schmidt (Ed.), *Attention and awareness in foreign language learning* (pp. 1–63). University of Hawai‘i Press.
- Tomlin, R. S., & Villa, V. (1994). Attention in cognitive science and second language acquisition. *Studies in Second Language Acquisition*, 16(2), 183–203. Detection — attention as registration — is the component that matters for acquisition.
- VanPatten, B. (1996/2004). *Input processing*. Learners process what they attend to; unattended exposure is not intake.
