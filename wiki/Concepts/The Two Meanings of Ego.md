# The Two Meanings of Ego

The word ego names two unrelated things, and each one takes the opposite treatment from the other. One is self-regard: prose that works to raise the reader's opinion of its author, whatever the label says the subject is. The other, selfhood, runs as high as honesty allows. A piece loaded with the first recruits, and a reader who came for the subject finds himself cast as somebody's audience; a piece short of the second goes flat a different way, correct and evenly voiced and attributable to nobody. One word for both turns them into a single dial, and a writer who turns that dial down far enough to stop recruiting has turned his own judgment down with it.

The pair comes apart cheaply, since nothing but a calendar ever joined them. What stands in for the dial afterwards is one question, put to one sentence at a time.

## One Word by Accident

English had *ego* for a hundred and eighty years before either disputed sense arrived. From 1707 it was metaphysical vocabulary for the thing that feels, acts and thinks, and a psychology textbook of 1890 discusses the pure Ego at length with no analyst near it. The conceit sense dates to about 1891. The clinical sense came last, in 1894, and it came in by translation. The German was *das Ich*, the I, an unremarkable word for whichever part of a person deals with the world and gets things done, carrying no vanity; its English translator preferred Latin, three years after that spelling had picked up conceit. Three unrelated jobs now sit on one spelling, and a century of argument about how much of himself a writer should show has run inside the merger. No idea joined these senses, only a calendar, so nothing breaks when they come apart.

## Self-Regard

Self-regard runs to a short catalogue of forms. The credential drop puts the author's standing, access or track record where the subject had no need of it. The prediction produced after the event confirmed it is the same move on a delay, and the process tour, an account of how the work got made in which the maker is the interesting part, runs it at length. The humblebrag files the claim as a complaint or a modest aside.

All four share a structure, which makes them checkable.

**A sentence that keeps working when a different subject is dropped into it was carrying the author.**

That is the swap test: whatever survives the substitution belonged to the author from the start.

```text
Weak:   After a decade of reading slush, I can tell you most rejections happen on page one.
Strong: Most rejections happen on page one.
```

The economics come out the opposite way round from the intuition. Self-esteem was set out long ago as a fraction, success over pretensions, with the pretensions underneath, so a credential enlarges the denominator: the claim goes on the record and the work has to be worth more than it was a line earlier. The same account calls surrendering a pretension as blessed a relief as getting it gratified. It is an expensive move, a debt contracted mid-paragraph and payable in work that has not been done.

What makes the debt tempting is that a person carries as many social selves as there are people holding an image of him, and a credential goes in to adjust one of those images. Keeping the image up has a running cost, since image and work draw on one supply, so hours spent on how a thing looks come out of the hours available for looking at it. The present economy makes the withdrawal feel obligatory. A career organized as a one-person business turns self-promotion into compliance, and the sentence that leaves the credential out feels like a mispriced asset.

## Selfhood

Selfhood is one person's judgment left where a reader can see it: this example kept, that claim turned away. Two people working the same material almost never cut it the same way, and the cutting is where the person is. Take the person out and the judgment goes too, which leaves prose that is complete, correct and shaped by nothing: compiled, assembled from what was available rather than chosen by anyone. The usual verdict, that anybody could have written this, is accurate: the choices only one person would have made are the ones that got sanded off.

Descriptions of the substance reach for instrument words rather than personality words. The shortest is three words long, writing is selection, which puts the author in every choice and in none of the sentences. Longer versions name what the selecting runs on: a filter of accumulated taste and experience nobody else holds a copy of, and a voice, the sound of one mind recalling material no one else witnessed. The absence has a clinical picture as well as a critical one. The false self runs in degrees, politeness and social reserve at the mild end, and only at the far end achievements that perform perfectly and feel dead from inside.

The most quoted statement of the instrument view is rougher than the view. Writing is the act of saying I, "of imposing oneself upon other people, of saying listen to me, see it my way, change your mind," which the essayist who wrote it filed under the tactics of a secret bully. Her camera belongs to a separate claim about syntax, that shifting a sentence's structure alters its meaning as inflexibly as moving a camera alters the photographed object. Aggression at the desk and aim on the page sit together comfortably.

## The Line-Level Test

Held apart, the two collapse into one question, put to any line under suspicion: does the line change what a reader sees of the subject, or only what he thinks of the author? A failing set down in enough detail that a reader recognizes his own copy of it is selfhood doing knowledge-work, particular experience spent on making the subject usable to somebody else; the identical failing, arranged so a reader registers how candid the author is being, is self-regard in other clothes.

Sentences with no substitute subject stall the swap test, and a second move covers them: the first-person clause comes out and the loss gets counted. Facts about the subject going missing mean the clause was load-bearing; only an impression of the author means it was display.

Neither move is worth much run by the writer on his own draft in the hour he wrote it. What a line does to a reader can be confirmed by a second person; what the author intended by it can be confirmed by nobody. Both are review instruments, wanting a reader who does not know the author, or enough delay that the sentence arrives as a stranger's. What to expect is deletions that cost the piece no fact about its subject. A deletion that takes subject-information with it means the test misfired on a line that was carrying selfhood.

Several vocabularies reach the same question. A style manual's first reminder puts the writer in the background so that attention goes to the sense and substance of the writing rather than the mood and temper of the author. A guide written from the cognitive side makes direction the whole test, asking whether a sentence points outward at the world or back toward the writer, and it supplies the gentlest account of the failure: much of it is the curse of knowledge, a writer narrating his trade because he can no longer imagine not knowing it. Two more ask whose standard the sentence answers to, the writer's own or an imagined audience's, and whose business the answer is, since what a reader concludes about the author belongs to the reader.

The agreement is worth less than it looks, since the four descend from one humanist and rhetorical line rather than from four independent observations. The manual's co-author later called the essayist congenitally self-centered, a remark about motive that leaves his rule about display standing.

## Humility as Costume

Self-regard dresses down as readily as it dresses up, which is where the test starts to pay. Self-criticism staged so that its candour will register is self-regard in a hair shirt, and readers price it the way they price a boast.

The disguised form is the one with a measurement behind it. Across nine studies, humblebragging came out behind plain bragging on liking, perceived competence, compliance with requests and financial generosity. Sincerity is the mediator: a straightforward boaster is at least believed to mean it, while the humblebragger forfeits the boast and the credit for honesty together. Humblebrags built on complaints do worse than plain complaining.

The costume comes at two temperatures. The cool one is the post-wounded voice, practised flatness and irony about one's own pain, worn in advance against a charge the writer expects; the critic who named it defends the wounded voice and reads accusations of cliché as alibis for a closed heart, so the label describes a posture and settles nothing about the pain underneath. The warm one is floodlighting, intimate material poured over a reader to compel a response, disclosure operating as a demand.

**What separates any of these from honest disclosure is the direction the writing faces.** Writing that faces the failing hands a reader something to carry off, and a note kept for the author's own use that others are allowed to overhear does the same by accident. The staged kinds face the author, at either temperature.

The correction overshoots at the far end, and the overshoot has a name. Echoism is a fear of seeming self-absorbed grown heavy enough that stating a need becomes impossible, and prose arrives in the same condition once every trace of its author has been rubbed off, having given up the one thing a reader came for. A pass that starts striking the particulars that made a piece unlike anyone else's has stopped working, and the person goes back in.

## Writing the Self Honestly

None of this rules out writing about oneself, and the book that founded the essay settles both substances in a single paragraph. It promises the author's portrait with the flaws left in, "it is myself I paint," and refuses what it calls borrowed beauties, the decoration its author says he would have worn if the world's favour had been the point. Both settings decided in one paragraph, dated 1580.

An essay on the form gets the discipline down to seven words, never to be yourself and yet always, with personality named as the material an essay is made of and the thing likeliest to wreck it. The craft books turn that into procedure. Personal narrative splits into situation and story, the events on one side and the understanding the writer has arrived at on the other, and unworked confession fails by handing over the first while withholding the second. The writer becomes a character inside his own piece, drawn in the round and shown to be wrong, on the argument that protecting one's dignity and earning a reader's trust cannot both come first. Performed humility looks different beside that requirement, because the staged confession is selected so that nothing is actually at risk. The genre's defenders draw the line in the same place: narcissism is building a self-image that pleases you, and honest self-writing keeps working past the drafts that flatter.

## The Case Against the Split

Four objections carry weight, and each costs the split something. The first is that self-audit is worthless in principle: the mind keeps a press secretary inventing respectable motives for decisions already taken, which leaves whoever needs the test most the worst-placed person to run it on himself. That is the argument for testing sentences instead of motives, and for the second reader.

The second is that the drive for status cannot be brought to zero, only paid in another currency: with the credentials gone it returns as visible restraint, as conspicuous taste, as humility played as a manoeuvre, and denouncing status-seeking pays well in the virtue game. Zero is a direction rather than a state, and recognizing the drive in its new currency is most of the walking.

The third denies there is any substance to be present. One dramaturgical account splits expression into what a person gives, the message intended, and what he gives off, the signs an audience treats as more reliable — the swap test in its original form, since a credential gives information and gives off an impressive author. That account then goes where the split cannot follow, making the self a dramatic effect arising from the scene rather than a cause behind it, on which reading selfhood is a compliment paid to a performance that worked.

The fourth is the oldest. The case for extinguishing personality wants the artist to leave no trace, a catalyst that comes out of the reaction unchanged, and the psychoanalytic reply is that the wish to be admired never departs but matures — into creativity, empathy, humor, the capacity to contemplate one's own impermanence, and wisdom. Both hand the person back on the way out: the catalyst is still a particular medium, a mind whose combinations of experience belong to it alone, and five mature transformations are selfhood renamed. The sharpest form of the dissent is the essay that puts sheer egoism first among the motives for writing and then insists nothing readable gets written without a constant struggle to efface one's own personality.

Independence is the weakest plank, and the pressure comes from inside the same literature: one clinical account puts image and work on a single energy budget, another treats admiration-seeking and creative work as one drive at different degrees of maturity. What survives all four objections is the procedure. The stronger claim, that a self holds two separable substances, does not.

## The Generic Floor

The stakes moved recently, and one controlled experiment puts numbers on the move. Writers handed story ideas by a language model produced work rated more creative, better written and more enjoyable, with the largest gains going to those who had scored least creative unaided, and their stories resembled one another more closely than the unaided ones did. The floor came up and the spread narrowed in one trial, which relocates the loss: inside any single piece the writing is fluent and often better than what it replaced, while the shortfall shows up across pieces, in how little distance separates them, which is where a writer can check. Algorithmic distribution presses the same direction by promoting whatever travels. So the generic floor, the level of fluent, organized, competent prose now available for the asking, has stopped distinguishing anybody, and the complaint that anyone could have written this has widened to include machines under "anyone."

What the floor cannot manufacture is the layer that traces to one life: a failure with a date on it, a stake, a refusal that cost something. A competing account puts the scarce component in accumulated taste rather than in biography, a second exit from compiled prose: a writer with nothing autobiographical in the piece can still be the only one who would have selected those things in that order. Either route makes judgment the scarce good, and polish stopped being evidence of judgment some time ago.

The stake has a cleaner statement in two words usually treated as one. Attention is notice, value-neutral and extractable, winnable by anything loud and by any interchangeable author who sounds impressive. Recognition is being seen whole by someone whose seeing counts for the writer, and it is never taken, only made possible. Self-regard bids for attention, and selfhood is the condition under which recognition becomes available at all. The swap test lands on the same pair, since attention survives a change of subject and recognition does not.

What the objections leave standing is a procedure rather than a picture of the self. The word can go on meaning three things while the writing does not have to. Selfhood as high as honesty allows and self-regard at zero are two settings rather than one dial, and the sentence in doubt goes to a reader who does not know the author with one question attached, about what it changes for him. An honest answer of "only what he thinks of me" takes the sentence out.
