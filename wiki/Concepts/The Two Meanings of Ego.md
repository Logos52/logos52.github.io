# The Two Meanings of Ego

Most readers have had both experiences, though the two rarely get connected. There is the piece where the author keeps arriving — the credentials, the well-placed names, the process told as a small heroism — and the slow sense of being recruited: you came for the subject and got cast as someone's audience. And there is the piece with nobody home, competent and complete and so evenly voiced it could have come from anywhere. The first is how "ego" became an insult. The second is what removing it all the way buys. One word is doing the work of two here. Self-regard is the author asking to be admired, and selfhood is the author being present at all — different things, moving independently, deserving opposite treatment.

Telling them apart is the whole game in anything written close to the self. And it turns out the two have been told apart for centuries, under other names, because the shared name was an accident to begin with.

## One Word by Accident

The shared name is an accident, and a documented one. Freud never wrote "ego"; he wrote das Ich, "the I," his plain word for the ordinary part of a person that faces reality and gets things done. His English translator preferred Latin, and the Latin landed on an English word already in use — "ego" had meant conceit since about 1891, and the psychoanalytic sense arrived only in 1894. Three years apart, a vanity word and a word for the whole present person were welded into one, and a century of advice about how much ego belongs in creative work inherited the weld. Which is worth knowing mostly for the permission it gives: the word can be taken apart without breaking anything. It was two words to begin with.

## Self-Regard

Self-regard is the recruiting substance, the one from the first experience. It is writing that works to raise the reader's opinion of the author, and once named it is easy to spot: the credential drop, the prediction remembered after it came true, the process tour starring its author, the humblebrag. There is even a quick test for a suspect sentence. Swap in a different subject and see whether the sentence survives. A sentence whose real cargo is "the author is impressive" survives any subject, because the subject was never doing the work.

What makes the forms so durable is that they are cheap. William James worked out self-esteem as a fraction, successes over pretensions, and noticed that a person keeps a different social self for every audience that carries an image of him — which explains the economics of the credential drop: it inflates the claim side of the fraction without adding any work to the top. Karen Horney added the running cost. The idealized image and the actual seeing draw on the same finite energy, so every hour spent maintaining how the work looks is an hour taken from the looking. And the present culture tilts the ledger further: when the self is run as a small enterprise, self-promotion stops feeling like a vice and starts feeling like compliance, and leaving the credential out feels like under-pricing an asset. The pressure is real. It is also why work that visibly declines the sale reads, now more than ever, like relief.

## Selfhood

Selfhood is what the second piece was missing. It is the person as the instrument that did the seeing — taste, stakes, angle, history, refusals — and on the page it shows up as judgment: this cut and no other, this example kept, that claim turned away. Judgment is personal by nature; no two people cut a subject the same way. So when the person is scrubbed out, the judgment goes with them, and what remains is complete, correct, and shaped by nothing. It reads as compiled. "Anyone could have done it" is the exact symptom, because the choices only one particular person would make are precisely what got sanded off.

The writers who describe this substance keep reaching for instrument words rather than personality words. Joan Didion, in the essay that calls writing "the act of saying I," spends her I entirely on aiming — the self is where the camera stands, and every sentence of it points outward at the subject. John McPhee gets there in three words, "writing is selection": the author everywhere in the choosing and nowhere in the sentence. Rick Rubin's version is the filter — one person's taste and accumulated experience, the only component of the work nobody else has — and Mary Karr's is voice, the audible shape of one specific mind remembering things nobody else was there for. The clinical literature even has a picture of the substance's absence from the inside: Winnicott's false-self patients, whose accomplishments perform perfectly and feel dead. That pairing — visible competence, felt deadness — is as good a description of compiled prose as any critic ever wrote.

## The Line-Level Test

Held apart like this, the two substances reduce to one working question, asked of any doubtful sentence: does it change what the reader sees of the subject, or only what the reader thinks of the author? A failing described with enough lived specificity that a reader recognizes their own version is selfhood doing knowledge-work. The same failing arranged to display the author's honesty is self-regard, whatever it is wearing.

The question is old enough to have been discovered several times. Strunk and White's first rule of style is "Place yourself in the background" — send the reader's attention to the sense and substance of the writing, never to the mood and temper of the author — and White himself, years later, cheerfully called the essayist a congenitally self-centered person, a pair of positions that only fits together if ego names two things. Steven Pinker's version asks where a sentence points the reader's gaze, out at the world or back at the writer, and adds the kindest available caveat: a lot of author-intrusion is not vanity but the curse of knowledge, the writer narrating his profession because he can no longer imagine not knowing it. Carl Rogers asked whose standard a sentence answers to, an internal one or an imagined audience's. The Adlerians put it as a matter of whose business is whose: the reader's opinion of the author is the reader's task, and a sentence written to manage that opinion has wandered off its own job. Four vocabularies, one question — which is some evidence the question is real.

## Humility as Costume

The test earns its keep at the humble end, because self-regard also dresses down. A confession staged for an audience — self-criticism arranged so the reader will notice how forthcoming the author is being — is self-regard in a hair shirt, and readers feel it the same way they feel a boast. This has been measured. A seven-study research program on humblebragging found that disguised self-promotion does worse than the open kind on every count: liking, competence, even willingness to help. A plain braggart is at least credited with sincerity; the humblebragger loses the boast and the honesty at once, and the reader's penalty lands on the staging rather than the ambition.

The costume comes in every temperature. Leslie Jamison named the cool one, the post-wounded voice: irony and studied numbness about one's own pain, which look like the opposite of confession and are still aimed at the audience's verdict — armor fitted in advance against a charge the writer saw coming. Brené Brown named the warm one, floodlighting: intimate material dumped on the reader to force a reaction, disclosure doing the work of a demand. Direction is what separates all of these from the honest article. A real piece of failings-writing points at the failing so the reader can use it, or overhears a note the writer wrote for himself; the staged versions all point, whatever their temperature, at the author.

There is also a name for overcorrecting until nothing is left. Craig Malkin calls the far pole echoism: the fear of seeming self-absorbed grown so strong that a person can no longer state a need or take up space. Prose can have the condition too. A page scrubbed of its author down to the last trace has the writing version of it — and has given up exactly the substance a reader came for.

## Writing the Self Honestly

None of this forbids writing about yourself; the essay tradition exists because the self can be the subject, and its founders worked the two substances out in practice. Montaigne opened the form's founding book by promising his own portrait, defects and all — "it is myself I paint" — and in the same preface refused what he called borrowed beauties, the ornament he would have reached for had he been courting the world's favour. Maximum selfhood and renounced self-regard, in one paragraph, in 1580. Virginia Woolf compressed the whole discipline into a phrase, "never to be yourself and yet always," and called personality the essay's essential material and its most dangerous antagonist in the same breath.

The modern craft books turn the intuition into working method. Vivian Gornick splits every personal narrative into situation and story — what happened, and what the writer has come to understand — and locates the failure of raw confession precisely there: it delivers situation with the story missing. Phillip Lopate has the writer turn himself into a character, rendered in the round and shown wrong, with dignity named as the admission price of the reader's trust; put next to that, performed humility shows its real shape, a dignity-preserving move wearing a dignity-surrendering costume. And Melissa Febos, defending the genre from inside, draws the same line once more: narcissism is building a self-image that pleases you; honest self-writing keeps interrogating past the versions that flatter.

## The Case Against the Split

The split has critics worth taking seriously, and three of their objections bite. The first says the self-audit cannot be trusted: the mind is a press secretary, confabulating respectable motives for whatever it was already doing, so the writer most in need of the test is the least equipped to run it on his own intentions. True — and it is why the test is phrased against the sentence rather than the soul. What a line does to a reader is checkable by someone else; what the author meant by it is not. The second objection says the status drive cannot be zeroed, only re-denominated: strip out the credentials and the display resurfaces as conspicuous restraint, ostentatious taste, humility played as a move. Also true, and worth folding in rather than fighting — zero self-regard is a direction to keep walking, and knowing the drive changes currencies is most of the walking. The third objection is the oldest and grandest. Eliot wanted the artist extinguished in the work, a catalyst leaving no trace, and Kohut answered from psychoanalysis that the wish to be admired never disappears anyway; it matures, into creativity, empathy, humor. Read closely, both dissents smuggle the person back in. Eliot's catalyst is still "a particular medium," a mind in which experiences combine in ways peculiar to it, and Kohut's matured transformations are a portrait of selfhood under another name. The split survives as a working test even where it loses as a metaphysics.

## The Generic Floor

All of it lands harder now than it would have a decade ago, because the floor moved. Fluent, organized, competent prose is no longer scarce; models produce it on demand, feeds flatten what is left, and "anyone could have done it" now includes machines in "anyone." What the floor cannot produce is the layer that traces to one life — a specific failure, a specific stake, a specific refusal — and the taste that selects with it. The scarce good was never polish. It was always a particular person's judgment, and that has simply become obvious.

The cleanest way to say what is at stake splits attention from recognition. Attention is value-neutral and extractable; anything loud can win it, and any interchangeable, impressive-sounding author can win it. Recognition is being seen in full by someone whose seeing you value, and it cannot be taken, only made possible. Self-regard bids for attention. Selfhood is the precondition for recognition. The swap test compresses into that pair — attention survives a subject swap, recognition does not.

Which returns the word to where it started, split. The dislike "ego" earns belongs to self-regard alone; the flatness of scrubbed work is a selfhood deficit alone; and the single dial the shared word implies would have a writer trade one failure for the other forever. Montaigne broke that dial in his century, Orwell broke it in his — sheer egoism at the desk, a windowpane on the page — and the rule that falls out has stayed the same for four hundred years. Selfhood as high as honesty allows. Self-regard at zero, held as a direction. And for the sentence in doubt, the one question: what does it change for the reader — because if the honest answer is "only what they think of me," it goes, and because attention was never the thing worth being seen for.
