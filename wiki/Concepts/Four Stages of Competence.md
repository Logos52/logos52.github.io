# Four Stages of Competence

The Four Stages of Competence is a model of how a skill improves. First the learner does the skill wrong without knowing it. Then the learner sees the mistakes and cannot yet fix them. Then the learner does the skill right when concentrating. Last, the learner does the skill right without thinking about it. The model names those four points as unconscious incompetence, conscious incompetence, conscious competence, and unconscious competence.

Each stage has a different kind of improvement available in it. Most of the work of learning a skill happens inside a stage. Little of it happens at the crossings between stages. The next practice block is chosen by the stage the skill is in.

## Core takeaways

- Unconscious incompetence is doing a skill wrong with no picture of what correct looks like. Most skills sit in this stage, including the skill of learning itself.
- Conscious incompetence is seeing the mistakes before being able to fix them. Improvement starts in this stage, and confidence usually drops because the mistakes are now visible.
- Conscious competence is doing the skill right with effort. The stage runs from two successes in ten with total concentration to nine in ten with little effort, and crossing it is most of the visible work of skill development.
- Unconscious competence is correct execution as the habit. Attention is freed for refinement and for new techniques.
- Each stage has one kind of gain that fits it: a standard, caught mistakes, lower effort with higher consistency, or refinement. Working for a gain that fits another stage produces frustration and no progress.
- The model is useful in daily practice and is not scientifically robust for complex skills. A skill that runs automatically can still be wrong.

## The four stages

**Unconscious incompetence (UI).** The learner does the skill wrong and has no picture of what correct looks like. Often the learner does not know that a correct way exists, so the learner feels confident without any basis for it. Most skills sit in this stage, including the skill of learning itself. School rarely teaches working learning strategies. Habits built alone come with no standard to compare against. Confidence without basis, together with no picture of correct, keeps a skill in this stage.

A learner working alone has one exit from this stage. The learner can say that the same person ten years from now would do this skill better, and that statement is already obvious. The gap is already visible. The learner already knows that unknowns exist. No coaching program is needed for this step.

**Conscious incompetence (CI).** The learner sees the mistakes and cannot yet do the skill right. Mistakes become visible as patterns before they become fixable. Real improvement starts in this stage. How long a skill stays in this stage varies a great deal. A simple mechanical fix can pass through in minutes. An instrument or an athletic skill can stay in this stage for years. Cognitive and learning skills under deliberate, well-sequenced training stay longer than the learner wants and shorter than a musical instrument.

Confidence usually drops on entering this stage, because the mistakes are now visible. The drop is a real experience. It is a separate thing from the research finding about how unskilled people rank themselves. [[Encoding and Retrieval]] explains the drop as a memory fact. [[Confidence Calibration]] explains how to read the drop and keep practicing through it.

**Conscious competence (CC).** The learner does the skill correctly when applying effort. This stage covers a wide range. At the low end, the learner succeeds two times in ten, and only when conditions are right and concentration is total. At the high end, the learner succeeds nine times in ten with little effort. Those ratios are pictures of the range and are not trial results. Crossing the range is most of the visible work of skill development. It takes many cycles of practice and reflection.

**Unconscious competence (UC).** Correct execution is now the habit. Effort is minimal, consistency is high, and doing the skill the old wrong way would itself take effort. Attention is freed. Refinement and any new technique become affordable, because the old technique no longer needs deliberate attention. [[wiki/Concepts/Global Workspace and J-space|Global Workspace and J-space]] describes this handoff: execution has left the deliberate workspace.

## The gain that fits each stage

Each stage has one kind of gain available in it. Working for a gain that belongs to another stage produces frustration and no progress.

| Stage | The gain that fits this stage |
|---|---|
| UI | A standard. The learner learns to see right versus wrong before execution improves |
| CI | Caught mistakes. The learner finds as many distinct wrong ways as can be seen. The mistake count falls while the result is still not right |
| CC | Lower effort and higher consistency. Success rate and effort are the two things to measure |
| UC | Refinement and personalization. The learner tunes a working default into a version that fits and builds the wider system around it |

In CI the gain is seeing mistakes. The result does not improve much yet, and most of the outcome benefit arrives near the top of the stage. A learner who watches only outcomes usually abandons the skill in this stage.

In CC, success rate and effort change together, from two in ten with strain toward nine in ten with ease. Those two ratios are pictures of the same range.

In UC the working default is already running. The remaining work is to adapt this version to the learner and to build the wider system around it.

## Most of the work happens inside a stage

Competence is a spectrum, and each stage covers a range of it. Most of the work happens inside a range. Little of it happens at the crossings between stages. The usual mistake is to read the long middle of a stage as failure. The long stretch of CC, where the skill is right only with effort and only on some days, is the work itself.

Working for a gain from a different stage is a mistake of the same kind. Two examples: working for fluency while the standard is still missing, and working for a result while the mistakes are still being learned. [[Metacognition - The Control Layer|Metacognition as a Skill]] covers the trainable skill of reading which stage a skill is in and choosing a gain sized to that stage.

## How many techniques to work on at once

A new technique is added only when the existing set of techniques is at least approaching unconscious competence. Five techniques all held at low CC add their effort costs together into overload. Their low consistency lowers motivation at the same time. [[Marginal Gains|Pacing Skill Development]] gives the how-many-at-once rule that this capacity limit sits inside.

## What this model tracks

This model is for tracking skills. Knowledge is tracked with mastery levels. Mapping understanding onto competence stages confuses both diagnostics, because knowledge types and competence stages are different tools. [[Knowledge Mastery - From Recognition to Usable Knowledge]] covers the knowledge side. A fact is never mapped onto UI.

## Where each gain comes from

[[Marginal Gains]] describes the path of improvement: the next improvement small enough to see, then the next one, stacked. [[Kolbs Experiential Cycle]] is the procedure that produces each gain: what happened, how it felt and why, what rule that suggests, and what changes next time. The four stages say what kind of gain each step on the path is. Looked at closely, an improvement curve is a stack of those cycles, and each cycle works for the gain that fits its stage.

[[Rapid Skill Acquisition]] describes an automaticity signal: execution speeds up without the learner trying to speed up. That signal marks the top of CC and the move into UC.

## What the model does not claim

The model is useful in daily practice. It is not scientifically robust for complex skills. No trial establishes the four stages as discrete natural kinds. No psychometric validation exists for the model, and daily use does not need one. A subtly wrong skill that has become automatic does not fit the four stages. It looks like UC, and it is still UI, because the learner cannot see the error.

The closest research model has three phases: cognitive, then associative, then autonomous. It describes the same movement and makes no claim of clean crossings between phases. It is a related model. The four stages stay the working model.

The time spans given for CI and the two-in-ten and nine-in-ten ratios are this system's teaching pictures. They are not research findings. A popular chart describes how unskilled people rank themselves against others. That chart does not describe the confidence drop on entering CI. The drop on entering CI is a real experience.

Two patterns lead to quitting. The first is adding a technique while every current technique still takes full concentration. The second is reading a long middle stretch as evidence that the method failed. One test can be checked before a practice block: the learner can name the stage the skill is in and the gain that fits it.

## Automatic does not mean finished

Automatic means this version of the skill runs without effort. It does not mean the version is right. The next practice block is still chosen by the stage the current version is in. The block aims at one of four gains: a standard, a caught mistake, a lower-effort success, or a refinement of something already running. The gain matched to the stage is what the hour of practice is for.

## How to practice this

1. Before a practice block, name the stage the skill is in and the gain that fits it. If you cannot name both, the block has no target yet. Notice whether the block you planned aims at that gain or at a different one.
2. Ask whether a version of you ten years from now would do this skill better. If the answer is obviously yes, a gap is already visible. That visible gap is the exit from unconscious incompetence, and no coaching program is needed for it.
3. In conscious incompetence, count the distinct mistakes you can see instead of watching results. Notice the mistake count falling while the result is still not right. That falling count is the gain for this stage.
4. In conscious competence, track how often you succeed and how much effort each success takes. Notice both changing together, from success only with strain toward success with little effort.
5. Before adding a new technique, check whether the current set is close to running without effort. If it is not, wait. Adding now stacks the effort costs into overload and lowers motivation.
6. When a skill runs without effort, ask whether this version has been checked as right. Automatic means only that the version runs without effort, so it may still be wrong. Choose the next block by the stage this version is in.

## Sources

- Burch, N. / Gordon Training International (1970s). Four stages for learning any new skill. The public model with the four stages.
- *Management of Training Programs*, New York University (c. 1960). Sometimes cited as an earlier public source for the same four stages.
- Fitts, P. M., & Posner, M. I. (1967). *Human Performance*. Cognitive, associative, autonomous: the related research model, the same movement in three phases, no claim of clean crossings.
- Kruger, J., & Dunning, D. (1999). Unskilled and unaware of it. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*. Relative ranking of the unskilled. Not a result about a confidence drop on entering CI.
- Gignac, G. E., & Zajenkowski, M. (2020). The Dunning-Kruger effect is (mostly) a statistical artefact. Later work narrowing the popular picture.
- Anderson, J. R. (1982). Acquisition of cognitive skill. Compilation and automaticity behind the last stage of the progression.
