# Four Stages of Competence

Competence is a spectrum, not a switch. You are not simply able or unable. A skill moves through four stages, sometimes called the four stages of learning or the four stages of competency. The model is often mislabeled as Maslow's, which it is not. It shows what kind of progress to expect at each stage, and what kind of gain is even available there. Learning itself is one of the more complicated skills you can pick up, so the same ladder applies to study methods as it does to an instrument or a sport.

## Unconscious incompetence

At the bottom you cannot do the skill correctly, and you have no idea that this is so. You may not know what correct looks like, or even that a correct form exists. Most people live here for most skills.

This is why someone can feel very confident very early. They cannot see the mistakes they are making, and they cannot see how much better the skill could be. Combined with overconfidence, that blindness is dangerous. You are closed off to the possibility of getting better because you do not believe you need to.

A simple check cuts through the special-case feeling that this applies to everyone else. Would the person you become 10 years later, if you kept exploring, be more efficient and know more about how to learn than you do today? If the answer is yes, those later gains are already proof of gaps still hidden from you.

On learning skills in particular, a decade or more of ordinary schooling often still leaves people here. What schools teach about study is frequently outdated, weak, or missing, so people assemble methods from friends and videos without a standard for what good looks like. They only know whether a method sort of works for them.

## Conscious incompetence

The next stage is where real improvement can start. You are still getting it wrong, and now you can see that you are. You may begin to notice patterns in the errors. However hard you try, you cannot yet produce the correct form.

For some skills this stage lasts 10 minutes, 15 minutes, or 20 minutes. You see the mistake, you learn a new way, and you can already do the new way. For athletics and instruments it can last years. You know the piece is not quite right for a long time before your body can produce what your ear already hears. Some thinking and encoding skills sit in between. You know how the move is supposed to work, and it still does not click when you try it. Those skills should not take years. Months of directed practice is a more typical stretch.

## Conscious competence

At this stage you can do the skill correctly if you apply effort. The band is wide. Being able to do it at all is not the same as doing it correctly, consistently, and easily. Both ends, and everything between them, sit inside this one stage.

Near the bottom, success is rare and expensive. If you tried 10 times, you might succeed on 2 attempts out of those 10 tries, and only when conditions line up and you concentrate hard. Near the top, success is common and cheap. The same 10 attempts might succeed 9 times, with little effort, almost like a habit. Moving from the bottom of this band to the top takes a long run of practice, review, and small improvements stacked on each other.

## Unconscious competence

At the top the correct form has become automatic. The effort required is minimal or close to none. Doing the old, incorrect form would itself take work. The skill has been retrained until the new form is the default. That is the place that frees attention for the next technique, and for refining the system you already have.

## Do not pile skills that still cost a lot

If too many skills sit at the low end of conscious competence, the load adds up. Five techniques that each take high effort and succeed only now and then can drain motivation, because inconsistency feels like a lack of progress, and can push you into overload. You should not work on that many high-effort skills at once. Add a new technique only when the ones you already use are approaching unconscious competence.

Dumping a large pile of methods at once works against this. A workshop that introduced more than 40 techniques for managing time left people remembering that there were many methods, not how to use them, and was more likely to lower motivation than to raise it.

The engine that moves you up the ladder is a cycle of trying, reviewing, and adjusting. A view of progress as many small gains helps you see movement even when a single session does not feel like a leap. The stages tell you what kind of gain is realistic. The cycle is how you earn it.

## Open questions

How would I know whether I am still blind to my mistakes, or finally able to see them?

How many of the techniques I am using still take high effort and succeed only now and then?
