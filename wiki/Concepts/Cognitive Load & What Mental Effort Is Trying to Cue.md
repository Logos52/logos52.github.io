# Cognitive Load & What Mental Effort Is Trying to Cue

Cognitive load is how much your brain is holding and working on at one moment, and mental effort is what that load feels like from the inside. Reading the feeling correctly settles a daily decision: whether to keep going, slow down, or unload something onto paper.

## Core takeaways

- Effort tells you what kind of work you are doing at that moment.
- Learning needs load: reading that feels easy and smooth is usually storing very little.
- Load that feels like confusion, asking where a thing fits and how it is like what you know, is the load that builds memory.
- Feeling stuck means you are holding too much at once; thinking harder adds fatigue and no clarity.
- Feeling overwhelmed means you do not know what to do next; the fix is to pick the one thing to do next.
- How much anyone can hold at once is the same for everyone. Experts feel less effort because their knowledge is already sorted; they hold no more than anyone else.

## How it works

- Working memory is the bench where the brain holds the things it is thinking about right now.
  - It keeps an item for about 15 to 30 seconds unless you do something with it.
  - It holds only a few things at once, roughly three to seven, and one dense term can weigh as much as seven digits.
  - Five factors that affect each other exceed it, because the relations between them count as items too.
- Storing something for the long term happens on that bench, inside those seconds, and it costs effort.
  - Sorting a new piece into what you already know is where the effort goes; once the piece is placed, the effort drops.
  - Learning rises with load up to a threshold, then falls. Below the threshold nothing is stored; above it nothing useful comes out.
- The feeling of effort tracks how much is on the bench and how much sorting is going on.
  - Bored or drowsy: almost nothing on the bench, the passive mode where most study time is spent.
  - Confused, asking questions: sorting is happening, the productive load.
  - Stuck, going in circles, tired after each pass: too much on the bench at once, overload.

```
load    low          middle           high
feel    bored        confused         stuck
stored  nothing      most             nothing
do      add work     stay             unload
```

## Reading the signal

- Easy and smooth: add work. Test yourself, paraphrase, compare, teach it, draw it. Which move matters less than leaving passive mode.
- Confused: stay. This is the expected feel of a technique that stores things, and being lost with a new one is normal.
- Stuck: cut what is on the bench. Take two or three factors, settle how they connect, write that down, then bring in the next.
- Overwhelmed by many demands: treat it as a triage cue. Ask which single blocker, if removed, would make the rest simpler, and work on that one.
- Intake outrunning processing: stop taking in more. Pause whatever you are watching or reading and digest what is already there before taking the next piece.

## How to apply it

- Handle each new item at once, inside the 15 to 30 seconds: connect it, simplify it, make an analogy, ask how it relates to the last thing.
- Use paper as a second bench. A connection written down no longer has to be held, so the head is free for the next one. When a decision feels tangled, write down every factor and draw which ones block which.
- Do not write everything down as it arrives. That offloads the load before any sorting happens, so you get a record and no memory.
- Grow tolerance slowly: hold a little more before writing, then a little more, toward 15 to 30 minutes of reading before notes.
- Take on one or two new techniques at a time. A dozen new techniques, each with two or three points to watch, is overload by itself.
- Block background speech and numbers. They take bench space even when ignored.
- Give hard thinking a long block. A tangled problem given only ten minutes feels overwhelming, and the short block is part of the cause.
- Build the habit of noticing first: mark passive or active as you study, notice each drift into passive, and switch. Detecting the drift reliably takes about a month at ten or more hours a week; switching reliably takes another week or two.
