# Cognitive Load & What Mental Effort Is Trying to Cue

Cognitive load is how much your brain is holding and working on at one moment, and mental effort is what that load feels like from the inside. Reading the feeling correctly settles a decision that comes up many times a day: whether to keep going, slow down, or unload something onto paper.

- Effort shows what kind of mental work you are doing.
- Easy, smooth reading usually stores very little.
- Confusion about where a thing fits is the load that builds memory.
- Feeling stuck means you are holding too much at once.
- Feeling overwhelmed means you do not know the next step.
- Everyone can hold about the same amount at once.
- Experts feel less effort because their knowledge is already sorted.

## How it works

Working memory is the part of the mind that holds what you are thinking about right now. It keeps an item for about 15 to 30 seconds unless you do something with it, and it holds only a few things at once. Storing something for the long term happens there, inside those seconds, and it costs effort. Most of the effort goes into sorting a new piece into what you already know, and once the piece is placed the effort drops.

- Working memory holds roughly three to seven items.
- One dense term can weigh as much as seven digits.
- Five factors that affect each other overflow it.
- The relations between them count as items too.
- Learning rises with load up to a threshold, then falls.
- Below the threshold nothing is stored.
- Above it nothing useful comes out.

The feeling of effort tracks how much is being held and how much sorting is going on. Boredom or drowsiness means almost nothing is being held, which is the passive mode where most study time goes. Confusion with questions running means sorting is happening. Feeling stuck, going in circles and tired after each pass, means overload.

```
load    low          middle           high
feel    bored        confused         stuck
stored  nothing      most             nothing
do      add work     stay             unload
```

## Reading the signal

Each feeling points to a different response, so the first step is naming which one you have. Thinking harder while stuck adds fatigue and no clarity. Being lost with a new technique is normal, because confusion is how a technique that stores things is expected to feel.

- Easy and smooth: add work.
- Test yourself, paraphrase, compare, teach or draw it.
- Leaving passive mode matters more than which move you pick.
- Confused: stay with it.
- Stuck: take two or three factors and settle how they connect.
- Write that down, then bring in the next factor.
- Overwhelmed: find the one blocker whose removal simplifies the rest.
- Intake outrunning processing: pause and digest before taking more.

## How to apply it

The general approach is to handle each new item while it is still in working memory, and to use paper as a second place to hold things. A connection written down no longer has to be held in the head, so the head is free for the next one. Writing everything down as it arrives has the opposite effect, because it unloads before any sorting happens and leaves a record with no memory behind it.

- Handle a new item within 15 to 30 seconds.
- Connect it, simplify it, make an analogy, relate it to the last one.
- For a tangled decision, write every factor and draw what blocks what.
- Hold a little more before writing each time.
- Build toward 15 to 30 minutes of reading before notes.
- Take on one or two new techniques at a time.
- A dozen new techniques at once is overload by itself.
- Block background speech and numbers, which take space even when ignored.
- Give hard thinking a long block, since ten minutes makes it overwhelming.

Noticing comes before switching. Mark passive or active as you study, notice each drift into passive, and switch. Detecting the drift reliably takes about a month at ten or more hours a week, and switching reliably takes another week or two.
