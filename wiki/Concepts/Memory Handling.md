# Memory Handling

> What looks like a poor memory is usually poor handling of memory. Raw capacity is similar for most people; champions use trained strategies anyone can learn. Information lands in a short workspace measured in seconds and weighted by complexity, not in “seven slots” but in how heavy each item is. Six moves make that window productive: raise process complexity, handle material immediately, pause so handling can finish, block distracting sound, practise retrieval at real cues, and think on paper so the workspace is not asked to hold the whole structure alone. Learning also has two halves (encoding stores knowledge, retrieval uses it), and they trade off over months: lead with retrieval while encoding is weak, then shift the balance as encoding improves, without ever dropping retrieval to zero because memory always leaks.

## The short workspace

Short-term memory lasts about 15 to 30 seconds. The exact span depends on how complex the material is and how familiar you are with it, but it is measured in seconds, not hours. Early research already pointed toward a span of seconds, and later work has stayed close to that range. The longer store that holds what you keep is long-term memory. If you reach the bottom of a page 2 minutes later and cannot remember the top, that is not a short-term memory failure. Two minutes already belongs to that longer store. The first window has already closed.

That short window is the reason the workspace exists. New information enters through the senses and lands there first. You then have 15 to 30 seconds to work with it, shape it, and make it mean something. If the result is vague, it tends to drop into the forget pile. If you form something clear and meaningful inside that window, it can pass into long-term memory. A better name for this workspace is **working memory**, because the point is the work you do there, not the brief storage.

Working memory also has a weight limit. An older estimate treated this as an item limit of about seven things at once. A more useful picture is weight. Seven light items, such as short digits, can fit. One heavy item, such as a dense term you have to unpack every time you see it, can fill the same space. Read hundreds of words in 30 seconds and you will not retain them all, because you exceeded the weight the workspace can hold. When load exceeds weight, [[wiki/Concepts/Cognitive Load & What Mental Effort Is Trying to Cue|cognitive overload]] shows up and useful output stops.

## Six ways to handle it better

**Raise process complexity.** Rereading, copying a line a few times, or merely intending to understand are simple processes. They do not transform much. A more involved process extracts key words and ideas, restates a concept in easier language, builds an analogy, connects the idea to something you already know, judges how important it is for your purpose, asks what problems it would help you solve, or prepares you to teach it. Those moves engage the mind more actively and turn raw input into something more meaningful, which you are more likely to keep later.

**Handle the information immediately.** A common habit is to consume everything first and make sense of it later. That habit assumes the material will still be waiting in long-term memory when you come back. It usually will not. You read, you understand in the moment, and days later you cannot recall enough to start from anywhere but the beginning. As soon as something enters working memory, do something with it. Get curious, connect it, teach it, or simplify it inside those 15 to 30 seconds. Spending about 10 extra seconds handling it now saves the later time you would have spent relearning what you forgot.

**Pause so the handling can happen.** If more information keeps arriving while you are still working on the last piece, you exceed the weight limit and enter cognitive overload, where the mind can no longer produce useful output. Respect the limit. Pause the video, stop reading, and give the workspace time to build meaning. Then open the gate for the next piece. This start-and-stop rhythm raises the amount of lasting memory you get from each hour of study.

**Keep distracting sound out of the workspace.** Other words and numbers occupy space even when you are not trying to listen to them. Someone reciting random numbers beside a person doing mental arithmetic steals capacity from the sum. If people talking or other noise is eating attention you need for active thought, do not try to push through it. Move, or block the sound, so working memory can do the job you sat down to do.

**Practise retrieval.** Working memory is also the place where long-term knowledge is pulled back. A memory can be stored well and still fail to come when you need it. That is retrieval failure, not a storage failure. Practice using the knowledge at the level of fluency you will actually need. The practice shows gaps and errors in the original encoding, and those errors can then be corrected and stored again. Be careful about the cues you practise with. If you will need the knowledge to answer a hard question, teach it, solve a messy problem, or make a decision, and you only practise with flashcards, the cues do not match. You may recall the card and still fail to bring the knowledge forward in the setting where it matters. That cue mismatch is a common weakness of flashcards as a main method.

**Think on paper.** Holding several related ideas in your head at once blows past the 15-to-30-second weight limit. Notes, sketches, and diagrams create a second surface the workspace can look at. Once a connection is written down, working memory no longer has to hold it. It can focus on the next small comparison or link, then put that on the page too. The growing record shows how the ideas fit, and the quality of what gets stored goes up because the workspace was never asked to carry the whole structure alone. [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing|Deep processing]] methods live on that external surface.

## Encoding and retrieval as one system

Learning has two halves. **Encoding** gets information into long-term memory. **Retrieval** is any later use of that information (self-testing, teaching, explaining, solving problems, discussing). Both matter, but they pay off on different timescales.

Retrieval skills take a day or two to learn and improve results quickly, so they come first. Encoding is the bigger long-term lever, raising both retention and depth, but it takes weeks to years to get good at. Early on, retrieval should fill most of study time (often 70% or more), because weak encoding lets knowledge leak away. About half of new material can be gone after a week without reinforcement. As encoding improves, retrieval’s share falls to under 20% for an experienced learner, but it never reaches zero. Some retrieval always remains, because memory always leaks.

Picture knowledge as water in a bucket with a hole. Better encoding shrinks the hole, but the hole never closes completely. Retrieval scoops up what drips out and tips it back in. It is repetitive and takes time, but it keeps performance up while encoding slowly improves.

Retrieval also exposes invisible misunderstanding. A learner can misread something between reading it and testing it without knowing. Testing finds gaps. The cycle runs: test, find gaps, re-encode by studying again, test later to confirm the gap closed. Stronger encoding cuts how much retrieval you need; stronger retrieval finds gaps and guides re-encoding more effectively.

### Where study time goes at first

For most learners, retrieval should take 70% or more of study time at first, and that is normal. A weekly pattern spreads the work across sessions of different depth:

- At the start of the week, prestudy the concepts and how they relate, with a very basic map and no memorising.
- After each class day, revise that day, deepen understanding, extend the map, and make flashcards for details.
- At the end of the week, revise everything with an interleaved retrieval method, return to weak spots, and start memorising details.
- In spare moments, run flashcards.
- At the end of the month, go over the month’s material using a different interleaved method.

Leading with retrieval has a cost. Retrieval does not raise baseline retention by itself, so it involves repetition, and at first total hours can creep up a little. That is the price of keeping knowledge while the leak is large. Gaps also cost more the longer they go unfound, because knowledge builds in layers and a mistake made early warps every layer that rests on it. Testing soon after learning keeps that cost low.

When time allows only one move, solid prestudy beats going back over old material, because better prestudy means less is forgotten and less review is needed later.

### Spacing reviews

A simple spaced schedule reviews material after roughly a day, a week, and a month. The first review comes within a day or two of learning. About 7 to 10 days later comes the second; 3 to 4 weeks after that, the third. The timing does not need to be exact. The schedule works best as fixed blocks (such as a half-day block closing each week and a day or so at the end of each month). Heavy content calls for an extra mid-month or mid-week review. The weeks before a test call for more practice papers and challenge questions.

As encoding gets better, less leaks out, so retrieval shrinks from most of study time to under a fifth for an experienced learner. The month-end review starts to feel surprisingly easy because little has been forgotten. **Fluency** becomes the main retrieval target: how fast information comes back and how complex the uses it supports. Some facts can simply be looked up, but knowledge meant for real expertise needs high fluency, and the reason for learning something sets the level needed.

[[wiki/Dimensions/Retrieval/Spaced Interleaved Retrieval|Spaced interleaved retrieval]] is the standing practice for this rhythm.
