# Riding the AGI

AGI, artificial general intelligence, is AI that can do most tasks a person can, and riding it means working with AI models while they improve month by month. The method is to use the newest tools every day, stay ready to switch tools, and expect any edge to last weeks. It comes from a July 2026 conversation between the investor Naval Ravikant and three founders who run AI agents in their daily work, and it changes how long a bet on one model, one product or one skill can be expected to pay.

- A lead from the best model now lasts weeks.
- Open models trail the best closed models by months.
- Software is getting cheap to build, so it is hard to own.
- Small errors compound, so judgment work gets the smartest model.
- People who use AI daily report having more work.
- Use the newest tools now to see where they are going.

## How advantages shrink

For most of the history of software, a company that built something good kept its lead for years. With AI models the gap between the leader and the rest closes fast. The best open models were about twelve months behind the best closed ones, then nine, then six, and some now put the gap at three months, uneven from task to task. Whoever has the smartest model has it for a few weeks before others catch up, and a few months after that a free version exists.

- The open-model gap shrank from twelve months toward three.
- Open models cost five to ten times less to run.
- Once open software takes the lead, it tends to keep it.
  - Other tools get built around it, as happened with Linux.
- A plan can only look a few weeks ahead.

## What gets cheap and what stays scarce

The founders sort the AI business into layers by how easy each one is to copy. Hardware is a commodity, and most of it is made in China. Software is becoming one too, since a coding agent can build most programs from a clear description in hours. AI research stays scarce, because it needs huge computer clusters, rare data and a small number of researchers, so a few companies hold it.

- Hardware: cheap and mostly made in China.
- Software: cheap once it can be described clearly.
- AI research: scarce, costly, held by a few labs.
- General models tend to beat models built for one field.
  - A tool sold for one profession risks losing to them.
- Money raised by labs mostly buys computing power.

## Why the smartest model is worth paying for

When an agent runs many steps in a row, the error rate of each step multiplies with the next. A model that is right 90 percent of the time on each step, run for 100 steps, almost never finishes cleanly. A model right 99.9 percent of the time on each step finishes cleanly about 90 percent of the time. That arithmetic is why the founders pay for the strongest model on decisions and keep cheaper models for repeated simple tasks.

- 90 percent per step over 100 steps: near zero.
- 99.9 percent per step over 100 steps: about 90 percent.
- Cheap models suit repetitive, low-stakes work.
- Cost, more than intelligence, limits running agents at scale.
  - One founder cut the cost per user from $100 to $2.84 monthly.

## How to ride it

The working method is to live in the future and build what is missing, a rule from the investor Paul Buchheit. In practice that means using the tools every day, even when the first results are poor, so you know what they can do before others do. Coding agents became practical for most people around December 2025, and the people who use them report being busier and more productive than before. The speed of the change is the risk, since farm work fell from half of US jobs to 2 percent over about a hundred years, and the move to AI may take far less time.

- Use the newest tools daily to learn their limits.
- Keep workflows easy to move to a better model.
- Refusing the tools is the likely path to being replaced.
- Human wants still decide what gets built.
- The founders invest in AI companies, which shapes their view.
