# Accuracy Before Speed

Accuracy before speed is a rule for work and practice: do the task right at a pace you can hold, and let speed come on its own as the method settles. It settles a decision that comes up every day, whether to push harder on a familiar task. Pushing buys almost nothing, because the errors it adds cost more time than the extra speed saves.

## Takeaways

- On familiar work, the unused speed is only 5 to 10 percent.
- One error costs about four times doing the work right once.
- The four-times figure is a practitioner's estimate, unmeasured by any study.
- A rush that adds errors loses time overall.
- The one speed lever with real range is a better process.
- Practise a new technique for accuracy alone.
- Speed that arrives without pushing shows a method has settled.

## The arithmetic

On a familiar task, most people already work at 80 to 90 percent of the fastest pace their quality bar allows. Going faster raises the error rate, and most people can feel where their own rate starts to climb and sit just under it. Each error then costs time four ways: noticing it, rebuilding the context around it, redoing the work, and unpicking whatever was built on top of it. To pay for 10 percent more errors at four times the repair cost, a rush would have to be about 40 percent faster, and the unused band is only 5 to 10 percent.

- Errors stack and hide where they started.
- A learner who holds 1+1=3 goes on to hold 1+1+2=5.
- The comparison is within one method, calm against rushed.
- Even an efficient method loses when rushed.
- A better method can be several times faster than a worse one.
- Over a day, a rush can look productive.
- Over months, it yields the same work and a more tired worker.
- Tiredness raises the error rate further.

```
calm pace   -> base errors -> work done once
rushed pace -> 10% time saved
            -> 10% more errors x 4 repair = 40% added
            -> net: about 30% more time
```

## Learning a new method

A new technique is practised for accuracy only, with no steps cut to save time. Time falls by itself as the checks find fewer gaps. Careful practice feels slow, with fewer pages covered and more of them held, and fast shallow practice is paid back later as relearning. Relearning a move that was learned wrong runs about three times slower than learning it right the first time, on the same kind of estimate, because each move has to be watched for the old version.

- Errors in foundations hide for weeks, then show up everywhere.
- A rush makes the learner feel more sure, so errors go uncaught.
- Weeks on the calendar do not count as practice.
- Two months without running the method is no practice.
- Two weeks of heavy accurate practice can be enough.
- Measure what was practised and whether a check runs without strain.
- Staying on a level too long costs a little work.
- Moving on too early is what happens by default.
- When unclear, stay another week or two.

## Agent work

The same rule applies to AI agents that write code or documents. Accepting an agent's output unchecked saves minutes now and costs about four times as much later. The cost is steeper than in study, because later work builds on the bad output. More gets done through a better harness, meaning the prompts, checks and patterns the agent runs inside.

- Check agent output before building on it.
- Improve the harness to get more done.
- Watching more agent jobs at once is the same rush.

## Hustle

When a person is trying hard, the pause that checks the method gets skipped. Productive means moving toward the goal, so rest, a walk or an hour with people counts as productive when focus or mood is the limit. A level that can only be held by grinding shows the method is wrong. The fix is to accept the short-term loss and build a better method.

- Stop now and then to check whether the method is right.
- Rest counts when focus or mood is the limit.
- Grinding to hold a level means the method needs changing.

## What is known

Published research supports the direction of the rule. It measures error rates under speed and the cost of software defects found at different stages. No study measures the time it takes to repair a learning error, so the four-times figure remains a teaching number.

- Faster responses produce more errors.
- Schedule pressure raises the defect count in software.
- A defect found late costs more to fix than one found early.
- No study measures repair time for learning errors.

## Sources

- Fitts 1954, speed–accuracy tradeoff: faster responses produce more errors. Support for the direction of C8, not for a study-repair multiplier.
- Wickens, *Engineering Psychology and Human Performance*; Pachella 1974: same SAT family. Bound: the finding is error rate under speed, not repair-time arithmetic.
- McConnell, "Software Quality at Top Speed": excessive schedule pressure associated with up to 4× defects. Different claim: defect *count*, not time-to-repair a learning error.
- IBM Systems Sciences Institute (widely cited); Boehm defect-cost curve; Labovitz & Chang 1992: late defects cost more than early ones (design-to-release ~4–5×; earlier phases up to 100×). Different 4x: phase of discovery. Fits agent-output better than study errors.
- Newell & Rosenbloom 1981, power law of practice; Fitts & Posner 1967, autonomous stage: hands get faster once the method is known. Support for unforced speed as a habituation mark.
- Bouton 2004: extinction is not unlearning. Support for early-automated errors being expensive to reverse.
