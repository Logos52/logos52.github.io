# Selfhood and the Ledger

Many people decide whether to speak in a meeting or a group chat by counting what speaking might cost them: bothering others, looking foolish, being judged. They rarely count what their words would give the group, so the count favours silence nearly every time. Studies in social psychology find that people overrate how much others notice and judge them, and that groups lose information when members keep quiet.

- People count the costs of speaking and skip its value.
- Others notice and judge us less than we think.
- Others like us more after a talk than we guess.
- Staying quiet costs the group something too.
- Many people privately agree with what nobody says aloud.
- Groups miss facts held by only one member.

## How the ledger works

Picture a ledger with two columns, one for what speaking costs and one for what it delivers. Most people fill in only the first column. Meiwaku, a Japanese word for causing trouble to others, works this way when avoiding it becomes a rule for speech, since every sentence then counts as a small imposition and nothing it adds gets entered. Selfhood, as the word is used here, is a person's own judgment on a subject, shown by what they choose to say, and a ledger with one column keeps that judgment unsaid.

- Cost column: taking time, seeming pushy, being wrong in public.
- Value column: a fact, an objection, a view others share.
- Silence has a cost too, since the group decides without it.

## Why the costs are overestimated

Several well-tested findings point the same way. People believe they are watched more closely than they are, and that their nervousness is easier to see than it is. After talking with a stranger, they guess the stranger liked them less than the stranger did. The cost column is inflated before anything is said.

- Spotlight effect: others notice our looks and slips less than we fear.
- Illusion of transparency: nervousness shows less than it feels.
  - Speakers told this before a talk gave better talks.
- Liking gap: partners like us more than we guess.

## Why the value is underestimated

The value column is hard to see because a contribution that was never made leaves no trace. When people misread what others think, each stays quiet on a view most of them hold. Groups also tend to talk about what everyone already knows, so a fact held by one person often never reaches the decision. Surveys show the gap in numbers, and correcting the misread has changed what people do.

- Pluralistic ignorance: each person thinks they are alone in a view.
  - US adults backed climate policies at 66 to 80 percent.
  - They guessed support was 37 to 43 percent.
- Hidden profiles: groups discuss shared facts and miss single-held ones.
- Spiral of silence: people speak more when they expect agreement.
  - A 2014 Pew survey: 86 percent would discuss an issue in person.
  - Only 42 percent of social media users would post about it.
- Telling people the real norm has changed their behaviour.

## How to use it

Before staying quiet, fill in the second column. Ask what the group loses if the point stays unsaid, and whether others might be holding the same view. Expect the cost column to feel larger than it is.

- Name what your point adds before judging its cost.
- Assume some others share the view.
- Share the fact only you hold first.

- Stasser & Titus (1985), *JPSP* 48(6), 1467–1478: hidden profiles; meta-analysis in Mesmer-Magnus & DeChurch (2009).
- Hampton et al. (2014), "Social Media and the Spiral of Silence," Pew Research Center: the in-person versus platform figures (86% / 42%), the recruitment figure (0.3% of those unwilling in person), and the perceived-agreement multipliers (2.92× at work, 1.91× on the platform); N = 1,801, ±2.6.

Effect sizes reported in secondary summaries of several of these studies were left out deliberately: the citations are confirmed, the magnitudes were not re-verified, and only the direction of the findings is relied on.
