# Meiwaku

Meiwaku (迷惑) is the Japanese word for trouble or inconvenience caused to other people. Japanese children are taught not to cause it, which is why people on a Tokyo train keep their voices low and do not take phone calls. The owner uses the idea as a personal rule: before doing something, count what it costs the people around you.

## Core takeaways

- Meiwaku means trouble you cause other people.
- Every choice puts some cost on others: noise, space, attention, upkeep.
- Counting those costs is a habit of consideration.
- A message costs its reader attention, so keep it short.
- Staying silent also has a cost when others needed the information.
- Keep the action when what it gives outweighs what it costs.

## How to use it

The rule is a quick count before acting. Ask what a choice will take from other people: their quiet, their room, their time, or the work of looking after something you leave behind. Then ask what it gives them. The action goes ahead when what it gives is larger, and it gets smaller or quieter when what it takes is larger.

```
action --> what it takes from others
       --> what it gives them
               |
     gives more?  yes --> do it, at the lowest cost
                  no  --> shrink it or drop it
```

- Noise: a phone call in a shared space.
- Space: things left in a common area.
- Attention: a long message where a short one would do.
- Upkeep: a possession someone else will have to look after.

## Where it fails

Counted only on the cost side, the rule always says do less, and applied to speech it always says stay quiet. A person who follows it that way stops asking questions, sharing news and warning others, and the people around them lose what they would have learned. Withholding a useful fact is also a trouble caused to others, so it belongs in the count.

- Count what an action gives as well as what it takes.
- Silence that hides a needed fact causes trouble too.
