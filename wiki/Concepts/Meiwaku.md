# Meiwaku

迷惑 (めいわく, *meiwaku*) is the Japanese word for unnecessary trouble caused to other people. The trouble takes the form of noise, space, attention, time, and energy that your presence exports to everyone nearby.

Used as a habit, the word becomes a form of social cost accounting. You count the burden you put on other people and price it before you act. The count applies at every scale, from where to stand to how to live. The goal is presence without unnecessary imposition.

## Core takeaways

- Meiwaku names trouble you cause other people that you did not need to cause: noise, space, attention, time, and energy.
- The habit is to count that burden before you act, cut the part that serves no purpose, and keep the part that does.
- The keep step is required. A loop that cuts everything turns consideration into self-removal.
- The norm is different from politeness. Politeness covers the person in front of you; the count covers people who never chose to deal with you.
- In Japan the word covers how you behave in public. The home, neighbourhood, workplace, and life-plan tiers are an extension made in this knowledge base.
- The habit fails in four ways: erasing yourself, resenting others without saying why, treating every inconvenience as a moral failure, and mistaking a tidy look for a low burden.

## What the word means in Japan

In Japan the word names the burden itself. The phrase *meiwaku o kakenai* means do not impose avoidable inconvenience. It is a consideration norm, one of several behind the everyday discipline around quietness, cleanliness, punctuality, and restraint.

The norm is wider than politeness. Politeness governs how you treat the person in front of you. The count covers people who never chose to deal with you, and what your presence costs them.

## Three tiers of the count

The count can be run at three tiers.

- Micro: how you walk, speak, park, and consume in public.
- Meso: home, neighbourhood, and workplace.
- Macro: life plans, career, and family structure.

The micro tier is the native Japanese usage. The meso and macro tiers are an extension made in this knowledge base. They apply the same count to larger decisions, and they are not part of what the word means in Japanese.

## The counting loop

The count happens before the act. It does not wait for a complaint. First, name the burden the act exports: noise, space, attention, maintenance, or risk. Then cut the portion that serves no purpose. Then keep the portion that does. After the loop you are still present, and the burden you put on others is smaller.

The keep step is required. Without it the loop cuts everything, and the practice becomes self-removal instead of consideration.

## Everyday examples

At street level the burden is usually treated as personal preference until its social cost is counted. A loud exhaust or an oversized vehicle exports noise and spatial risk to a whole block. A bag on a train seat makes every boarding passenger navigate around it. Performative or attention-hungry communication uses up collective attention and returns no value. A poorly designed or cluttered system costs every user mental effort on every visit.

## Ignorance and open defiance

The strongest reaction to a breach is reserved for open defiance, and never for ignorance. Someone unaware of a custom gets patience. Someone who visibly enjoys imposing, using noise, space, and attention as a status display, gets contempt.

The full failure sequence has four steps. A person wants status, convenience, or self-expression. The person pushes the cost of it onto the shared environment. Dominance through noise, space, and attention becomes normal. The commons is degraded for everyone in it.

## Four ways the habit goes wrong

A careful reader is most likely to fall into the first one, self-erasure.

- **Self-erasure.** Avoiding all impact in order to avoid all burden: suppressing speech, action, creativity, and self-advocacy until nothing is left to impose. The keep step of the loop already rules this out. The count also prices the other side. [[wiki/Concepts/Good Faith|Good Faith]] counts hidden information as an imposition on the person who needed it. The full accounting for speech, what a contribution returns and when the silence is the imposition, is in [[wiki/Concepts/Selfhood and the Ledger|Selfhood and the Ledger]].
- **Quiet resentment.** Expecting others to follow the norm without ever naming it. When the count is not shared, the other person's behaviour looks like rudeness to you, and your reaction looks like arbitrary judgment to them. The repair is to convert the value into explicit rules: a stated request, a house norm, or a design constraint.
- **Over-policing.** Treating every inconvenience as a moral failure. The count targets repeated, avoidable, high-cost burdens. Simply being present is not a burden to count.
- **Aesthetic minimalism.** A visually quiet space can still export noise, shifted work, and poor function. The count measures burden. Appearance is not part of it.

## Practices that do the same work

Two practices carry out the habit directly. Recessive [[wiki/Concepts/Style|Style]] is a wardrobe that does not draw attention and an interface that does not compete with its message. It asks no attention for itself, which leaves room for the substance, or the person, to be noticed. [[wiki/Minimalism/Minimalism as Systems Design|Minimalism]] cuts exported burden by owning less: less storage and maintenance for you and your household, less spatial and visual load on shared space, and fewer objects that make work for someone else.

## Five questions before you act

1. Am I taking more space, time, or attention than the situation needs?
2. Does this object or habit create ongoing work or friction for someone else?
3. Is my communication useful, or is it primarily demanding attention?
4. Would removing or quieting this thing meaningfully improve the shared environment?
5. What burden am I exporting that I could internalize or eliminate?

In Japan the word means the trouble you did not need to cause. The counting habit tracks two costs. One is the burden a presence exports. The other is the cost of a presence withheld.

## How to practice this

1. Before an act, name the burden it exports: noise, space, attention, maintenance, or risk. Notice which of those you had been calling personal preference.
2. Cut the part of the burden that serves no purpose. Notice how much of the act is left after the cut.
3. Keep the part that serves a purpose. Notice any urge to cut that part too. That urge is the start of self-erasure.
4. Run the five questions before the act. Notice which of the five names a burden you had not counted.
5. When you expect someone to follow the norm, state it as a request, a house norm, or a design constraint. Notice whether they still read your reaction as arbitrary judgment.
6. Count the cost of staying silent alongside the burden of speaking. Notice when the silence is the imposition on the person who needed the information.
