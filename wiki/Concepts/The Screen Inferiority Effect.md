# The Screen Inferiority Effect

People understand a text they are studying slightly better on paper than on a screen, and the main cause is the reading habit they bring to the screen. Most screen reading is messages, news and captions that ask for nothing afterwards, so people skim. A reader who knows this can learn from a screen as well as from paper by choosing to read deeply.

## Takeaways

- Paper beats screens by a small, steady margin for study.
- The gap vanishes for stories and for untimed reading.
- Screens train a habit of shallow reading.
- Screen readers overrate how much they understood.
- Told to think harder, screen readers match paper readers.
- Scrolling, long lines and alerts add smaller costs.

## When the gap appears

Studies comparing paper with screens go back decades and find a small, steady benefit for paper. The benefit does not appear everywhere. It shows up when the text is read to learn from, when there is a time limit, and when the text runs longer than about 500 words, especially where the reader has to scroll. Most real study meets all three conditions.

- Novels and stories: no difference.
- No time limit: little or no difference.
- Long, scrolling, informational text: paper wins.
- E-readers and tablets: the gap shrinks or disappears.

## Why paper wins

Four causes have been proposed, and the fourth matters most. Working memory, the part of the mind that holds new information while making sense of it, is small. Anything that takes a share of it, such as an advert, a message or a decision about where to click, leaves less for learning. The first three causes are about the screen, and the fourth is the reader's own habit.

- Distraction: alerts and navigation use up working memory.
- Position: printed words stay put, so the mind remembers where each was.
  - Scrolling moves the text and breaks that memory of position.
- Line length: very long lines make the next line hard to find.
  - About 50 to 70 characters per line reads best.
- Habit: people read screens shallowly because screen text rarely needs more.

## The habit and the false signal

The screen inferiority effect is the name researchers give to that fourth cause. Learning needs effortful processing: linking a new idea to ones already known and using it on a new problem. The screen habit skips that work. Screens also give more signs of progress than paper, such as a scrollbar moving down and links clicked, and those signs make reading feel effective when little has been stored. When screen readers are told to think harder about the text, they score as well as paper readers.

- Recognising a fact while reading it feels like knowing it.
- Recalling it later without the text is the real test.
- Slowing down to compare and connect ideas feels slower and stores more.

```
screen habit:  skim -> scroll -> "got it" -> little kept
deep reading:  read -> connect -> check recall -> kept
```

## Books at home

One large study looked at the homes children grew up in and counted the books. Children from homes with many books went on to more schooling, and the difference held after allowing for the parents' education, jobs and class. The leading explanation is that a home full of books builds the habit of reading deeply, which is the same habit that screens wear down.

- About 70,000 people across 27 countries.
- About three more years of schooling for children raised among books.
- The effect appeared in every country.
- It grew up to about 80 books and then levelled off.

## How to read from a screen

A book is still the easier choice for a long study project, because it removes distraction, keeps the text in place and sets a good line length without any effort from the reader. When a screen is the only option, the reader can set up the same conditions by hand. The window and the printout can be set up once, and the deep reading has to be chosen again at each sitting.

- One window open, nothing else on the monitor.
- Narrow the window to about 45 to 70 characters per line.
- Print anything that will take a long time to read.
- Stop at each section and ask how it connects to the last.
- Test recall with the text closed before moving on.
