# Declarative, Procedural, and Conditional Knowledge

Declarative, procedural and conditional knowledge are three names for three different things a person can hold about one skill: what it is, doing it without thinking, and when to use it. Keeping the three apart tells a learner which kind of practice to do next when a skill that is fully understood still fails in use.

## Core takeaways

- Declarative knowledge is anything that can be explained: facts, concepts, and every step that is still chosen on purpose.
- Procedural knowledge is execution that runs with no attention on the steps, like driving. It is built only by doing, and it comes slowly.
- Conditional knowledge is knowing when and why to use what is held. On this desk it counts as part of declarative knowledge, since the when and the why can be explained.
- Most failures that look like a lack of procedure are declarative gaps: the learner does not know how to approach the problem.
- Read the gap from the error before choosing practice. A wrong method, a wrong order or a broken chain of reasoning is declarative. The right method done clumsily is procedural.
- Conditional knowledge grows from practising the same skill in changed contexts. It does not grow from more of the same practice.
- Explanation teaches the declarative part. Only the attempt teaches the other two, so start the attempt as soon as the theory is enough to find the first mistakes.

## The three kinds

- Declarative: what the thing is, what it does, and how it is done, in words.
  - The plan and the reasoning count too. Taking the facts of a case, picking a diagnosis and writing a plan is choosing every step, so it is declarative from end to end.
  - Content subjects lean declarative almost entirely: history, law, science, theory of any kind.
- Procedural: running the skill with no attention on the running.
  - The closest picture is muscle memory. Being able to say how a thing is done is still declarative.
  - Thinking has it too: a practised way of taking a problem apart that runs without being chosen.
  - A physical skill needs it, since there is no time to decide each movement. A cognitive task with no time pressure can be done with none, one paused decision at a time. There, procedure buys speed, and over time accuracy.
  - Once it locks in, the explanation can fade: a fluent speaker who cannot state a grammar rule.
- Conditional: when and why to use a given method.
  - Researchers do not agree that it is a separate kind. Some teaching work treats the when and the why as something to teach on its own.
  - The line drawn on this desk: conditional is a subset of declarative, and it arrives as a by-product of building the other two across varied contexts, challenge types and time pressures.

| Kind | Held as | Best teacher | Gap looks like |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Declarative | explanation, chosen steps | being told, notes, critique | wrong method, wrong order, broken reasoning |
| Procedural | automatic running | the attempt, repeated | right method done clumsily |
| Conditional | when and why | the attempt in changed contexts | right method known, not reached for |

## Diagnosing the gap

- Suspect declarative first. Even in subjects that look procedural, what usually goes wrong is deciding which method to use and in what order, and that decision is declarative.
- A learner who finishes the usual practice set and then falls apart when the same problem arrives inside a real case is missing a concept. More of the same set will not supply it.
- Essay example. The ideas connect and the examples fit, but the prose reads stiff: procedural, fixed by more writing. The prose reads fine, but the logic of the argument is off: declarative, fixed by reworking how the ideas connect. Rereading the topic does not fix the first, and writing more essays does not fix the second.
- "I know the topic but I am bad at writing the answer" does not hold. With the knowledge and the skill in place, the answer follows, so a failed answer marks a gap somewhere.
- Practice follows the diagnosis. Retrieval methods split along the same declarative and procedural line, so the kind of gap chooses the method.

```
attempt a messy problem
        |
wrong method, wrong order,     yes    declarative gap:
reasoning breaks?  -------------->    re-explain, re-plan
        | no
        v
right method, clumsy run?      yes    procedural gap:
                   -------------->    repeat the attempt
```

## How each kind is built

- Declarative comes from explanation: notes, analogies, connections, critique.
- Attempt the skill as soon as the theory is enough to discover the first mistakes. Certainty before the first attempt is not available, and more clarifying questions only postpone the teacher that works.
- Find mistakes early, while they cost little. A mistake found half a year before it counts costs little. The same mistake found in the last week costs a lot.
- A skill may offer hundreds of possible mistakes, and any one person makes about five to seven of those mistakes. Trying to head off the whole list with what-if questions overloads working memory and stalls the start. The start is what shows which five to seven.
- Some habits leak in from another skill and are invisible from inside. An observer sees them.
- After each attempt: compare the result against the explanation, refine, retry.
- Conditional knowledge is built by changing the context on purpose: same skill, different angle, different constraint, different time pressure. Practising the same way each time gives progress for a while and then a plateau, because the thing holding the learner back now is no longer the thing that held them back at the start.
- A learning log with three entries tracks the practice: what is being practised, why in this way, and what was learned from it. Nothing learned means the practice needs a new angle.

## Where this desk's cut differs from the field

- The field often treats applying knowledge as a mix of declarative and procedural, and treats the when and the why as teachable in their own right. The cut on this page is a diagnostic tool, not a consensus definition.
- Varied practice is a technique for conditional knowledge, so "it develops for free" is too strong.
- Five to seven mistakes per person is a teaching number, not a measurement.
