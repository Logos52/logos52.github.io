# Declarative, Procedural, and Conditional Knowledge

Declarative, procedural and conditional knowledge are three names for three different things a person can hold about one skill: what it is, how to do it without thinking, and when to use it. Keeping them apart tells a learner which kind of practice to do next when a skill that is fully understood still fails in use.

## Core takeaways

- Declarative knowledge is anything that can be explained.
- Procedural knowledge runs with no attention on the steps.
- Procedural knowledge comes only from doing, and slowly.
- Conditional knowledge is knowing when and why to use a method.
- Most apparent procedure failures are declarative gaps.
- Read the gap from the error before choosing practice.
- Start attempting once theory is enough to find the first mistakes.

## The three kinds

Declarative knowledge covers what a thing is, what it does and how it is done, in words, including every step that is still chosen on purpose. Procedural knowledge is running the skill with no attention on the running, the way an experienced driver drives. Conditional knowledge is knowing when and why to reach for a given method. Researchers do not agree that it is a separate kind, and on this wiki it counts as part of declarative knowledge, since the when and the why can be explained.

- Planning a case from its facts is declarative from end to end.
- History, law and science lean almost entirely declarative.
- Muscle memory is the closest picture of procedural knowledge.
- Being able to say how a thing is done is still declarative.
- Thinking has procedure too: a practised way of breaking down problems.
- Physical skills need procedure, since there is no time to decide.
- Untimed mental tasks can be done one paused decision at a time.
- There, procedure buys speed and, over time, accuracy.
- Once locked in, the explanation can fade.
- A fluent speaker who cannot state a grammar rule is one example.
- Conditional knowledge arrives from practice across varied contexts.

| Kind | Held as | Best teacher | Gap looks like |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Declarative | explanation | being told, notes, critique | wrong method or order |
| Procedural | automatic running | repeated attempts | right method, clumsy |
| Conditional | when and why | attempts in new contexts | right method not reached for |

## Diagnosing the gap

Suspect a declarative gap first. Even in subjects that look procedural, what usually goes wrong is deciding which method to use and in what order, and that decision can be explained. A learner who finishes the usual practice set and then falls apart when the same problem arrives inside a real case is missing a concept, and more of the same set will not supply it. The kind of gap then picks the practice, since the ways of practising recall split along the same declarative and procedural line.

- Essay with good logic but stiff prose: procedural, so write more.
- Essay with smooth prose but broken logic: declarative, so rework the ideas.
- Rereading the topic does not fix stiff prose.
- Writing more essays does not fix broken logic.
- "I know it but write it badly" marks a gap somewhere.

```
attempt a messy problem
        |
wrong method, wrong order,     yes    declarative gap:
reasoning breaks?  -------------->    re-explain, re-plan
        | no
        v
right method, clumsy run?      yes    procedural gap:
                   -------------->    repeat the attempt
```

## How each kind is built

Declarative knowledge comes from explanation: notes, analogies, connections and critique. Procedural and conditional knowledge come only from attempts, so the attempt should start as soon as the theory is enough to reveal the first mistakes. Certainty before the first attempt is not available, and more clarifying questions only put off the attempt. A skill may offer hundreds of possible mistakes, and any one person makes about five to seven, so trying to head off the whole list with what-if questions overloads working memory and stalls the start.

- The first attempt shows which five to seven mistakes are yours.
- A mistake found months early costs little.
- The same mistake found in the last week costs a lot.
- Habits leaked in from another skill are invisible from inside.
- An observer can see those leaked habits.
- After each attempt, compare against the explanation, refine, retry.
- Build conditional knowledge by changing angle, constraint or time pressure.
- The same practice each time gives progress, then a plateau.
- The plateau comes because what holds the learner back has changed.
- Keep a log: what is practised, why this way, what was learned.
- Nothing learned means the practice needs a new angle.

## Where this split differs from research

Learning research often treats applying knowledge as a mix of declarative and procedural. It also treats the when and the why as something that can be taught in its own right. The split used here is a tool for diagnosing errors, and researchers have not agreed on it.

- Varied practice is a technique for conditional knowledge.
- So conditional knowledge does not develop entirely for free.
- Five to seven mistakes per person is a teaching number.
