# Social Media - Curvilinear Design & the Theft of Time

Social media feeds are built so that people keep scrolling without noticing time pass, and the layout ideas come from casinos. A half hour on a feed leaves few memories, so the day looks shorter afterwards and the lost time is hard to account for. Knowing how the design works shows where to put back the points of choice that make a person notice the time.

## Core takeaways

- Time feels long when you are alert or remember a lot.
- Feeds lower alertness during use and memory afterwards.
- Casinos avoid sharp corners because corners make people decide.
- Infinite scroll and autoplay removed the end of the feed.
- A feed has no story, so little of it is remembered.
- Put deliberate stops and new experiences back into the day.

## How time is judged

People judge time in two ways. In the moment, time drags when they are alert to it, as in an airport wait. Looking back, a period seems long when many memories are attached to it, which is why a holiday passes quickly and then feels long afterwards. A feed shortens both, since attention stays low while scrolling and almost nothing is stored.

- In the moment: more alertness, slower time.
- Looking back: more memories, longer period.
- Users underestimate their time on apps within minutes.
- Constant alarming posts come to feel routine, and routine is forgotten.

## The casino design

In the 1970s Bill Friedman, a gambler who became a casino manager, wrote the design books the casino industry used. He laid casinos out as mazes, with even the paths to the toilets winding past machines. He wanted paths with as few right-angle turns as possible, because a corner forces a choice of direction, and a choice makes people think about the time and the exit. Social media feeds copy this layout on the screen.

- Curved paths with no corners keep people on autopilot.
- Early feeds had an end, which worked as a corner.
- Infinite scroll and autoplay removed that end.
- Links placed off-topic pull people away from what they came for.
- Notifications pull people back, as cheers from a nearby table do.

```
corner path              curved path
-----+                   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
     |  <- decide here   no point to decide
     +-----              keeps walking
```

## Why little is remembered

Stories are easy to remember because each part follows from the one before. A feed gives each post no link to the next, so there is no order to hold it by. People also rate an experience as more fun when they believe time flew, which makes scrolling feel enjoyable while little of it stays. Switching attention between the feed and the rest of life adds a cost of its own, and people lose track of time even off the app.

- Unconnected posts give memory nothing to follow.
- Time flying gets read as having fun.
- Switching back and forth breaks attention off the app too.
- Quiet rest after learning helps memory, and scrolling is no rest.

## How to take time back

Quitting social media can make time feel longer quickly, and in one study of 35,000 people it slightly improved mental health within weeks. The saved time often moved to other apps with the same design, including chatbots that end each answer with a new offer. The lasting fix is to add corners to the day: moments of choice, new experiences, and activities with a story. Surprising events feel longer than routine ones, so even small changes to a routine help.

- Open the phone only with a clear thing to see.
- Given a choice, pick what is most likely to become a story.
- Change small routines: a new route, a new restaurant.
- Ask why you are doing something, and stop without an answer.
- Keep reminders that time is limited where you scroll.
