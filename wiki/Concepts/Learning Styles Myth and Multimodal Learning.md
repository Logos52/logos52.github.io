# Learning Styles Myth and Multimodal Learning

The learning styles idea says that each person has one channel that suits them best: seeing, hearing, reading, or doing. It also says that teaching should be matched to that channel. The preference half of the idea is real. There are channels you reach for and channels you avoid. The instruction half has not held up in testing. Matching material to a declared type does not outperform running the same material through several channels. The version that works is multimodal learning: the same content met in more than one way. A stated preference is then information about what you will enjoy. It is not a constraint on how the next hour of study gets spent.

## Core takeaways

- Preferences for seeing, hearing, reading, or doing are real. They describe comfort and set no ceiling on what you can absorb.
- Studies built to test matching find no matching effect on immediate tests and none on later tests. Running the same material through several channels does better than matching.
- How engaged you are during an activity predicts learning more strongly than which channel carries the material. An activity you can perform while mentally absent is not doing the encoding.
- Turning a paragraph into an abstract doodle forces critical processing, re-representation, and rendering. Drawing beats writing on recall because of those operations.
- The left-brain, right-brain explanation that used to go with the advice to mix modes did not hold up. The outcome evidence for words plus pictures, and for drawing at encoding, holds without it.
- A style label gives permission to skip every other channel. That is the harm a label does.

## What the studies found about matching

The claim that was tested is that matching a declared style to a lesson improves learning. The study design that would prove that claim almost never appears. The studies that do use that design find no matching effect on tests taken immediately, and none on tests taken later. In one follow-up study, children who preferred visual material scored higher on both listening and reading. Preferences themselves are real. For some information you would rather see a diagram, hear an explanation, or read the text, and the preference shifts with the material. A preference is comfort. It is not an aptitude, and it gives no reason to match instruction to it.

## How a preference comes to feel like an identity

Schooling trains one channel until the preference feels like part of who you are. For more than a decade, almost everything arrives as text and is graded as text. Style tests then measure that conditioning and report it back as a type. The type is not a genetic finding. It is a trained feeling that a quiz has given a label.

Adopting a style label frames the brain as a fixed object. It also gives permission to skip every other channel. A student labelled auditory opts out of writing-heavy work. The label is the trait trap, the same move [[Fixed vs Growth Mindset]] names, applied to learning itself.

## Attention matters more than the channel

Being talked at is easy to drift through. Keeping your eyes on a page or a diagram makes drifting harder. That difference helps you choose an activity. It does not rank the channels: a live lecture can hold attention, and attention can wander during silent reading. How engaged the brain is during the activity predicts learning more strongly than which channel carries the material. Inattentive study in any mode encodes nothing. A technique is worth using when it is difficult to perform while mentally absent, because it forces you to re-represent, decide, and produce. Getting sleepy over study material when you are not sleep-deprived is another sign of the same problem: the technique has stopped engaging you. With no engagement there is no retention. [[Attention is Important]] states that rule for language. The rule applies to every mode.

## Two rules to use instead of matching

Preferences describe comfort. They set no ceiling on what the brain can absorb. Two rules do the work that matching was supposed to do.

First, pick activities by how hard they are to zone out of. Engagement outranks mode. The test is whether you can perform the activity while mentally absent. If you can, the activity is not doing the encoding.

Second, stack modes, and give extra weight to the channels schooling never trained. After a decade of text, more sentences add little. The untrained channel is where the largest gains come for the least effort.

## Drawing and other ways to re-represent the material

The useful direction is toward re-representing the material. Making a prettier copy of the page is not re-representation. Reading and then writing is the lowest step. Reading and then drawing a picture is a step up. An abstract doodle that re-represents the idea sits higher. Teaching the material aloud, hearing it explained back, or applying it as a live skill sits higher still. Drawing beats writing on recall. Teaching and self-explanation are moderate-utility moves. The full ordering is a judgment. No study measured the differences between those steps.

After years of text-heavy schooling, notes written as more sentences reuse a channel that is already at capacity. Doodles, symbols, and diagrams use a channel that has been idle. Turning a paragraph you have read into an abstract doodle forces three operations: process it critically, re-represent it in a different form, and render the result. The encoding happens in those three operations. The drawing itself matters very little. An abstract mark that forced the three operations beats an accurate copy that did not force them. [[wiki/Dimensions/Deep Processing/Mindmaps|Non-Linear Note-Making]] covers the craft of routing material through that visual channel.

Problem-heavy subjects supply the application layer through real problem-solving. Discussion supplies the auditory layer in both directions: explaining, and hearing the explanation back. Whatever mode carries the material, the processing it must serve is [[Higher-Order Learning]]: relationships, comparison, and use, not isolated recall. Drawing more, on its own, is not the advice.

## The left-brain, right-brain story and why it was dropped

The advice to mix modes used to come with a left-brain / right-brain explanation: one hemisphere handles critique, the other handles abstract and artistic work. That explanation did not hold up. Personality does not track hemisphere. Nobody has shown a cognitively dominant side that handles information as a whole. Motor and sensory laterality, such as handedness and visual fields, is real, and it is a different claim. Functions do activate distinct regions, and some of those regions sit on the opposite side from where the left/right account puts them. The multimodal benefit cannot be attributed to hemispheres rather than to cortices. What the outcome studies measured is words plus pictures, and drawing as an encoding act. Dual-channel processing is cognitive, verbal against pictorial. It is not hemispheric.

The advice to mix modes was tied to those outcome studies. The hemisphere account was only an explanation placed on top of them. Remove the explanation and the advice still stands. A habit follows from this that applies elsewhere. When the explanation for a recommendation breaks, keep the recommendation if outcome evidence still supports it, treat the explanation as a hypothesis, and drop any extra claim that existed only because of the broken explanation.

## What remains

Running the same material through several channels still beats matching a declared type. The hemisphere story that used to be given alongside that advice is gone. What remains is the two rules, and the harm a label does when it gives permission to skip a channel.

## How to practice this

1. Before a study activity, ask whether you could perform it while mentally absent. If you could, it is not doing the encoding, so pick a different activity. Notice when you get sleepy over material while not sleep-deprived, since the technique has then stopped engaging you.
2. Turn a paragraph you have just read into an abstract doodle. Process it critically, re-represent it in a different form, then render the result. Notice that the encoding comes from those operations rather than from the quality of the drawing.
3. Look at how much of your study goes through text. Route the next piece of material through a channel schooling did not train, such as doodles, symbols, or diagrams. Notice whether that channel gives more gain than adding more sentences would.
4. Explain the material aloud to another person, then listen as they explain it back. Notice whether you had to re-represent the idea in order to say it.
5. When a style label tempts you to skip a channel, name the work you would be opting out of. Do that work anyway, as a student labelled auditory would still do the writing-heavy work. Notice that the preference describes comfort and sets no ceiling on what you can absorb.
6. When the reason given for a study habit breaks, check whether outcome evidence still supports the habit. If it does, keep the habit and treat the reason as a hypothesis. Notice which extra claims existed only because of the broken reason, and drop them.

## Sources

- Pashler, H., McDaniel, M., Rohrer, D., & Bjork, R. (2008). Learning styles: Concepts and evidence. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 9(3), 105–119. The design required to test matching, and the finding that studies built to it do not support it.
- Rogowsky, B. A., Calhoun, B. M., & Tallal, P. (2015). Matching learning style to instructional method: Effects on comprehension. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 107(1), 64–78. First study built to that design; no matching effect on immediate or delayed tests.
- Rogowsky, B. A., Calhoun, B. M., & Tallal, P. (2020). Providing instruction based on students' learning style preferences does not improve learning. *Frontiers in Psychology*. Same design in fifth-graders; visual-preference children scored higher on both listening and reading.
- Riener, C., & Willingham, D. (2010). The myth of learning styles. *Change*. Students have preferences; preferences are not styles.
- Newton, P. M., & Salvi, A. (2020). How common is belief in the learning styles neuromyth, and does it matter? A pragmatic systematic review. 37 studies, 18 countries, weighted 89.1% of teachers still endorse matching. The 89% that exists is teacher belief, not a student identity-share.
- Nancekivell, S. E., Shah, P., & Gelman, S. A. (2020). Maybe they're born with it, or maybe it's experience. *Journal of Experimental Psychology*. Many people treat styles as innate.
- Nielsen, J. A., et al. (2013). An evaluation of the left-brain vs. right-brain hypothesis with resting state functional connectivity magnetic resonance imaging. *PLOS ONE*, 8(8), e71275. Lateralization is local; no whole-brain left/right cognitive style.
- Wammes, J. D., Meade, M. E., & Fernandes, M. A. (2016). The drawing effect. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology*. Drawing at encoding beats writing for free recall.
- Wammes, J. D., Jonker, T. R., & Fernandes, M. A. (2019). Drawing as a tool for memory. *Cognition*. The encoding lives in the multimodal operations, not in the quality of the picture.
- Mayer, R. E. Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning. People learn better from words and pictures than from words alone. Dual-channel is cognitive (verbal vs pictorial), not hemispheric.
- VARK official research statistics (vark-learn.com). Single read/write is a few percent; most people are mixed. Used only as the contrast that retired the 80–90% figure.
