# Learning Styles Myth and Multimodal Learning

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Learning styles, the idea that each person is a visual, auditory, reading and writing or hands-on learner, do not exist. Large, long-running studies tested the idea and found it false. People do have learning preferences, meaning they prefer certain kinds of information delivered in certain ways, but almost everything is learned more efficiently through several modes than through one. The factor that tracks learning most closely is how switched on the brain is, whatever the mode. Believing in a fixed style does harm, because it treats the brain as a fixed object. The practical rule is to mix modes on purpose, and especially to add visual work to notes, since schoolwork already leans hard on reading and writing.

## Core takeaways

- There is no such thing as a fixed individual learning style that a person must match.
- What remains after the myth is preference. A preference is not a limit, since every learner can learn in multiple modes.
- Mode matters less than attention. A technique works when it is hard to do with the brain switched off.
- Each added mode makes learning more effective, and notes are the easiest place to add a less used mode such as pictures, symbols and diagrams.
- A popular left-brain, right-brain story does not explain why drawing helps. Mixing modes still helps, whatever the reason.

## What the studies found about matching

The old model was VARK, for visual, auditory, reading and writing, and kinesthetic, the last meaning learning through experience and practice. Many people still describe themselves with one of those labels. When researchers tested the labels, most students came out as reading, writing and visual learners. That pattern reflects schooling, which leans heavily on reading, writing and visuals, and not a fixed trait. Auditory learning looks weak for a different reason. It is easy to zone out while listening and harder to zone out while reading or watching.

## How a preference comes to feel like an identity

What remains after the myth is preference. Some material a person likes to have explained aloud, some they like to read and some they like to see as a video, flowchart or diagram. A preference is not a limit, since every learner can learn in multiple modes.

Belief in a single style undercuts that. It treats the brain as fixed, when the brain can learn well across a very wide range of situations. Having preferences is fine, but the useful move is to use as many modes as possible.

## Attention matters more than the channel

Mode matters less than attention. Reading without paying attention is not effective, and neither is watching a video, drawing a picture, listening or running a chemistry experiment without attention. A technique works when it is hard to do with the brain switched off.

## Two rules to use instead of matching

Use as many modes as possible, rather than matching work to a single label. Prefer techniques that are hard to do with the brain switched off, because attention matters more than the channel.

## Drawing and other ways to re-represent the material

Each added mode makes learning more effective. Reading something and then writing about it teaches a little. Reading it and drawing a picture teaches more, and the picture works better still when it is a bit abstract instead of accurate. Teaching the idea aloud, hearing it explained and practicing it in real life where possible each add more.

Notes are where this is easiest to apply. Reading and writing already fill most study, so notes made only of words use the brain in the way it is already being used. Cutting words and adding pictures, symbols, doodles, shorthand and diagrams brings in a less used mode. Visuals also act as landmarks in memory and make notes more personal. Turning a paragraph into a doodle means reading it critically, working out a different way to represent it and then drawing that, which is far more work than copying it.

The product matters less than the process. Other people may not understand a set of doodles, but their maker has a strong connection to them because of the thinking that went into imagining and drawing them.

## The left-brain, right-brain story and why it was dropped

A popular explanation for why doodles help puts critical thinking in one hemisphere and abstract, artistic thought in the other, so drawing would recruit both halves. Current evidence does not back that account. Different brain regions do have different functions, yet personality has no tie to either hemisphere. A person may favor one side for movement and the senses, but nobody seems to rely on one side for processing information.

## What remains

The evidence supports multimodal teaching without showing that the benefit comes from using both hemispheres. For a learner the mechanism does not change the advice, because mixing modes helps whatever the reason.

## How to practice this

- After reading a paragraph, represent it in a second mode, such as a slightly abstract drawing, a diagram or a short spoken explanation.
- In notes, cut some words and add pictures, symbols, doodles or shorthand, so the work is hard to do with the brain switched off.
- Do not treat a preference for one mode as a limit on how you study.
