## Sources

- Pashler, H., McDaniel, M., Rohrer, D., & Bjork, R. (2008). Learning styles: Concepts and evidence. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 9(3), 105–119. The design required to test matching, and the finding that studies built to it do not support it.
- Rogowsky, B. A., Calhoun, B. M., & Tallal, P. (2015). Matching learning style to instructional method: Effects on comprehension. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 107(1), 64–78. First study built to that design; no matching effect on immediate or delayed tests.
- Rogowsky, B. A., Calhoun, B. M., & Tallal, P. (2020). Providing instruction based on students' learning style preferences does not improve learning. *Frontiers in Psychology*. Same design in fifth-graders; visual-preference children scored higher on both listening and reading.
- Riener, C., & Willingham, D. (2010). The myth of learning styles. *Change*. Students have preferences; preferences are not styles.
- Newton, P. M., & Salvi, A. (2020). How common is belief in the learning styles neuromyth, and does it matter? A pragmatic systematic review. 37 studies, 18 countries, weighted 89.1% of teachers still endorse matching. The 89% that exists is teacher belief, not a student identity-share.
- Nancekivell, S. E., Shah, P., & Gelman, S. A. (2020). Maybe they're born with it, or maybe it's experience. *Journal of Experimental Psychology*. Many people treat styles as innate.
- Nielsen, J. A., et al. (2013). An evaluation of the left-brain vs. right-brain hypothesis with resting state functional connectivity magnetic resonance imaging. *PLOS ONE*, 8(8), e71275. Lateralization is local; no whole-brain left/right cognitive style.
- Wammes, J. D., Meade, M. E., & Fernandes, M. A. (2016). The drawing effect. *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology*. Drawing at encoding beats writing for free recall.
- Wammes, J. D., Jonker, T. R., & Fernandes, M. A. (2019). Drawing as a tool for memory. *Cognition*. The encoding lives in the multimodal operations, not in the quality of the picture.
- Mayer, R. E. Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning. People learn better from words and pictures than from words alone. Dual-channel is cognitive (verbal vs pictorial), not hemispheric.
- VARK official research statistics (vark-learn.com). Single read/write is a few percent; most people are mixed. Used only as the contrast that retired the 80–90% figure.
