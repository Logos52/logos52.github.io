# Good Faith

Good faith is the commitment to give the other person a true picture (real constraints, real intentions, and downsides you could have kept quiet) even when telling them costs you something now. The cost is what makes the disclosure believable. Anyone can say cooperative words, but a disclosure that could have hurt you is the one they can trust. Practised this way, good faith brings problems to the surface while they are still small, lets trust compound, and builds a reputation for meaning what you say. It is selective, covering the facts that change the other person's sense of fit, trust or understanding.

You can practise it in negotiation, feedback, close relationships and public work. A costly truth lands better when it comes with [[wiki/Concepts/Charisma|Charisma]]. Anti-marketing, which means showing limits before anyone invests, applies good faith to an audience that has not yet committed.

## What you disclose

Good faith discloses three kinds of fact: the constraint you cannot bend, the intention you are actually pursuing, and the downside you would rather hide. Hiding any of them takes work that never stops, because a deception, an omission or a managed impression has to be kept up for as long as it lasts.

A disclosure only signals trust when it could have cost you something. Telling someone what they could already see adds nothing, while telling them something that gives up your leverage is an act of cooperation.

Timing decides whether the same fact reads as a change of plan or as a betrayal. Told early, the problem can still be fixed. Told late, it arrives after the other person has already spent their trust on a version of events without it. In public work, the same rule means showing what is known, what is not, and what the work demands before anyone commits, which is anti-marketing applied to knowledge.

## Anti-marketing

Anti-marketing shows constraints, flaws, trade-offs, and weak points before the other party invests, instead of saving them for after a polished pitch. It asks who should still want this after seeing what it is, rather than how to look desirable to the largest audience. An early weakness reads as honesty, while the same weakness found late reads as concealment. Mismatch is cheapest at the start in hiring, dating, and product trials.

Naming limits raises credibility when the listener can use the information to decide fit. Reveal the facts that change fit, trust, or understanding, and leave out the rest. A page of limits with nothing to want fails as surely as a hidden flaw.

Anti-marketing fails in three ways: honesty as performance (humblebrag, strategic vulnerability), telling more than someone asked for, which burdens them, and negativity that lists only limits. [[wiki/Concepts/Selfhood|Selfhood]] and meiwaku (the Japanese habit of counting the trouble you cause others) cap the last one, because a burden placed on others is also an imposition.

## When to hold back

Extend good faith where the other person can return it. When your disclosures are used against you and none come back, stop volunteering leverage, but do not lie. Withholding shifts the burden to the person who needed the information, and meiwaku counts that cost too.

## How to ask for feedback

Asking for feedback is a disclosure too, because a good request shows your own view of the work. Feedback here means information about how you perform or what you understand that moves you toward a learning goal, and it helps with process goals (how you learn) as well as outcome goals. Its sources differ in reliability. A coach's comments are strong but slow, and peers are fast but uneven. Your own review is the recommended first response, and it is most valuable once your judgment is strong.

How you ask decides what you get back, since a vague or sweeping request produces noise. The suggested shape for a useful request is CLEAR, named for the five parts of a request.

1. Context covers anything in your situation that would change the advice, such as deadlines, constraints, and how far along the program you are.
2. The learning goal is the skill or process you are targeting, pitched at your current stage.
3. Evaluating your own work is the longest part. Say what you tried, what worked, what was hard, what surprised you and where improvement would help most, because "I don't know" is not enough.
4. The ask names one or two focused areas, since too many changes at once cannot land.
5. Review means absorbing the reply, following up on anything confusing, then experimenting and producing new work before asking again.

Receiving a reply well starts with analyzing it without rushing to disagree, and keeping comments on the work. From there, put the changes in order and plan experiments, since a single reply can fuel several tries. Giving feedback to peers trains your own judgment. Be specific, start from what works, stay on the work, and offer a next step.

## Practice

- Before a disclosure, ask whether the other person could already see it. Notice whether you give something up.
- Name a real constraint in negotiation you would usually bluff past.
- Tell a small problem early, and notice whether it lands as a change of plan or as a betrayal.
- List flaws and limits before the other person invests, then ask who should still want this.
- Shape the next feedback request with CLEAR, and close the loop with experiments before asking again.
- Track one counterpart: if leverage only flows one way, reduce volunteering without going false.
