# Learning Efficiency

Learning efficiency is how much usable, remembered knowledge you end up with for the hours you spend. Most people count pages or lectures covered per session, and that count says nothing about what is still in memory a week later. Measuring it properly tells you whether to study longer or to change how you study.

- Content covered per hour does not measure learning.
- Count what you still know and can use after a week.
- Measure at each level of use, from recalling a fact upward.
- Include every hour, reviews and relearning as well.
- If more hours stop raising what you retain, change how you study.

## The equation

The measure divides what you have reached by the time it took. What you have reached is how much you still recall a week later at each level of mastery an exam or a job asks for, set against how much it asks for at that level. The levels run from recalling a fact at the bottom to comparing, judging and applying at the top. The time is every hour spent on the subject, review sessions included.

```
efficiency  =  total mastery  /  total hours

partial mastery = one-week retention at a level
                  / retention required at that level

total mastery   = average of the partial masteries
```

- Pick the lowest and highest levels you will be tested on.
- Test your recall at each level one week after studying.
- Write down how much each level requires.
- Divide recall by the requirement for each level.
- Average the results.
- Add up all hours spent, including review.

## A worked example

A student preparing for a second-year economics exam is tested from level 1, recalling facts, up to level 4. After some weeks he measures his one-week recall at each level and compares it with what the exam needs. His weakest spot is the top level, where he has half of what he needs.

| Level | Recall | Needed | Partial |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 60% | 80% | 75% |
| 2 | 60% | 90% | 67% |
| 3 | 50% | 70% | 71% |
| 4 | 30% | 60% | 50% |

- Total mastery is the average of the four partials: 66%.
- He has studied 30 hours, so the result is 66% over 30 hours.
- With a weak method, 60 hours lifts him only to 76%.

## Time or method

The score tracked over time shows which problem you have. If the score keeps rising roughly in step with the hours, the method works and you need more time. If the score flattens while the hours climb, the method has a ceiling, and extra time will not reach the higher levels. Rereading and highlighting build recognition, and recognition does not turn into the ability to compare, judge or apply.

- A poor, shallow method: about 50% after 100 hours.
- A good, deep method: about 90% after 30 hours.
- Cramming looks better on the day and worse a week later.
- Train the kind of use you will be tested on.
- Lower targets are reached sooner.
- Set the target before you start.
