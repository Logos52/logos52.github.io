# Learning Efficiency

Most people treat learning efficiency as coverage. An "efficient" session, on that view, is one that gets through five lectures in two hours. That number ignores whether anything will still be there later. It ignores the extra time spent relearning what leaked away. It also ignores whether the person can use the knowledge in more than one way. Coverage per sitting is a poor measure.

What is worth measuring is how long it takes to reach high-quality, usable knowledge and to keep that knowledge at the level the task requires. Efficient learning means reaching the mastery you need, holding enough of it at each required level, and doing both in a short time. In one line, learning efficiency is overall mastery and retention divided by the hours spent getting them.

## The formula and where its numbers come from

Total mastery is the average of the partial masteries. Each partial mastery is the retention you actually have at one mastery level, divided by the retention that level requires.

The knowledge-mastery page defines the levels. This page only needs the highest and lowest levels your assessment actually asks for. After that, four more figures have to be gathered. First, measure how much you still hold one week later, as a percentage, at each of those levels. One week matches a spacing plan that includes a retrieval session at that mark, and it can be read forward to how much you are likely to hold for a test. Second, write down the retention each level requires for that test. Third, compute partial mastery at each level by dividing actual one-week retention by required retention. Fourth, add up the total time spent on learning and retrieval for the subject, across however many weeks the work has run.

Total mastery is the average of those partial scores. Learning efficiency is that average, stated together with the hours that produced it.

A short sketch of the difference is enough. An inefficient learner might reach 50 percent mastery over 100 hours, while an efficient learner reaches 90 percent over 50 hours.

## A worked example

A second-year university student is preparing for an economics exam. The exam goes as high as the fourth mastery level and sometimes asks for plain fact recall at the first mastery level.

After a couple of weeks, the student's one-week retention looks like this:

| Mastery level | Retention after one week | Retention the exam needs | Partial mastery |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 60 percent | 80 percent | 75 percent |
| 2 | 60 percent | 90 percent | 67 percent |
| 3 | 50 percent | 70 percent | 71 percent |
| 4 | 30 percent | 60 percent | 50 percent |

At the fourth mastery level, the student has only half of the mastery and retention the exam needs. At the first mastery level, the student has 75 percent of what the exam needs. The average of 75 percent, 67 percent, 71 percent, and 50 percent is 66 percent. The student has spent 30 hours so far. Learning efficiency is therefore 66 percent mastery over 30 hours.

## Why more hours stop helping

When this efficiency calculation is run on inefficient learners, extra hours often move the mastery number very little. If this student doubled the time from 30 hours to 60 hours and only reached 76 percent mastery, the second block of 30 hours would have bought a gain of 10 percentage points.

That flattening happens because some methods are limited. It is like trying to put up a house when a hammer is the only tool you have. More time swinging the hammer helps at first. Later there are tasks the hammer cannot do, no matter how long you swing it. Higher mastery levels need methods chosen for those levels. Some methods cannot reach them at all, unless the person already processes new material unusually deeply.

A few regularities follow:

- Someone using a strong method can get more mastery in fewer hours.
- More time does not reliably raise mastery and retention. With a large volume of material, forgetting often stays high enough that 100 percent retention never arrives, especially at the higher levels.
- The method strongly shapes how easily those higher levels can be reached.
- Efficiency also depends on how high the requirement is. A lower bar is easier to meet in less time.

## Four learner profiles

Method quality and depth of processing produce four rough patterns:

| Learner | What mastery looks like | Time | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weak methods and low deep processing | Lower levels only. Higher levels stall. Retention is generally low. | High, because so much time goes to relearning. | 50 percent over 100 hours. Extra hours barely move the total. |
| Weak methods and high deep processing | Most levels are reached. Retention stays low to moderate, depending on how deeply the person processes. | Moderate. | 70 percent over 50 hours. Extra hours raise the total slowly. |
| Strong methods and low deep processing | Most levels are reached, with a struggle at the top. Retention is generally moderate. | Moderate to high. | 70 percent over 50 hours. Extra hours raise the total in proportion. |
| Strong methods and high deep processing | High achievement across levels. Retention is high. | Low. | 90 percent over 30 hours. Extra hours raise the total in proportion. |

The last of those four patterns produces the highest mastery in the least time. The first pattern is the case where extra hours move the total least.

## Time problem or method problem

If hours rise and mastery barely moves, the method is limited. If mastery rises in proportion as hours rise, more time can still help. Treating "how much did I cover today" as the score ignores retention, the time later spent relearning, and whether the knowledge can be used in more than one way.

## Open questions

Which mastery level is the current task actually asking for, and which level are you measuring?

If you doubled the hours you are spending now, would total mastery rise in proportion, or flatten?

Is the current ceiling coming from the method, from how deeply the material is being processed, or from a requirement that is higher than you thought?
