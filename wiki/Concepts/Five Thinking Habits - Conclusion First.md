# Five Thinking Habits - Conclusion First

Five habits for saying what you think out loud so another person can follow it: give the conclusion first, say what you saw before what you concluded, play the question back, name the counterargument to your own decision (the antithesis), and cut to the short version. People judge your thinking by how it comes across, and a listener who loses the thread rates good thinking as poor. The same five habits also catch gaps in your own reasoning before you speak.

## Core takeaways

- Say the conclusion first, then the reasons. The conclusion tells the listener how to weigh everything after it.
- Before speaking, ask yourself what your conclusion is. If you cannot say it in a line, you do not know the subject as well as you thought.
- Give feedback as what you saw, then what you conclude from it. Verdicts with no observations plant doubt about how you reached them.
- When asked for something, say back what you think is really being asked. Silent readings end in "this is not what I asked for".
- Name the conditions that would change your decision at the time you state it. A decision-maker who names them is trusted more.
- Compress a complex opinion to one or two lines before you give it. Saying every thought you have reads as disorganized.

## The five habits

| Habit | What you say | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Start at the end | "This is what I think. Here is why." | Frames the reasons that follow |
| Observations first | "I noticed X and Y, so I think Z." | Keeps the talk on the facts |
| Clarify the question | "So what you are really asking is..." | Buys time, exposes gaps on both sides |
| State the antithesis | "We should do A. These conditions would change that." | Shows the decision is open to evidence |
| Keep it concise | The one- or two-line version | Forces you to know what matters |

## How it works

- Start at the end
  - Experts often walk a listener through the whole chain of reasoning before the opinion. A few minutes in, the listener has lost the conclusion.
  - A listener cannot hold several points at once and piece them together. The conclusion, given first, is the frame the points fit into.
  - Asking "what is my conclusion?" before speaking is a self-check. Believing you understand something until you try to explain it step by step has a name in the research literature, the illusion of explanatory depth, and the question catches it.
  - If the check finds gaps, still open with the conclusion, with the uncertainty said in it: "I think we should do it this way, but I am unsure about two points. Here is what I see so far."
- Separate observations from conclusions
  - Weak form: "The workshop was not good. Fix this slide, reword that question."
  - Better form: "During that activity people were yawning and looked confused, and nobody raised a hand when you asked questions. So I think these parts could change."
  - With the observations on the table, everyone can draw their own conclusion, and the discussion is about how to read the facts. Locked conclusions turn it into opinion against opinion.
  - Splitting the two also makes you check your own reading. Everyone has bias, and the shallowest thinkers are the most confident.
- Clarify the question
  - In your head: what is the crux, what problem is really being solved, what purpose sits behind the ask.
  - Out loud: play it back, or split it into parts and take them one at a time.
  - The asker often sharpens too. They confirm the parts and add a third they had not named.
- State the antithesis
  - The hypothesis is what you think will happen. The antithesis is the counterargument. The thesis is where you land between them.
  - Form: "We should launch two months from now. Here are the three conditions that would delay that."
  - Saying the counterarguments yourself tells the listener you will not defend the decision past reasonable evidence.
  - Experienced people under time pressure fall back on pattern recognition: we have always done it this way. The stated antithesis is a checklist for whether this time is different.
- Keep it concise
  - Deep thinkers have many thoughts and want to say all of them. Endless elaboration reads as messy and also stops the thinker from going deeper.
  - Cutting to what is necessary takes the most expertise, which is why teaching a thing to a ten-year-old is a test of understanding.
  - Before giving a complex opinion, find the one- or two-line version that carries every important point.

```
Chain first:
  angle -> factor -> context -> ... -> conclusion (lost)

Conclusion first:
  conclusion -> reason 1 -> reason 2 -> open points
```

## On the page

The same five habits are the shape of a page on this wiki: the answer in the first lines, the evidence before the claim, the question named before it is answered, the strongest counter next to the claim, and the short version before the long one.
