# Five Thinking Habits - Conclusion First

Five habits make what you say out loud easy for another person to follow: give the conclusion first, say what you saw before what you concluded, play the question back, name the counterargument to your own decision, which is called the antithesis, and cut to the short version. People judge your thinking by how it comes across, and a listener who loses the thread rates good thinking as poor. The same habits also catch gaps in your own reasoning before you speak.

## Core takeaways

- Say the conclusion first, then the reasons.
- A conclusion you cannot state in one line is not yet understood.
- Give feedback as what you saw, then what you conclude.
- Say back what you think is really being asked.
- Name what would change your decision when you state it.
- Cut a complex opinion to one or two lines first.

## The five habits

Each habit is a short thing to say at a particular point in a conversation. Each also works as a check on your own thinking before you say it. The table gives the words and what each one does for the listener.

| Habit | What you say | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Start at the end | "This is what I think. Here is why." | Frames the reasons |
| Observations first | "I noticed X and Y, so I think Z." | Keeps talk on facts |
| Clarify the question | "So what you are really asking is..." | Exposes gaps on both sides |
| State the antithesis | "We should do A. These would change that." | Shows openness to evidence |
| Keep it concise | The one- or two-line version | Forces you to know what matters |

## How it works

Experts often walk a listener through the whole chain of reasoning before giving their opinion, and a few minutes in, the listener has lost the thread. A listener cannot hold several points at once and piece them together, so a conclusion given first becomes the frame the points fit into. Asking yourself "what is my conclusion" before speaking is also a check on yourself. Believing you understand something until you try to explain it step by step is called the illusion of explanatory depth, and the question catches it.

```
Chain first:
  angle -> factor -> context -> ... -> conclusion (lost)

Conclusion first:
  conclusion -> reason 1 -> reason 2 -> open points
```

- If the check finds gaps, still open with the conclusion.
- Say the uncertainty inside it: "I think X, but two points are unclear."

Feedback works the same way. "The workshop was not good, fix this slide" gives a verdict with nothing under it, and it plants doubt about how the verdict was reached. "During that activity people yawned, looked confused and did not raise hands, so I think these parts could change" gives the facts first and the verdict after.

- With the observations shown, everyone can draw their own conclusion.
- The talk is then about how to read the facts.
- Bare verdicts turn it into opinion against opinion.
- Splitting the two also makes you check your own reading.
- The shallowest thinkers tend to be the most confident.

Playing the question back happens in two places. In your head, you ask what the crux is and what purpose sits behind the ask. Out loud, you say it back or split it into parts and take them one at a time, and the asker often sharpens their own question in reply.

- The asker may confirm the parts and add one they had not named.
- Silent readings end in "this is not what I asked for".

The antithesis is the counterargument to what you expect will happen, and the thesis is where you land between the two. Stating it yourself tells the listener that you will not defend the decision past reasonable evidence. Experienced people under time pressure fall back on pattern recognition, and a stated antithesis checks whether this time is different.

- Form: "We should launch in two months. These three conditions would delay it."
- A decision-maker who names such conditions is trusted more.

Deep thinkers have many thoughts and want to say all of them, which reads as messy and also stops them from going deeper. Cutting to what matters takes the most expertise, which is why teaching a thing to a ten-year-old tests understanding.

- Before a complex opinion, find the one- or two-line version.
- That version should still carry every important point.

## On the page

The same habits give the shape of a page on this wiki. The answer comes in the first lines, the evidence before the claim, and the question is named before it is answered. The strongest counterargument sits next to the claim, and the short version comes before the long one.
