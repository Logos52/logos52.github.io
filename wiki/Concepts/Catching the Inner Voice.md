# Catching the Inner Voice

Catching the inner voice is noticing a sentence you say to yourself at the moment it runs, and then choosing what to do with it. Most self-talk passes unnoticed. The part that replays a bad moment costs mood, action and health in proportion to how long it runs, so catching it early is where a few minutes a day of effort pay off.

## Takeaways

- Self-talk runs on the brain's speaking machinery, so it trains like a habit.
- Your sense of how often you talk to yourself is unreliable.
- The target is the loop: replayed distress that produces nothing new.
- The loop's cost to the body grows with its duration.
- Arguing a critical line into its opposite can backfire.
- A cue rehearsed in advance beats one invented in the moment.
- Remove triggers first, since about 43 percent of behaviour repeats by context.

## How it works

Children talk themselves through hard tasks out loud, in words learned from adults, and over the years that talk goes silent and gets shorter. Silent self-talk is compressed, so one word can stand for a whole argument, and a critical line arrives already condensed and only half in words. Saying it aloud or writing it out expands it back into something you can inspect. Self-directed speech also gets louder when a task is hard, and that is the moment worth watching.

- The brain sends an advance copy of what it is about to say.
- The line arrives marked as your own and raises no alarm.
- Awareness of drifting off task comes and goes.
- Random prompts catch episodes you never catch yourself.
- Questionnaires put self-talk in most of the day.
- Random prompts find it in about a quarter of moments.

Replaying distress leaves the quality of your solutions alone and lowers the chance that you act on them. Negative material is also processed more thoroughly and held longer than positive material of the same size. Blood pressure, heart rate and stress hormones rise with the minutes spent in the replay. The loop persists because it feels like problem-solving.

- A critical line outweighs a kind one.
- A forty-minute replay of a thirty-second slight costs forty minutes.
- The catch cue: reflection has stopped producing anything new.
- A cue works by moving attention, so name where to look.
- A pep talk moves attention less than a named target.

A swap that works is from "why is this happening to me" to "how do I do the next step". Using your own name instead of "I" lowers reactivity a little, and in daily life that helped only when preparing for something.

## How to do it

Start with the places where a line fires, since a trigger removed costs less than a loop caught. Then gather real samples instead of relying on memory, and write a plan on the exact sentence you catch. A plan on a category such as "negative thoughts" does not fire. The plan names which of the four to make, label, sort, defer or switch, and each can be used on its own.

- Audit cues: the meeting, hour, app or room where the line fires.
- Set random prompts a few times a day.
- Log the situation, the exact wording and what happened next.
- Plan: "If I catch myself thinking [line], then I [move]."
- Label: put "I'm having the thought that" in front of it.
- Labelling lowers the line's believability and sting without any argument.
- Sort: within your control or not, and dismiss what is not.
- Defer: park the loop to a fixed daily window.
- Most parked items are never collected.
- Switch: turn a "why" question into a "how" question.

A new line is installed by saying it out loud on real occasions, then whispering it, then running it silently. It has to be trained in the situations where the old line fires, since a trained line rarely carries over to other situations. One dose that has been tested is a daily diary entry in the third person about the day's main event, kept up for about a month. Another is ten minutes a day of focused-attention practice for two weeks, where the early gains are large for the most distractible and then flatten.

- Count the prompts that caught a loop.
- Count the loops that got deferred.
- Count whether the planned move fired.
- Behaviour counts measure change, and self-rated awareness measures confidence.

## Where it fails

Inner speech varies a lot between people. Some adults report almost none, and its measured effect is on verbal rehearsal, with no effect found on switching between tasks. People also explain the causes of their own behaviour with confidence and no real access to them, so a log should hold what was observable and treat any reason given for the line as a guess. The effects are small: reflecting on a stressor from the outside, by name or from a year ahead, beats reflecting from inside it by a pooled effect of 0.19, with a lower bound near zero.

- Trained cues show a moderate effect, near 0.47, steady over a decade.
- Replacing a critical line with its opposite backfires for low self-confidence.
- Change what the thought does and leave its truth undecided.
- Talk of "rewiring" comes from a 19th-century argument without measurement.
- Attention training has the weakest evidence for changing the brain.
- Whether stopping a thought makes it rebound is contested.
- One three-day study found no rebound and lower anxiety.

Venting meets the emotional need and leaves the thought unchanged, so it can deepen the loop.
