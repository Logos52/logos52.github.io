# Dating Apps - The Gini Coefficient

The Gini coefficient is a number between 0 and 1 that says how unevenly something is shared across a group, where 0 means everyone has the same amount and 1 means one person has all of it. Economists use it for income. Run on the likes sent inside a dating app, it shows attention from women landing on a small share of men, which is why most men on the apps get few matches and why the apps can charge those men for a small edge.

- Likes received by men are more unequal than most countries' incomes.
- Half the likes from women go to the top 15% of men.
- Women like under 5% of the men they see.
- Men like 62% of the women they see.
- Men outnumber women about 2 to 1 on the main apps.
- Women rate men harshly, and a swipe acts on that rating.
- The apps earn from the men who lose out.

## How it works

A dating app is a market with men on one side and women on the other, and the two sides differ in size. Tinder is 78% male, Hinge about 65% and Bumble 62.5%, and some European markets reach 9 men per woman. Each side meets a pool of millions of strangers and reacts in its own way, women by choosing few and men by swiping in volume. The likes then pile up on a small group of men, and filters and paid features push the pile higher.

- A woman swipes right about 2,300 times over her time on Tinder.
- A man swipes right about 15,600 times.
- The average woman on Tinder gets 81 matches a month.
- The average man gets 33.
- A man's chance of a match on one swipe is about 0.87%.
- On Hinge, the top 1% of men get 16% of all likes.
- The bottom half of men get almost none.
- On Grindr every user is a man, so the sides are equal.
- Grindr shows no such pile-up of likes.

```
2 men per woman on the app
        |
women select (<5% liked)   men swipe in volume (62%)
        |                          |
        +-----> likes pile on the top 15% of men
                        |
        most men match rarely  ->  apps sell them an edge
```

## Filters and prices

Filters sharpen the pile-up. In leaked Bumble data from 2023, about 60% of women set a minimum height of 6 ft, a height about 14.5% of American men reach, and men under 5 ft 9 get about 70% fewer matches than average. Tinder added a paid height filter for subscribers in 2025. Premium tiers sell a small advantage to men who are not matching, and the top tier, Tinder Select, costs up to $499 a month.

- Tinder's paying users fell 16% from late 2023 to late 2025.
- Revenue stayed flat because the users who stayed paid more.
- Paying men on Tinder get about 13 dates a year.
- Paying women get about 32.
- Per date, men pay about five times more.

## Why the swipe changed the numbers

In 2009 OkCupid published a study called "Your Looks and Your Inbox" on how its users rated and messaged each other. Women's ratings of men and women's messages to men pointed at different sets of men. A swipe app keeps only one of the two. A left swipe is the rating and a right swipe is the message, so only the rating is left.

- Women rating men from 1 to 5 put about 80% below average.
- Men rating women produced a bell curve.
- Women messaged a much wider range of men than they rated well.

## Where the numbers sit

A Gini of 0.542 for likes received by men on Hinge in 2017 means little on its own. Set beside income figures for whole countries, it lands between the United States and South Africa. Run on Tinder data, the coefficient gave 0.58 for men, which is more unequal than the income of 95% of countries. Women on Hinge, as receivers of likes, sit below the United States.

| Group | Gini |
|---|---|
| Denmark, income | 0.28 |
| Women on Hinge, likes received | 0.376 |
| United States, income | 0.41 |
| Men on Hinge, likes received | 0.542 |
| Men on Tinder, likes received | 0.58 |
| South Africa, income | 0.63 |

## What followed

Young men have started leaving the apps, and the value of the companies has fallen with them. Between 2023 and 2025, use of Tinder, Bumble and Hinge by young men fell 15% to 22% while use by women stayed flat. In March 2025 the founder of Bumble said the apps run on rejection and judgement, and the new head of Match Group said they felt like a numbers game. A class action filed in February 2024 in a federal court in California says the apps are built to be addictive and hold rewards back on purpose.

- Matches that became a date: 4.8% in 2020, 2.1% in 2025.
- In a 2024 survey, 47% of men had quit or paused dating.
- 63% of men said the apps left them drained, ignored or invisible.
- Satisfaction across all users: 44% in 2019, 22% in 2025.
- Pew Research, 2022: 63% of men under 30 single, 34% of women.
- Bumble's stock fell 96.9% from its 2021 peak to February 2026.
- Match Group's value fell from about $45 billion to about $10 billion.
- Run clubs grew 59% in 2024, with many joining to meet people.
- Attention in run clubs piles on the most attractive members too.

Match Group denies the claim in the class action, and the case is pending.

## How to use the idea

A low match rate for a man follows from the ratio of men to women on the app and from where the likes go, and it says little about the one man. Women's attention piled on a few men before the apps existed. The apps measured that attention, put a price on it, and pushed it further.

- A premium tier moves a profile up a queue.
- It does not change the ratio or where half the likes go.
- Meeting in person removes mass swiping, and women stay selective.
