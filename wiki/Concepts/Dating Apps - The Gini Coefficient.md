# Dating Apps - The Gini Coefficient

The Gini coefficient is a number between 0 and 1 that says how unevenly something is shared across a group: 0 means everyone has the same amount, 1 means one person has all of it. Economists use it for income. Run on the likes sent inside a dating app, it shows that attention from women lands on a small share of men, which explains why most men on the apps get few matches and why the apps can charge those men for a small edge.

## Core takeaways

- On Hinge in 2017, likes received by men had a Gini of 0.542 and likes received by women 0.376. The same method on Tinder data gave 0.58 for men, more unequal than the income of 95% of countries.
- Half of all likes women send go to the top 15% of men. The top 1% of men on Hinge get 16% of all likes. The bottom half of men get almost none.
- Women like under 5% of the male profiles they see. Men like 62% of the female profiles they see.
- Men outnumber women about 2 to 1 on the main straight apps, so women are in short supply before anyone swipes.
- A swipe is a rating and a message in one action. When the two were separate, women rated harshly and messaged a wider range of men. The swipe keeps only the harsh rating.
- The apps earn from the men who lose out. Premium tiers sell a small advantage to men who are not matching, up to $499 a month for Tinder Select.

## How it works

- Two sides, men and women, unequal in size
  - Tinder is 78% male, Hinge about 65%, Bumble 62.5%. Some European markets reach 9 men per woman.
  - Grindr, where both sides are gay men and about equal in size, does not show the same concentration of likes.
- Each side reacts to a pool of millions of strangers in its own way
  - Women respond with selectivity: a woman swipes right about 2,300 times over her whole time on Tinder.
  - Men respond with volume: a man swipes right about 15,600 times.
- The likes concentrate
  - The average woman on Tinder gets 81 matches a month, the average man 33.
  - The average man's chance of a match on any one swipe is about 0.87%, under one match per 100 women.
- Filters sharpen it
  - In leaked Bumble data from 2023, about 60% of women set a minimum height of 6 ft. About 14.5% of American men are that tall. Men under 5 ft 9 get about 70% fewer matches than average.
  - Tinder added a paid height filter for subscribers in 2025.
- The companies charge the men who lose out
  - Tinder's paying users fell 16% between late 2023 and late 2025, and revenue stayed flat because the remaining users paid more.
  - Paying men on Tinder get about 13 dates a year, paying women 32. Per date, men pay about five times more.

## Why the swipe changed the numbers

In a 2009 OkCupid study, "Your Looks and Your Inbox", women asked to rate men from 1 to 5 put about 80% of men below average, while men rating women produced a bell curve. The same study tracked whom the women then messaged, and the messages spread across a much wider range of men than the ratings. On a swipe app that gap between rating and messaging closes: a left swipe is the rating, a right swipe is the message, and only the rating survives.

## What followed

- Between 2023 and 2025, use of Tinder, Bumble and Hinge by young men fell 15% to 22%. Use by women stayed flat.
- The share of matches that became a date fell from 4.8% in 2020 to 2.1% in 2025.
- In a 2024 survey, 47% of men had given up on active dating or paused it, and 63% said the apps left them feeling drained, ignored or invisible. Satisfaction across all users fell from 44% in 2019 to 22% in 2025.
- A Pew survey from 2022 found 63% of men under 30 single against 34% of women under 30.
- Bumble's stock fell 96.9% from its 2021 peak to February 2026. Match Group's market value fell from about $45 billion in 2021 to about $10 billion in late 2025. In March 2025 the Bumble founder said the apps run on rejection and judgement, and the new Match CEO said they felt like a numbers game.
- A class action filed in February 2024 in a federal court in California says the apps are built to be addictive and hold rewards back on purpose. Match Group denies it. The case is pending.
- Run clubs grew 59% in 2024, and 72% of Gen Z members joined to meet people. Attention inside them concentrates on the most attractive members in the same way.

## How to use the idea

- A low match rate follows from the ratio and the concentration above. It says little about the one man.
- Do not buy a premium tier expecting the ratio to change. It moves a profile up a queue in a market where half the likes go to 15% of men.
- The pattern existed before the apps. The apps measured it, priced it, and pushed it further. Meeting people in person takes away the mass swiping and leaves the selectivity in place.
