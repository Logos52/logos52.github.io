# Cave Theory

Cave Theory is the idea that the human brain was shaped to solve survival problems, and that reading, note-taking, classes and rote drill are recent ways of learning it handles badly. It explains why a school fact learned on its own fades within days, and it settles what to do first with new material: attach it to a problem or to something already known before trying to memorise it.

## Core takeaways

- The brain keeps a fact by how many other things it connects to, and drops a fact that connects to nothing.
- School material arrives one fact at a time, answering to no problem yet, so the brain drops it unless the learner attaches it.
- Attach each new piece to a problem, a use, or something already known before any memorising.
- Ignore subject walls; a fact from one class may be attached to a fact from another.
- Guidance still matters: connect inside a taught structure, and do not explore without a plan.
- Rote drill remains for the small share of exam detail that has nothing to attach to, and it comes last.

## The mechanism

- Timescales
  - The brain's machinery is roughly 300,000 years old.
  - Writing is a few thousand years old, universal schooling a few hundred.
  - So the brain was shaped by life before writing, and books, classes and notes came after it was finished.
- What shaped it
  - Problems where failure meant death: food, shelter, keeping the group safe.
  - Learning was clue-gathering toward one purpose: the position of the sun, tracks on the ground, each clue joined to the others to solve the problem in front of the person.
  - No curriculum existed, so no fact was kept apart from its use.
- The energy rule
  - The brain burns a large share of the body's energy, and food was scarce, so holding a memory has a cost.
  - The brain sweeps out what connects to nothing else.
  - A piece connected to many others reads as important and stays.
- The frame problem
  - School delivers facts in the shape the sweep removes: isolated, and useful for nothing yet.
  - The same brain learns a hobby to great depth without notes, because a hobby comes with a purpose and every new piece attaches to old ones.
- Recall rebuilds a memory from its connections each time, so more connections also mean more chances to catch an error.

     yes                     no
      |                       |

## The hobby test

Pick something learned by choice: a sport, an instrument, a game, a book series. Imagine a few hours to teach it to a beginner. Nobody plans to write it all out, hand it over to be read and copied, and then run quizzes on it. Most people use that plan for coursework, though the same brain learned the hobby without a single note.

## How to apply it

- Before opening a source, write down the problem the material should answer.
- Attach each new fact to something already known or to another new fact; one attachment is enough to start, and the brain builds outward from it.
- Follow relevance across subjects and ignore which class a fact came from.
- Use the material on problems soon after reading it; use raises retention further.
- For dry material, build interest by putting a problem first rather than waiting to feel interested.
- Keep to a taught structure. Learning with little guidance performs worse in studies of classrooms, so the connecting happens inside a plan.
- Leave rote drill for the residual detail, near the end.

## Where it fails

- Waiting for curiosity. School knowledge does not come free through curiosity; the attachment has to be made on purpose.
- Open exploration. Minimally guided discovery underperforms, so the attaching happens inside a structure someone taught.
- Treating it as a claim about prehistory. The signal to keep a piece rests on how it was encoded, so attaching material works whether or not the story about ancestors is right.

## Sources

- Geary 2008, *Educating the Evolved Mind*; Geary & Xu 2022: school knowledge is evolutionarily novel and does not come for free through organic curiosity.
- Roussel et al. 2024, *Educational Psychology Review*: the timescales, including the 300,000-year figure (Eskelson 2020, cited therein). Writing is a few thousand years old, universal schooling a few hundred.
- Craik & Lockhart 1972; Anderson & Reder 1979: depth and connectedness of processing drive retention. The signal to keep a piece rests on how it was encoded, whatever its history.
- Dunlosky et al. 2013: elaborative interrogation and self-explanation rated moderate utility. Outside support for connection-building study.
- Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006: the limit Cave Theory respects. Minimally guided discovery underperforms. Cave Theory licenses attachment, never unguided exploration.
