# Cave Theory

Cave Theory is the name for a mismatch between how the brain keeps information and how school delivers it. The brain forgets most of what it takes in, and it does this by design. Storing information costs energy. A piece of information that connects to nothing is discarded. School material arrives isolated, not yet connected to any problem or purpose. That is the kind of material the brain discards.

The mismatch is one of timescales. The brain is old. Books, notes, and classes are new.

## Core takeaways

- The brain discards most of what it takes in. Storing information costs energy, and a piece that connects to nothing is not kept.
- The brain's machinery is roughly 300,000 years old. Writing is a few thousand years old and universal schooling a few hundred. The brain was not built for books, notes, and classes.
- Information is held as a network. A piece that connects to a problem or a pattern is kept. A piece that connects to nothing is dropped. One connection is enough.
- A person learns a hobby without a note and learns coursework by reading, copying, and being quizzed. The same brain does both. The difference is whether the material is attached to a purpose the learner already holds.
- The study techniques built on this are not matters of taste. Each one gives material a problem or a question to connect to.
- None of this depends on prehistory. It rests on how material is encoded.

## Why the brain discards

The brain is an expensive organ, and calories were scarce. Storing information costs energy, so the brain keeps only what is connected to something and discards the rest. The brain discards aggressively.

School material arrives isolated. It does not yet connect to any problem the learner holds. That is the kind of material the brain discards.

## How old the brain is and how new school is

The brain's machinery is roughly 300,000 years old. Writing is a few thousand years old. Universal schooling is a few hundred years old. The brain was never built for books, notes, and classes.

The old brain learned about problems where failure was fatal: food and shelter. Every clue, from the position of the sun to tracks, was pulled into a purpose the person already held. Nothing was kept apart from its use.

## A hobby and a course

That old mode still shows. Take the thing you know best that nobody assigned you, and imagine you have an afternoon to teach it to a beginner. Nobody's plan is to write it all out, hand it over to be read and copied, and then quiz the beginner. Yet that is the coursework plan. The brain that follows the coursework plan learned the hobby without a note.

The same brain ran both. Only one thing differed: the hobby was learned toward a purpose the learner already held, and the coursework was not. Cave Theory calls that difference the frame.

## What the brain keeps and what it drops

Information is held as a network. A piece that connects to something, such as a problem or a pattern, is treated as important and stays. A piece that connects to nothing goes. One attachment is enough; the brain builds outward from it on its own.

None of this needs the prehistory. It depends on how material is encoded.

## The techniques that follow

The study techniques built on Cave Theory are not matters of taste. Each one gives material something to connect to.

- [[Schema|Survive and Thrive]] builds the network, meaning connections to what you already know and connections among the new pieces, and it details how the connections get built.
- [[Memory Handling]] covers what to do while a piece is still in working memory, which holds it for only a short time.
- [[Order Control]] follows relevance instead of the order in which a source presents material. When a learner thinks "that was another class", a connection does not get made. Order Control makes the connection anyway.
- [[Inquiry-Based Learning]] is questions written before a source is opened, then answered. Inquiry-Based Learning is never open wandering.

Cave Theory governs how material is attached. Cave Theory does not decide whether material is taught. For dry material, the learner builds interest by putting a problem first. Cave Theory does not wait for interest to arrive on its own.

## How to practice this

1. Pick the thing you know best that nobody assigned you. Plan an afternoon teaching it to a beginner. Notice that your plan is not to write it out, have them read and copy it, then quiz them.
2. Before opening a source, write the questions you want it to answer. Read to answer those questions. Notice which pieces you still have afterward: the ones that answered a question.
3. Take one new piece of material and connect it to one thing you already know. Leave another piece unconnected. Notice which of the two you still hold later.
4. When a new piece connects to something from another class or another source, follow the connection. Notice the moment the thought "that was another class" stops you.
5. For dry material, find a problem it solves before you start reading. Notice whether interest appears once the problem is in place.
