# Cave Theory

Cave Theory is the idea that the human brain was shaped to solve survival problems, and that reading, note-taking, classes and rote drill are recent ways of learning that it handles badly. It explains why a school fact learned on its own fades within days. It also settles what to do first with new material: attach it to a problem or to something already known before trying to memorise it.

## Takeaways

- The brain keeps a fact by how much it connects to.
- A fact that connects to nothing gets dropped.
- School delivers facts one at a time, tied to no problem.
- Attach each new piece to a problem, a use or known material.
- Subject walls do not matter for attaching.
- Connect inside a taught structure instead of exploring freely.
- Rote drill is for leftover detail, and it comes last.

## The mechanism

The brain's machinery is roughly 300,000 years old, while writing is a few thousand years old and universal schooling a few hundred. It was shaped by problems where failure meant death: food, shelter and keeping the group safe. Learning then was gathering clues toward one purpose, such as the position of the sun or tracks on the ground, with each clue joined to the others to solve the problem in front of the person. No curriculum existed, so no fact was ever held apart from its use.

- The brain burns a large share of the body's energy.
- Food was scarce, so holding a memory had a cost.
- The brain sweeps out memories that connect to nothing.
- A piece connected to many others reads as important and stays.
- School facts arrive isolated and useful for nothing yet.
- Isolated facts are what the brain sweeps out.
- Recall rebuilds a memory from its connections each time.
- More connections mean more chances to catch an error.

```
new piece arrives
        |
tied to a problem or to known things?
     yes                     no
      |                       |
reads as important       reads as unimportant
kept, builds outward     swept out in days
```

## The hobby test

The same brain that drops coursework learns a hobby to great depth without notes, because a hobby comes with a purpose and every new piece attaches to old ones. To see the gap, pick something learned by choice, such as a sport, an instrument, a game or a book series, and imagine having a few hours to teach it to a beginner. Nobody plans to write it all out, hand it over to be read and copied, and then run quizzes. Most people use exactly that plan for coursework.

- A hobby has a purpose from the start.
- Each new hobby fact attaches to earlier ones.
- Coursework is usually studied by copying and quizzing instead.

## How to apply it

The practical order is to give the material a problem before reading it, attach as you go, and use it soon after. One attachment per fact is enough to start, and the brain builds outward from it. Dry material gets the same treatment, with the problem put first instead of waiting to feel interested.

- Write the problem down before opening a source.
- Attach each fact to something known or to another new fact.
- Follow relevance across subjects, whatever class a fact came from.
- Use the material on problems soon after reading.
- Keep to a taught structure, since low-guidance learning does worse in classrooms.
- Leave rote drill for the leftover detail, near the end.

## Where it fails

Curiosity alone does not deliver school knowledge, so waiting to feel curious leaves material unattached. Open exploration without guidance also does worse, so the attaching has to happen inside a structure someone taught. The theory is also easy to misread as a claim about prehistory. Whether a piece is kept depends on how it was taken in, so attaching material works whether or not the story about ancestors is right.

- Waiting for curiosity.
- Exploring without a plan.
- Treating the idea as a claim about prehistory.
