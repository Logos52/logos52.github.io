# Bias and Framing

Real-world bias is delivered by framing. The facts of a case can pass through a sentence unchanged while the valence attached to them, the positive or negative charge, decides which conclusions feel reasonable. One word with a changed moral charge can be enough to move the conclusion. Bias is the systematic, directional error that results: a consistent deviation from accuracy, which needs calibration rather than the correction of a single mistake. Framing is how that bias is delivered, and the delivery is the part a reader can learn to notice.

## Core takeaways

- Bias is a systematic error with a direction. The correction is calibration. Fixing one mistake does not remove it.
- Framing delivers bias. It presents the same facts with a chosen valence, and the valence decides which conclusions feel reasonable before any of them are evaluated.
- A frame move is a substitution. A near-synonym with a different moral charge replaces a flat term while the facts stay the same. The test is whether putting the flat term back changes any fact.
- At a New York City housing hearing, the word "violence" assigned moral blame to the property owner before any argument was made. Every fact of the eviction stayed the same.
- A frame test, such as the exposure test, is only a test when it is run on your own beliefs as well as your opponents' beliefs.
- Naming frames can replace looking at evidence. If two weeks of it changes no judgment, go back to what is claimed and what evidence is shown.

## What bias is

Bias is a systematic or preventable error in thinking. It is a consistent deviation from accuracy or fairness, and it has a direction. The direction is what separates it from a random mistake, and it is why calibration is the only real correction. A rifle with misaligned sights is corrected by adjusting the sights. Re-aiming each shot does not fix it. Bias is corrected the same way, by calibrating the process that produces it.

Bias is usually invisible to the person holding it. The environment that produced it rewards the distorted view, and social feedback loops keep reinforcing it. It operates in the steps before a stated conclusion: how the question was asked, which evidence was selected, and which answers get rewarded.

## What framing is

Framing is presenting information in a way that emphasizes some aspects and downplays or omits others. The presentation guides the recipient's emotional response and interpretation.

There are four mechanisms:

- **Emotional Direction**: attaching positive or negative valence to the same facts.
- **Worldview Dependence**: effective framing aligns with (or exploits) the recipient's existing beliefs.
- **Strategic Omission**: leaving inconvenient information out while every stated fact stays true.
- **Directional Testing**: a frame test, such as exposure, incentive, or who benefits, becomes a frame move of its own when it is only ever run against opponents. Run in both directions it is a check. The test of the person running it is whether they can name a belief of their own that fails it.

Selection alone does not make a frame move, because every sentence emphasizes something. Framing differs from lying, which asserts something false that can be checked against the record. It also differs from emphasis, which foregrounds one true thing without changing the moral charge of the others. What marks a frame move is substitution: a near-synonym with a different moral charge is swapped in while the underlying facts stay identical. Nothing in the sentence can be disputed, and the conclusion has still moved. *Eviction* and *the violence of evictions* name the same procedure. *Abolished* and *got rid of* name the same act. The test is whether replacing the loaded term with its flat equivalent changes any fact. If it changes only how the fact feels, the substitution is the frame move.

## How framing produces bias

Framing is the main delivery mechanism for bias in real-world communication. The way a problem is framed decides which solutions feel reasonable before any of them are evaluated. Repeated framing from trusted sources, such as media, peers, and institutions, gradually shifts what a person treats as normal or obvious. Once a frame is accepted, contradictory evidence is reinterpreted to fit it rather than to challenge it. That is confirmation bias operating at the level of interpretation. Changing someone's mind therefore goes through the frame more often than through the facts. New evidence that enters an intact frame is absorbed by it.

## A worked case: the eviction hearing

At a New York City housing hearing, an activist said the city administration "is emboldening us so that we no longer tolerate the violence of evictions as a matter of business as usual." An eviction removes a tenant from a property. Every fact of that procedure, who was removed, from what, and on what grounds, stays the same in the activist's sentence. What changes is the valence. The party doing the removing becomes an aggressor. The party removed becomes someone being defended against harm. That is Emotional Direction carried by one word.

The word also acts before any argument begins. It assigns a moral judgment first: the owner has already committed violence. A later taking of the property then reads as a remedy rather than a seizure. The dispute moves from what is owned to who counts as an aggressor, and the frame has already answered that question. One panelist, responding, describes that sequence as a general charge against his opponents: "it has always started from this point of view that you are bad," and "the first framing is that the private property owner is evil." The charge is polemical and aimed at a whole political movement. What can be observed in the clip itself is narrower: the moral assignment comes first, and the argument is built on top of it.

Later in the same segment a second panelist runs an exposure test on the other side. The people holding these positions "can afford to have luxury beliefs about public spaces because they never use them": not the bus, the subway, or the parks. The costs fall hardest on the working class, who need those amenities. The test is sound and can be reused: ask whether the person holding a belief is exposed to its consequences. The panel never runs it on itself. The panel describes itself as venture capitalists, and it is arguing the property-owner side of a property dispute. A frame test run in one direction only is a frame move. Run in both directions, it checks both sides.

## Where this applies

- **Red teaming**: rewrite the claim's loaded terms as their flat equivalents, then read it again. If the argument still holds, the loaded terms added nothing to it. If the argument no longer holds, the loaded terms were carrying it.
- **Decision making**: before choosing between options, say who set the option list and what a third option would have to look like. A narrow option list limits the decision before any information is gathered, because it decides what counts as a choice.
- **Communication**: when you choose the stronger word, say what it adds beyond the flat one. If the only answer is that it has more force, you are making a frame move.
- **Reciprocity**: before running a frame test on someone else's position, run it on your own and say what it turned up. A test that has never cost you a belief is not being used as a test.

In the hearing clip, one word carried the valence, every fact of the procedure stayed the same, and the moral assignment arrived before the argument meant to establish it. That order is the thing to watch for, in a hearing, in a headline, or in a plan you wrote yourself. By the time an argument is being weighed, the frame that decided which answers count has usually already been accepted.

## Problems with the method

Every description selects, so frame-naming can be run on any sentence ever written. That is the method's failure mode. A reader who learns to name frames can dismiss a claim without looking at its evidence, and the naming feels like analysis when no analysis has been done. The cost of adopting the method is a standing temptation to answer arguments by describing how they were worded.

The reciprocity rule applies to the worked case above. The case reads a housing activist's word choice closely and treats the responding panel's vocabulary as the neutral baseline. The same test run on the panel finds "city dictator," "comrade," and "socialism corner" carrying the same kind of valence in the other direction, and the case does not examine them. The panel knows the method. An earlier segment of the same episode is about whether "IP theft" is the right name for a practice those speakers call an industry standard. The panel had the method and did not run it on its own side.

The evidence for the main claim is thin. The strong form of the claim, that *most* real-world bias arrives by framing, is an inference from close reading of a handful of cases, one of them a single partisan podcast segment. It would need measurement to hold at that strength.

Quit signal: after two weeks of reading this way, check whether any judgment changed. If you believe nothing you did not believe before, and checked nothing you would not have checked anyway, the method has produced only labels. Go back to what is claimed and what evidence is shown, per [[wiki/Red Team/Applied Critical Thinking - Testing Frames|Applied Critical Thinking]].

## How to practice this

1. Replace a claim's loaded term with its flat equivalent. Notice whether any fact changed. If only the feeling changed, that word was the frame move.
2. Read the whole claim again with the flat terms in place. Notice whether the argument still holds. If it no longer holds, the loaded terms were carrying the argument.
3. Before choosing between options, name who set the option list. Say what a third option would have to look like. Notice whether the list decided the choice before you gathered any information.
4. When you choose a stronger word in your own writing, say what it adds beyond the flat one. If the only answer is more force, you are making a frame move.
5. Run the exposure test on one of your own positions: ask whether you are exposed to its consequences. Say what the test turned up. Notice whether the test has ever cost you a belief.
6. After two weeks, check whether any judgment changed. If you believe nothing new and checked nothing new, the method has produced only labels. Return to what is claimed and what evidence is shown.
