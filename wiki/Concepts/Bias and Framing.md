# Bias and Framing

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Bias is a lopsided weight for or against an idea, and it differs from plain error because it is systematic and can be prevented. Framing is the way information is presented, and framing bias is the pull that presentation exerts on the conclusions a person reaches. The two connect because a frame supplies the starting tilt and a person rarely gets the feedback that would correct it. During study, a frame shows up as notes that copy a textbook's headings instead of the relationships that matter. During public argument, it shows up as a loaded word that decides who is the villain before any facts are weighed. The remedy in both cases is to rebuild the picture independently and ask who actually bears the cost.

## Core takeaways

- Bias is a systematic tilt in reasoning, like a rifle that puts its group of shots off center and needs recalibrating.
- In thinking the target is invisible, so aiming gives no clear feedback, and the little feedback a person does get often comes from others who share the same tilt.
- Framing bias means that conclusions and decisions follow from how the options are presented.
- During study, framing shapes the groups, or chunks, that a student forms, often copying a textbook's headings instead of the relationships that matter.
- During public argument, a loaded word can decide who is the villain before any facts are weighed.

## What bias is

Bias is easiest to picture on a shooting range. A rifle that is out of calibration puts its group of shots off center, and bias is the distance between the target and the middle of that group. Fixing it means recalibrating, which in thinking means realigning how one reasons.

Thinking makes that harder than shooting, because in thinking the target is invisible. Without a target, aiming gives no clear feedback, and the little feedback a person does get often comes from others who share the same tilt. Copying the people around us keeps the bias in place, since everyone confirms everyone else.

## What framing is

Framing bias means that conclusions and decisions follow from how the options are presented. The same options, wrapped in a different story or layout, lead people to different conclusions.

## How framing produces bias

Framing bias shapes the groups, or chunks, that a student forms. Students usually group information the way a teacher, a lecture or a textbook already grouped it, because that order seems logical and natural. Those given groupings are often not the best for memory.

A mind map of retinal detachment shows the cost. The obvious version runs every fact through the disease name, with no links across the branches, and it looks almost identical to the map for many other diseases. Facts arranged like that are easy to forget and have to be memorized one by one. Redrawing the map with links between ideas, built by asking why each part matters, gives a structure that sticks.

Students can also create their own framing bias. Pre-chunking is grouping material in depth before asking which parts are important. Chunks made that early are rarely organized around importance, which is the reason to ask the importance questions first.

## A worked case: the eviction hearing

Framing bias also works on public questions. At a New York City hearing on rentals, an activist called evictions "the violence of evictions". That one word sets the frame. Once eviction counts as violence, the property owner becomes the aggressor, and taking the owner's property can be presented as a remedy.

A counter-frame starts by naming that opening move. One panelist argued that taking property always starts with a moral story in which one side is good and the owner is bad, whether the owner is a landlord or a wealthy person judged to have too much. He called private property rights the foundation of liberty in the United States and argued that each small step away from them adds up.

A second panelist added a test of who is exposed to the result. When a landlord cannot evict a tenant who stops paying, the income for repairs disappears and the building runs down. Neighbors of an unruly tenant face noise, damage and disorder, and many of them are older residents on rent control who cannot afford to move. In his telling, people who back the policy from gated communities do not share those costs, so the frame feels kind to them while the tenants around the problem carry the result. The panel did not run the same exposure test on its own side of the dispute.

## Where this applies

Framing bias shows up in study, where it shapes the groups a student forms, and in public argument, where a loaded word can decide who is the villain before any facts are weighed.

## Problems with the method

Because the target in thinking cannot be seen, a person has no direct way to check whether a correction worked, and checking against people who share the same tilt only confirms it.

## How to practice this

- Before accepting a grouping of material, ask whether the groups follow importance and the links between ideas, or only the headings a textbook already used.
- When a public claim arrives with a loaded word, name the frame, then ask who is exposed to the result.
