# Bias and Framing

A bias is a lean in a person's judgment that runs the same way every time. Framing is the choice of words and emphasis that carries that lean into a sentence. Knowing how the two connect tells a reader where to look, because every fact in a sentence can be true while one word has already set which conclusion feels right.

## Takeaways

- A bias has a direction and a random mistake has none.
- Correcting a bias means finding its direction and adjusting for it.
- Framing changes how facts feel, so fact checking misses it.
- The sign of framing is a swapped word with a moral charge.
- Test by swapping the loaded word for a plain one.
- A framing test counts only when run on your own side too.
- Naming a frame can replace checking whether the claim is true.

## How it works

A bias sits under the stated conclusion, in how the question was asked, which evidence was picked and which answers got rewarded. The person holding it usually cannot see it, because the place where it was learned rewards that view and their peers confirm it. Framing is how a bias gets into words. A frame shows some parts of a case and plays down or leaves out the rest, so every stated fact can be true while the moral charge moves.

- The same facts read as fair or cruel by the word attached.
- A frame lands best when it fits what the listener already believes.
- Leaving a fact out works as well as a loaded word.
- An omission is harder to catch, since nothing said is false.
- In a 1981 experiment, "lives saved" and "lives lost" got opposite choices.
- Framing is the usual way a bias passes between people.
- The frame decides which answers feel reasonable before any are weighed.
- Once a frame is accepted, contrary evidence gets read as fitting it.
- Changing a mind then has to go through the frame.

| Move | Facts | What changes | How to catch it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lying | one is false | the facts | check them |
| Emphasis | all true | which fact comes first | read the rest |
| Framing | all true | the moral charge | swap the word |

## An example

In 2026, at a housing hearing in New York City, an activist spoke of "the violence of evictions". An eviction is a landlord removing a tenant from a property. Who was removed, from where and on what grounds stay the same under either wording, and only the moral charge moves. A panel of investors who discussed the hearing on a podcast said the same, that the moral charge comes first and the argument gets built on top, and then used loaded words of their own.

```
plain:   the landlord evicted the tenant
framed:  the landlord did the violence of eviction

same:    who, from where, on what grounds
moved:   the landlord is now an aggressor
result:  taking the property reads as a remedy
```

- The word sets a moral verdict before any argument starts.
- People then argue about aggressors instead of ownership.
- The panel called the city's mayor a dictator and a comrade.
- Those words work the same way in the other direction, untested.
- One panelist asked whether people who hold a belief bear its costs.
- He said those activists do not ride the bus or use the parks.
- The panel, investors on the owners' side, never ran that test on itself.

## How to apply it

The word-swap test works anywhere a claim or a choice is put into words. It is most useful when reviewing an argument, choosing between options someone else listed, or picking a word in your own writing. It only counts as a test when it is also run on a belief you hold.

- Reviewing an argument: rewrite loaded terms plainly and reread the claim.
- If the claim still holds, the loaded word added nothing.
- If it falls, the loaded word was carrying the claim.
- Decisions: ask who wrote the option list.
- Name a third option the list left out.
- Writing: say what the stronger word adds beyond the plain one.
- If it only hits harder, it is framing.
- Before testing someone else's framing, test one belief of your own.

## Where it fails

Every sentence selects some facts over others, so a frame can be named in any sentence. A reader who names frames can dismiss a claim without reading its evidence, and the naming feels like analysis. The idea that most bias travels by framing rests on a few cases and has not been measured.

- Naming a frame can replace checking the claim.
- The spread of bias by framing is unmeasured.
- Two weeks with nothing newly believed or checked is a quit signal.
- At that point the method has become a vocabulary.
