# The Price of a Training Corpus

In July 2026 a court approved a $1.5 billion payout for pirated books, at about $3,000 a title. An earlier court had already ruled that training on bought books is fair use. The $3,000 figure is the price of one legal claim, the claim for making unauthorized copies. It is not a rate for learning from a book.

What actually constrains a lab is the set of public positions it holds at the same time. Each of those positions can later be quoted in a complaint against the lab.

## Core takeaways

- $1.5 billion divided by about 500,000 eligible titles is $3,000 a book. That number prices the piracy claim only. It is not a licensed training rate.
- On 23 June 2025 a district court ruled that training on legally acquired books is fair use, and that downloading and keeping pirated copies is not. The settlement paid for the second claim, after the first had already been decided.
- A lab may argue in court that learning from the world's writing is lawful. The same lab may argue in Washington that learning from its own writing is theft. That lab has given the next plaintiff a quote. A lab's exposure is the set of claims it has made in public about everyone else.
- The test that survives the hypocrisy charge is displacement. A model that can talk about a book has taken no sale from the book. A product trained on a professional database in order to sell a rival database has taken the sale.
- The live dispute over distillation is about terms of service, not copyright. No case on that side has a dollar figure yet.
- The durable asset for rightsholders is the pipeline of new work. One settlement is not a market, and the next settlement's per-work number is the check on whether $3,000 is a rate.

## What the settlement paid for

The 25 July 2026 conversation treats the payout as the first major public dollar figure in this fight. The panel is not neutral. One seat is an administration official on AI policy. The rest of the room is venture capital that holds the asset class. The panel says twice that everyone speaking is talking in their own financial interest. The names are in the Sources section.

The claim that survived was the making of unauthorized copies. The libraries named on the docket are LibGen and PiLiMi. About seven million books were taken from those sites. The class that could recover is the part of those works that meet registration and ISBN rules, about 500,000 titles. $1.5 billion divided by 500,000 is $3,000 a book. All three figures match the settlement papers and the 20 July 2026 final-approval item. The fact that decided the case is that the defendant did not pay for one legitimate copy.

That $3,000 is the first observed price for the piracy claim. It is not a licensed rate for a training copy. A lab that treats it as a rate for learning from a book has overclaimed. Exposure is sized by multiplying the observed number by the works the lab cannot show a receipt for. Works it can show a receipt for fall under a different ruling.

## The June 2025 ruling that separated the two claims

On 23 June 2025 a district court in the Northern District of California had already separated the two claims. Training on legally acquired books is fair use. The opinion called it quintessentially transformative. Downloading and keeping pirated library copies is not fair use. The class was certified for piracy only. The settlement put a price on the piracy claim. Training on purchased books had already been won, and that is why the rest could be settled. The judge and the case caption are listed in the Sources section, with the reporting on the case.

On air, one speaker said training was still undecided. That speaker is either behind the ruling, or declining to treat a district-court summary judgment as settled law. The ruling stands regardless of what the speaker said.

This is the first major AI-training copyright lawsuit to settle. More are in the pipeline. A panel member estimated how many major cases are behind it, without giving a source. That estimate is not a usable count.

## Settling instead of getting a ruling

One hypothesis: court holdings appear only in cases that no party can afford to settle. A defendant who must go to court is governed by case law. A defendant who can pay enough to end the case is governed by the terms of the last settlement. This rests on one data point. The section on the case against tests it.

The usual story is that a well-funded defendant pays. Paying ends the opinion that would have bound the next defendant. So the opinions that exist are the ones nobody could afford to stop. This case differs in one respect. The training ruling came before the settlement. Purchased-and-digitized copies had already been held fair use. The settlement covered the copies nobody could show a receipt for.

What governs a company afterwards depends on which kind of company it is. A small builder that cannot settle with a class is governed by the opinions. A frontier lab is governed by the last settlement, plus whatever the other side's terms of service already forbid.

Two claims on the show about whether a court had already spoken contradict each other. One seat says courts had already held that training on copyrighted books is fair use. Two minutes later another seat calls the question unresolved. A third case is named as a final judgment on AI training copyright. The previous ruling is the June 2025 split. The named database case is *Thomson Reuters v. Ross*. The name is Thomson, not Thompson. It is a February 2025 district-court summary judgment against fair use, for a competing legal-research tool trained on a professional database's headnotes. The ruling turned on market harm. It is not about generative book-training. It is not an appellate decision. Calling it a final judgment on AI training copyright overstates what it decided.

The panel's own summary is that IP law does not yet have the nuance for this question. So society will decide what is fair. The decision will come through settlements, lobbying, and legislation. That is the panel's account.

## The public positions that constrain a lab

A sentence said in a policy setting can be quoted in a later lawsuit. A lab may argue in court that learning from the world's writing is lawful. The same lab may argue in Washington that learning from its own writing is theft. That lab has given the first set of plaintiffs a quote. The sentence said in Washington is an admission. The section on the case against records that the key fact here is asserted and denied by the same speaker. The mechanism holds regardless.

The cause of action that cannot be quoted back against a lab is the one that never claims a property right in outputs. A complaint about bulk fake accounts, proxies, and lying at signup, described as a deceptive business practice, can ask for government help without claiming that the outputs are owned. This reframing can be reused. It amounts to the advice: never say IP theft.

The test that survives the hypocrisy charge is displacement. A model that can talk about a book has taken no sale from the book. A product trained on a professional database in order to sell a rival professional database has taken the sale. Those are the two poles of factor four, the market-harm factor, stated as facts about the test.

## Weights and model outputs

On the panel's account, the parameter file is property, and the text a model emits is governed by contract. Taking the file would be theft. Learning from the emitted text runs into terms of service. That is the panel's distinction, not a court holding.

Weights are described on air as the numerical parameters inside the software. Taking that file would be theft. One seat says nobody has alleged it. Earlier in the same show, the same seat reports that labs have told the government that foreign companies can steal the file. Both statements are recorded. The full treatment of that speaker is in the section on the case against.

Learning from outputs is given an industrial precedent on the show: carmakers study each other's cars, and early search engines queried rivals to benchmark. These are analogies, not legal findings.

The live distillation question is enforcement of terms of service, not copyright. A case between a newspaper and a lab is pleaded on the same basis. No case on that side has a dollar figure yet. On the show, a February lab post is said to have coined "industrial scale distillation attack," and "IP theft" is said to be absent from the same post. That claim was not checked against the post. The useful action is to open the post and check. The detection drill for what that language is doing is on [[wiki/Red Team/Applied Critical Thinking - Testing Frames|Applied Critical Thinking - Testing Frames]].

The panel's easier hypothetical is diffusion. Reviews, catalog data, and other people's commentary are already public. A pre-AI study guide is offered as proof that what a good book generates cannot be contained. The floor everyone grants is that text cannot be lifted, reprinted, and claimed as one's own. The hypothetical answers an easier question than the one about millions of pirated books.

## Crawlers, withholding, and the cycle of settlements

On the panel's account, collective withholding is the only leverage a scattered group of rightsholders still has, and a single crawler removes it. The same bot fetches pages for search and pages for training, so refusing the second fetch also refuses the first. Splitting the two bots was a live industry demand in 2025–26.

The cycle rightsholders run, as named on air, is: litigate until settlement, treat the settlement as an admission that the use was licensed, and carry that admission to the next counterparty. Music is the worked example. What breaks the cycle, on the panel's view, is a fragmented base that settles cheap and early. Book authors, newspapers, and magazines are called "very meek." That is a judgment, and it is the panel's.

The demand replayed on the show is that rightsholders organize and name their terms. The answer given on the show is "You're going to get steamrolled." The industry demand is to split the two bots. A standoff between a newspaper and a platform ended in a negotiated paywall exclusion, because the publishers wanted the platform's users. That is the panel's compressed account.

## The pipeline of new work

The durable commercial claim is the forward pipeline. A stream of new work turns a one-time payment into a subscription. A model that stops receiving the next book and the next wire story becomes outdated. The pipeline is the asset. The back settlement is a sunk cost.

On the show, each party's training data traces to another party in the same dispute. One lab trained on publishers and settled for $1.5 billion. Another lab is being sued by a newspaper. Other labs trained on the output of American models. One speaker refused to use the word stealing, on the ground that ownership of the copyright is itself unclear.

## The case against

One settlement does not establish a market price. The $3,000 figure prices that one claim. One defendant could not defend the piracy fact. The class's lawyers were paid a fee. The figure was negotiated between those two parties. The next settlement can land at a fraction of it.

The hypothesis that holdings appear only in cases nobody can settle is built from two data points. A well-funded defendant that takes a training question all the way to an opinion would falsify it. The June 2025 ruling is already that data point: a training question run to an opinion, before the settlement. The ruling is a stronger counterexample than the panel recognized.

The key fact in the hypocrisy argument is asserted and denied by the same speaker. He never gives a source for the version told to the government. He is an administration official. He names his stake: tainted derivatives of foreign models, and the startup ecosystem. He opens by saying he is no fan of the company, and he names "regulatory capture." The co-host supplies the market-substitution argument without drawing the hypocrisy conclusion. Summarizing this as "the room is biased" would drop the mechanism. A liability of ten figures acts like the fixed cost in step 3 of [[wiki/Concepts/Regulatory Capture via Doom-Marketing|Regulatory Capture via Doom-Marketing]]: only the largest incumbents can absorb it. That page covers who holds the gate. How the corpus gets priced is a separate question. The loop test on [[wiki/Red Team/Epistemic Exceptionalism|Epistemic Exceptionalism]], which asks what a framework can output other than "trust me / my group," is the check to run on the hypocrisy case, subject to that page's gate.

A proposed pool of ten percent of builder revenue comes without a derivation. It has no defined base and no rule for who gets paid. It is countered on air with a prediction that the other side will ask for the whole stack. The useful part is the mechanism, a forward license. The number has no support. The diffusion hypothetical cannot be tested on any time horizon a reader could use. The ten-percent pool is the cheapest available outcome for every business in the room, including the show itself.

The scope is US copyright only. A jurisdiction that answers by statute is outside it. Personality and voice rights are outside the scope. A lab with clean acquisition has no line item on the piracy claim. What remains for such a lab is the fair-use question as decided at district court for purchased books, which is not binding on the next defendant. For licensed or open corpora the binding constraint is the licence.

## Four checks

Each of these checks can fail.

1. Rightsholders whose product is the licensed corpus itself, such as music and legal databases, win or settle above $3,000 a book. Claims over trade books, where the use is at the level of commentary, settle at or below. If the newspaper case settles for less than the book figure, displacement is not what sets the price.
2. Whether any frontier lab writes, in a brief or a signed public paper, that training without consent is IP theft. If one does and its fair-use defense survives, the exposure from that quote was overpriced.
3. Whether the bundled crawler is split without a collective demand. If it is, collective withholding was never the leverage.
4. The next AI-training settlement's per-work number. A number of the same order as $3,000 supports a rate. A number an order of magnitude away means one negotiation was mistaken for a market. This last check is the signal to stop treating the number as a rate. Treating the number as a rate carries a cost: the next settlement can land at a fraction of it.

## What the price measures

Because ownership of outputs is unsettled and no party is free of the same conduct, any IP enforcement regime is selective. The question it answers is who gets protected. When the price is set by settlement, a company's exposure is the set of claims it has made in public about everyone else. The corpus without receipts is only the part that already has a number. The split between the two claims holds. The public figure prices copies that were never bought. A court had already ruled on learning from copies that were bought.

## How to practice this

1. Size a lab's exposure by counting the works it cannot show a receipt for. Multiply that count by $3,000. Notice that works with a receipt fall under the June 2025 ruling instead.
2. When someone says a lab stole the books, ask whether they mean copying without paying or learning from the text. Notice whether the speaker separates the two claims.
3. List the public positions a company has taken about everyone else's data. Notice whether any of them could be quoted in a complaint against that company.
4. Run the displacement test on a product. Ask whether it takes a sale from the source it learned from. Notice whether it only talks about the source or replaces it.
5. Open the February lab post on distillation attacks. Notice whether "industrial scale distillation attack" appears and whether "IP theft" is absent.
6. When the next AI-training settlement lands, compare its per-work number to $3,000. Notice whether it is the same order or an order of magnitude away. An order of magnitude away means one negotiation was mistaken for a market.
