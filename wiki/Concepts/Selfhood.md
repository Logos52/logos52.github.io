# Selfhood

Selfhood is the filter a person runs on their own life: a repeated decision, object by object and sentence by sentence, about what earns its place against a real use and what gets cut. What shows on the outside is only what the filter kept. Handwriting identifies its writer without being shaped for recognition. A wardrobe, habit, or page built by this filter works the same way, as years of small decisions, none of which was made in order to be seen.

The filter applies to what you keep, write, practise and say. It swaps an identity managed for an audience for standards you actually hold, and it fixes the habit of counting only what your presence costs others while ignoring what it gives.

## Which standard the decisions answer to

Whether a standard is private or public is the wrong sort. The better sort is integrated versus introjected: a standard you genuinely endorse versus one you took in whole and now enforce from inside. Introjection still seeks approval from self or others, so removing the audience does not remove it. In unobserved lab tasks, people shifted endurance toward whichever direction they had been told was the healthy sign and reported the change as involuntary.

Early effort does not settle the question, because audience-driven goals and owned goals look identical in the first week and only diverge somewhere between the second and fourth weeks. A check that holds needs three conditions. The standard was specified in advance, it can return a verdict you do not want, and you would still act on it if acting became socially costly.

## The filter on objects and habits

On objects, each keep-or-cut decision should cost something when it is wrong. A mistake packed across repeated moves keeps the filter honest. The clearest sign of an integrated standard is an item kept although it looks wrong, because it costs more in appearance than it returns and you keep it anyway.

On habits, streaks and quotas build a record anyone could have. A map of walked streets or a tree of unlocked capacities is yours because every point was a choice. Tracking raises how much people do an activity and lowers how much they enjoy it with no external incentive. Daily behaviour in a consistent context becomes automatic with no tracking. That is why the question of whose standard is in use gets asked once, when you choose the practice, and is never run as a metric. Checked after practice, the record is growth you can look at. Checked before, it becomes an audit that decides whether the day is permitted.

## One test for every medium

Remove the part that faces other people and see whether anything left serves your actual week. For prose, delete the first-person clause. If facts about the subject are lost, the clause was needed, and if only an impression of you is lost, it was display. The same removal takes a different form in each medium:

1. For habits, ask whether the metric would mean anything after a month off.
2. For objects, ask whether you would acquire them again today.
3. For purchases, name what they signal.
4. For rooms, ask what behaviour the layout makes automatic.

Display aimed at an audience is cut. Zero display is a direction to move in and never a level you reach, because status keeps changing form into restraint meant to be noticed, taste meant to be noticed, and humility used as a move.

Outside writing, the test must produce something you can observe, not a feeling you report, such as thirty days with no announcement, six weeks with no tracker, or two years of daily use with no visible wear. A felt answer to "would I still do this if nobody saw" is worth nothing. The filter is a decision procedure for the moment of choice, and it does not answer what a person is. Some acts are for how someone sees you by design, such as a gift, an introduction, or work shown to be hired.

## Meiwaku: counting burden before you act

迷惑 (*meiwaku*) names unnecessary trouble you export to others: noise, space, attention, time, energy. The habit is to count that burden before you act, cut what serves no purpose, and keep what does. Without the keep step, consideration becomes self-removal. The norm is wider than politeness, since it covers people who never chose to deal with you. It starts with public behaviour, and the same count extends to home, work, and life plans.

The habit goes wrong in four ways: self-erasure, quiet resentment without naming the norm, over-policing every inconvenience, and an aesthetic minimalism that still exports noise and shifted work. Five questions come before acting.

1. Does this take space, time or attention beyond what is needed?
2. Does it create ongoing work for someone else?
3. Is the communication useful, or does it only demand attention?
4. Would quieting this improve the shared environment?
5. Is there a burden here you could take on yourself or remove?

## Speaking: the ledger with one column missing

Speaking always reaches another person. Meiwaku works on visible mess because cost is all there is. Applied to speech, it records what a sentence takes and not what it gives, yet value is the only reason to say one. Calling a remark something that "consumes attention without returning value" admits that value exists and then never weighs it. Each reasonable decision to stay quiet adds up, over years, to silence, which is how the slide looks from inside.

[[wiki/Concepts/Good Faith|Good Faith]] prices withholding: a problem revealed early does far less damage than one found after trust is spent, so silence can be the imposition. The repair is bookkeeping, not courage. Judgment made in the room favors silence because cost is vivid and value is not. Rules made at the desk work better, because they fire whenever their stated conditions hold, and these six cover speaking.

1. Say the thing only you hold when it bears on the decision and would not surface from someone else within ten minutes.
2. When the public version is impossible, shrink the room and keep the sentence. Tell one person, send a message, or raise it in a working group.
3. When you hold something uncertain, ask it as a question, since questions often draw out what no one else will say.
4. Deliver the point inside the listener's frame so the hard part costs less to receive.
5. Ignore how you felt you landed afterwards. What counts is whether anyone used, quoted, or acted on what you said.
6. Work out positions in writing before the meeting exists, on an ordinary day such as a Tuesday.

Both sides of the ledger meet one bar: name the person and the specific thing gained or lost. Two checks from [[wiki/Concepts/The Two Meanings of Ego|The Two Meanings of Ego]] catch display dressed as contribution. One drops a different subject into the sentence to see whether it still works, and the other deletes the first-person clause to see what is lost. Checking your own motive fails on speech, because wanting to say a true thing and wanting to be seen saying it feel identical from inside. The strongest safeguard is a witness who will answer "useful or noise" straight.

Quiet stays correct in six cases.

1. The listener cannot leave.
2. The point is already in the room.
3. The contribution creates a recurring obligation for someone else.
4. The point is standing.
5. Your conviction is unsettled.
6. The room is already loud.

Everything else meiwaku asks for, such as taking less space, making less noise and leaving things cleaner, stays as before. Once both sides of a sentence are counted, the verdicts come back mixed, and the person stays careful without falling silent.

## What is left with no score

Some activity holds attention with no scoreboard at all. Its test is whether the thing still holds attention with no metric. The filters say which standards to refuse, but they do not name this state.
