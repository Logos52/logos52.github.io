# Wabi-Sabi

Wabi-sabi is a Japanese idea of beauty that values natural materials, simple forms and small imperfections. A designer uses it to make a room, an object or a page feel made by hand and cared for over time, in places where a flawless finish would feel cold. The amount matters, since a heavy dose makes the object look neglected.

## Core takeaways

- Wabi is the beauty of slight imperfection, like handmade pottery.
- Sabi is the beauty that comes with age, like patina on copper.
- The core ideals are impermanence, imperfection and incompleteness.
- The object stays tidy and cared for even with its flaws.
- Use it lightly, since too much breaks the effect.
- Choosing how much is a taste call made by a person.

## How it works

The word joins the two ideas above, wabi and sabi. A story from sixteenth-century Japan shows the style at work. The young Sen no Rikyu, told by his teacher to tend a garden, cleared and raked it until it was perfect, then shook a cherry tree so that a few flowers and leaves fell at random. The garden stayed tended, and the fallen leaves made it look natural. Wabi-sabi works the same way, with the care done first and a small mark of nature or time added on top.

- Start from order and care.
- Add a few signs of nature, age or the hand.
- Stop before the object looks neglected.

```
too perfect        wabi-sabi           neglected
machine-made  -->  tended, with   -->  "wabi-slobby"
cold               a few flaws         falling apart
```

## Against Western defaults

Traditional Western design prizes symmetry, manufactured forms and materials that last. Wabi-sabi prefers the asymmetry of living things and materials that age and wear. Interest in sustainability has moved some Western taste toward it. A wabi-sabi interior is clean and minimal, with unfinished natural materials and muted colours taken from nature.

| Western default | Wabi-sabi |
| --- | --- |
| Symmetry | Asymmetry |
| Straight lines, right angles | Crooked lines, curves |
| Synthetic, durable materials | Natural materials that age |
| Complete and finished | Incomplete, still changing |

## How to apply it

The style suits audiences in East Asia and audiences trained in art or design, both of which tend to prefer some asymmetry. It needs a light hand. A house that looks ready to collapse is too impermanent and too incomplete. The sculptor Deborah Butterfield builds horses from found scraps of metal and wood, and her work is an example of the style done with control.

- Natural colours, materials and finishes.
- Organic shapes and motifs.
- Visible signs of the hand or of age, used sparingly.
- Keep enough order that the object looks looked after.

## Why a person has to judge it

A design scorecard kept in this vault scores each design principle for how much human judgment it needs and for how much an AI can do alone. Wabi-sabi gets the highest score for judgment needed and the lowest for what an AI can do. There is no rule for how many flaws are enough, so the call depends on the audience and the object, and a person makes it by eye.

- Human judgment needed: 5 out of 5.
- What an AI can do alone: 1 out of 5.
