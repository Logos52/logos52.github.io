# Reverse Causality

Reverse causality is a note-making mistake where an idea is called important because of something it produces, as in "mRNA is important because it creates the protein". The reason sounds sensible, yet it only works for a learner who has already memorised the protein. Catching the mistake saves memorising a second fact for every idea explained this way.

## Takeaways

- Explaining an idea by its product adds a fact to memorise.
- The idea and its product form a closed loop with nothing outside.
- Explain an idea by what causes it or what needs it.
- Ask why the material matters, in depth, before reading it.
- A neat grouping can still hide the mistake.
- A fill-in sentence and one question catch it in seconds.

## How it works

Asking why an idea matters helps memory only when the answer comes from something the learner already knows. Take five ideas, A to E. A note that says "A matters because it creates B" gives A one link, to B, and the fact that A creates B has to be memorised on top. A note that says "A matters because it changes C and D, and E produces it" ties A to three ideas the learner already holds, and when B arrives it has a place to sit.

- Reverse link: A matters because it makes B.
  - Recalling A needs the memorised fact that A makes B.
  - A and B float on their own, cut off from the topic.
- Cause link: A matters because of what makes it or needs it.
  - A attaches to ideas already in hand.
  - B then attaches to A.

```
reverse:   A ---> B        (one memorised arrow)

cause:     E ---> A ---> C
                  |  \
                  v   v
                  B   D    (B lands in a network)
```

## How to check for it

The check takes one sentence and one question. Write "A is important because B", then ask whether you would know that only by having memorised that B comes from A. A yes means the importance was memorised, and the reason has to be drawn again from what causes A. A map full of these links often shows the problem on the page, since groups of ideas appear from nowhere and the main line of the topic is hard to find.

- Fill in: "A is important because B."
- Ask: is this known only by memorising that A makes B?
- Yes means rewrite the reason from what causes A.
- Warning signs on a map:
  - a group of ideas that appears from nowhere
  - no clear main line through the topic
  - a flow that is hard to follow

## How to prevent it

Most cases start before reading, when the learner asks why the material matters and stops at the first answer. Writing down the questions the material must answer, before opening it, gives each idea a cause to hang on. Grouping ideas by what feels naturally connected tends to avoid the mistake, while listing reasons point by point or drilling short questions and answers tends to create it. Research on asking why points the same way, since the question helps memory when prior knowledge can supply the answer.

- Write what the material must answer before reading it.
- Ground each idea's importance in the whole topic.
- Asking why helps when prior knowledge can answer it.
