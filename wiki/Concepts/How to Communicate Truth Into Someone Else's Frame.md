# How to Communicate Truth Into Someone Else's Frame

Communicating truth into someone else's frame is a way of selling: first work out what the other person wants, then say what you believe is true in terms they already hold. Done this way, people who see through sales tactics still listen, and you know when to stop pitching and move on.

## Core takeaways

- Credibility does more than sales technique.
- The people you most want notice a pitch at once.
- Only sell what you believe in.
- If it feels like selling, the thing or the fit is wrong.
- Learn what they want and why they hold their view.
- Honesty first, with kindness on top of it.
- Never pitch from a position you cannot walk away from.

## How to do it

The order is to check yourself, read the other person's frame, meaning what they want and why they hold their view, speak into it, and then let go of the outcome. Checking yourself means waiting until the thing itself excites you past a threshold, and going then, whatever the calendar says. It also means taking your ego out, so that the listener feels they are hearing their own thinking said back more clearly. Smart people have a real reason for what they say, and it can usually be found by asking.

- Reason your way to their position and test whether it holds.
- If it holds, say so.
- Agree with the valid part first, then add your case.
- Disagree outright only when you fully disagree, and say why.
- Set a larger context first: the problem, a story, some history.
- Explain simply, with a comparison the listener already has.
- Keep the truth whole, since the people who matter notice exaggeration.
- Deliver it with care, so they can act on it.
- If it does not land, move on and find who it suits.

```
believe it  ->  read their frame  ->  say it their way
    |                 |                      |
 not yet:        no reason found:       does not land:
 keep building   ask, then reason       move on
```

Refusing to sell something builds credibility for later sales. An estate agent who steers you away from bad houses is trusted when the right one comes along. A seller who pushes every option has used up that trust before the sale that matters.

## Leading the same way

Leading means making people want the work, while telling them what to do is management and does not get their best. The steps are the same as in selling: listen to what they want, find the overlap with what needs doing, then point them at it, since motivation that is faked does not hold. Working together is a stag hunt, where each hunter alone can catch a rabbit but two who commit together can take a stag and share far more meat.

- Keep a team as small as the job allows.
- Keep trust in the team high.
- A pitch to a recruit is a pitch to join a stag hunt.
- Let a candidate interview anyone on the team.
- Tell them to walk away if those people are not brilliant.

## Deals and walking away

A contract is two parties giving up future options in exchange for working together on a bigger shared result. It narrows your future, so you have to be convinced that no better path is open to you. Returns follow a power law, where the single top outcome is worth more than all the others together. That is the reason to keep time and options free for a large chance instead of fighting over a small one.

- The harder a deal is to unwind, the higher the bar.
- Board seats, veto rights and preferred stock are hard to unwind.
- Start raising money six to twelve months before it is needed.
- Then a bad deal can be refused.
- Fight only for large stakes or on principle.
- Otherwise walk away and keep your time, reputation and peace.
- A sinking feeling of obligation when signing is information.

## Where it fails

Robert Cialdini's book Influence lists six levers: consistency, liking, authority, scarcity, social proof and reciprocity. Stacking them into a pitch makes the listener feel sold to, so the list is at most something to run over an email. The other failures come from breaking the steps above.

- Pitching because the calendar says so, before the basics are ready.
- Kindness that removes the truth.
- Truth so harsh that nobody acts on it.
- Selling something you do not care about.
