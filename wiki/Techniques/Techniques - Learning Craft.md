# Techniques - Learning Craft

A study technique is a named method such as flashcards, mind maps or practice papers. The same technique can work well or badly depending on how it is done, because what a learner remembers depends on the thinking the technique makes them do. Each common technique has one change that makes it work better, and each part of learning has its own page in the wiki.

## Core takeaways

- How a technique is done matters more than which one is picked.
- Most upgrades add thinking to a technique that used to allow less.
- Confidence marks a knowledge gap better than a right answer does.
- Pre-study gives the big picture and leaves out the details.
- Mnemonics and flashcards suit isolated facts and fail on connected knowledge.
- Rereading, highlighting and rewatching stay close to useless.

## The upgrades

Most techniques have a default way of use that limits what they give. A small change, usually one that makes the learner decide, compare or build something, moves several of them up a long way. A few techniques change little whatever is done with them: Cornell notes give a limited benefit even when done well, and the Feynman technique is hard to do badly. Flow during a study session helps, but it does not rescue a method that was weak to begin with.

- Practice papers: hide the answer sheet and write your own model answers.
  - Count an unsure answer as a gap, even a correct one.
  - Friends can write papers for each other and compare their answers.
- Pomodoro: time how long focus lasts instead of stopping at 25 minutes.
  - Rest for a quarter to a third of the focus session.
  - Rest with a walk or meditation.
  - A game runs past the break, so rest without one.
- Active recall: change the kind of recall at each spaced session.
  - Low level: facts and definitions.
  - High level: problems, applications, discussion and practice.
- Pre-study: stop once you can name the main ideas and their links.
  - Being able to explain the processes means it went too deep.
- Brain dumps and summaries: rebuild the topic in a new structure.
- Mind maps: decide the place, links and weight of every idea.
- Mnemonics: keep them for lists used like a checklist.
- Flashcards: use them only for the facts they suit.
- Music: play it only during tedious low-effort work.

A brain dump in a new structure works because an expert can explain a topic from several angles, while a beginner can only repeat the order it was learned in. Hard thinking leaves no spare attention for music, so if music makes studying easier to sit through, the study method is probably too passive.
