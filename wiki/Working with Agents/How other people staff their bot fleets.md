# How other people staff their bot fleets

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Grouped by how they are built, the public fleets fall into four shapes, a working grouping rather than one any source makes. The most common puts a chief of staff bot on top, which takes the owner's requests and hands each one to a bot with a single job. Marketing and sales teams often chain bots instead, so each bot finishes its step and triggers the next. Companies also build shared team bots that a whole function uses, and many fleets include a bot whose job is to build, rewrite and audit the other bots. A catalogue of 149 publicly described bots, 117 of them confirmed by a source link, has marketing as its biggest group with 20 bots, then sales with 15 bots and engineering with 14 bots. About half of those bots act when someone talks to them, and about a third run on a schedule. The official setup advice points the same way, starting with the smallest roster that works and adding a bot only when a job has become a stable specialty.

## Start small and add bots for stable jobs

The official guidance on teams sets the order of growth. One bot owns an outcome from end to end, and another bot joins only when part of the work has turned into a steady specialist role. Bots go into a shared group chat when the handoff between them needs to be visible, and anything that reaches outside the team waits behind a clear approval. The marketing guide adds that a project manager bot should arrive only after the handoffs start to hurt.

## A chief of staff over one-job specialists

Most fleets that individuals have shown in public follow this shape, with the owner talking to one bot and that bot routing the work.

1. An engineer runs a work fleet. She talks to a chief of staff, which passes coding work to an engineering lead bot whose standing instruction is never to do work itself and always to delegate. The lead hands work to three or four engineer bots, and those engineer bots run Cursor cloud agents. For a big project the chief of staff first plans the phases in a Notion document.
2. One creator runs eleven bots. A chief bot handles email and delegates, and a separate bot builds new bots and improves the existing ones. The rest plan the week, find video ideas, write scripts, track website traffic and find trending posts on X. Others sell things, watch flight prices, find family activities, keep up with school emails and errands, track workouts and clean up files and subscriptions.
3. Another creator runs thirty or so agents. One chief bot sweeps six inboxes and Slack and coordinates the rest. Their jobs include printing a family newspaper for the children, closing the queue of code changes, monitoring security controls, customer support, auditing subscriptions and personal shopping.
4. A twenty-two-bot fleet is split by department and modelled on a human company. On the personal side are bots for health, travel and wardrobe, and the business side has social media and revenue bots and a chief of staff. A bot-making bot creates the others and keeps an organization chart.
5. The official work and product guides each show a fleet. In the work guide an inbox manager, a calendar assistant and an intelligence scout all report to one boss bot. The product guide adds an engineering manager over five engineer bots, a data analyst, a product manager bot and a recruiter, and every bot reads one shared attention list as its filter.

## Chains that hand work down a line

When each step's output is the next step's input, teams wire bots in a line so that one bot finishing wakes the next.

1. A marketing workshop's video description lists a chain of bots. A trend-spotting bot feeds an event-planning bot, then an event-page bot, an attendee finder, an outreach bot and an approvals bot. Bots that need each other share a group chat, and outside email stays a draft.
2. The official marketing team has six bots. A market researcher feeds a product marketer, who feeds a performance marketer and a website operations bot, and an analyst reads the results. A project manager bot runs the handoffs.
3. The official Founder Mode sales team has nine bots. A chief of staff sends an 8 AM list and sits over outreach, selling, inbox, insights, product, stock, content and social bots. The social bot passes warm leads to outreach, and the inbox bot passes what customers say to insights, which passes it to product.
4. The official recruiting team has four bots. One bot sources candidates overnight, one writes a note before each interview, one turns weak points into first-call questions and one drafts feedback from interview transcripts.
5. A video creator runs a small team. A news bot sends a daily digest, a script bot turns that research into scripts and an expenses bot files receipts, and the whole team holds a daily standup in a group chat.

## Shared bots for a whole team

Some companies build a bot once and share it, so everyone in a function works with the same bot. One field engineer estimated that about 90% of the bots in his company are team bots.

1. A sales team shares a set of bots. An account intelligence bot prepares reps for calls, reads call transcripts afterwards and drafts follow-ups. A quote bot builds pricing proposals and order forms, shows a preview and asks before it changes the sales database, then sends exceptions to a deal bot. The deal bot handles unusual terms and discounts and stamps each approval with the decision, the reason and the person responsible. A competitive intelligence bot and a sales training bot sit beside them.
2. One Galaxy livestream host has team bots for marketing review, social media review and brand design, shared with colleagues through Slack, where some people own a bot and others only use it.

## Bots that build and check the fleet

As fleets grow, owners add a bot that looks after the other bots. The engineer's bot-maker designs new bots and audits the fleet on a schedule, with a transcript check every weekday morning and a routine check every Monday, and it proposes changes for her to pick from. Her release flow chains bots too. A release manager bot asks contributors for objections, starts a build and sends a swarm of ten or more agents to test it, then calls an engineer bot to triage and fix what they find. A daily loop takes feedback from Slack, has a cloud agent reproduce the bug and fix it, tests the fix and merges it after an hour unless she objects.
