# The Personal Uniform

Minimalist fashion has a reputation for sameness: picture it and a black t-shirt appears, maybe a capsule wardrobe of identical basics, the same outfit worn like a logo. Spend any time actually looking at minimalists, though, and the picture falls apart. Steve Jobs ran the black tee and blue jeans. Sibu, the Japanese minimalist, wears all black and all baggy. The uniforms repeat daily, but no two of them match — every committed minimalist seems to converge on a look that's distinctly their own. The constraint doesn't produce sameness; it produces signatures.

The mechanism is the repetition itself. Wearing few things daily is a filter running constantly: whatever doesn't earn its place gets worn out of the rotation, and what survives is, by definition, the things that fit one particular life — its climate, its work, its taste. Two people running the same filter over two different lives end up with wardrobes that look nothing alike, doing the identical thing.

The filter runs harsher overseas, and the honest reason is that bad purchases amplify there. I make them sometimes, like anyone — but at home a bad purchase retires quietly to a closet. Living abroad on one-year apartment contracts, everything bought has to be carried to the next country, so a mistake isn't a sunk cost, it's recurring freight. The stakes keep the filter honest.

Mine came out Everyman but distinct — rarely all black, more dark blues and dark browns. The signature pieces: 3/4 sleeve shirts, samue — the Japanese monk's workwear — and noragi blazers, the farmer's field jacket, with cropped pants. Linen and cotton for materials, and a dislike of microplastics that decides as many purchases as the look does.

And then Crocs, which don't match the aesthetic at all and belong anyway: minimalist in spirit rather than in style, and exactly right for HCMC weather. That one item gives the game away about what the signature is actually optimizing. It was never the look — it's the spirit: few things, each earning its keep against a real life, with the look emerging as a side effect. A wardrobe that kept the aesthetic and dropped the usefulness would have it backwards.

The filter doesn't stop at clothes. The everyday carry — EDC — runs it at higher frequency: clothes rotate through a week, but the carry is the same handful of objects every single day, so anything that doesn't earn its place announces itself within a month. The current verdicts: an iPhone Air with no case, the phone carried as built. A MacBook Pro M3 14", in a MOFT origami sleeve that folds into a stand — the one added object in the kit, holding two jobs to pay for its place. AirPods Pro 3 rather than bulky headphones, though the headphones are under consideration — the filter isn't dogma, and if the bigger thing earns its keep it gets in. And a Filson green Journeyman backpack holding all of it: two years as the daily staple, showing no signs of aging or wear at all.

The whole kit is tried and tested, with years of traveling across Asia behind it — and travel is the filter at its harshest, because a carry has to earn its place in an airport as much as at a desk. The Filson is the longest-serving proof. Most items in a carry are still on trial; the strongest verdicts are the ones that stopped being decisions years ago and just get picked up on the way out the door. Two years of daily use without visible wear means the decision was right once and never needed making again. A carry that can be listed from memory, in one breath, is a carry that's been decided.

Which is what the sameness reputation gets wrong. The black tee was never the point, and copying it produces a costume, not a uniform. What the minimalists have in common is the convergence — the filter that keeps running until the wardrobe and the bag are small, settled, and unmistakably somebody's. The uniforms only look interchangeable from far away; up close, they're as identifying as handwriting.
