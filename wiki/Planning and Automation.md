# Planning and Automation

Planning and Automation is how a block of work is sequenced and what a machine runs without a person. The automation half asks which tasks sink below the waterline when tools get cheaper and which roles absorb new work. The agent half asks how you plan one piece of work with a model, how an outer loop keeps planned work running, and how named programs such as ICS, prestudy-bear-hunter-retrieval, and thirty-day drills order a learning block.

## Automation and the job iceberg

Automation usually expands roles instead of deleting them. Over decades of census job titles, only the elevator operator vanished because the role had nothing new to take on when the task got cheap. Bank tellers, pilots, writers, and accountants were all predicted to disappear and instead grew as machines took tasks. A job is the work of turning an input into an output. The tasks along the way are not the job.

Beneath the visible economy sits demand that never became work because making the thing cost too much. Cheaper tools, including coding agents, can bring some of that demand to the surface. The record looks backward. It does not protect a person who refuses the new tools. The useful pair of tests is the operator test, whether the role has anything new to take on once tasks get cheaper, and the pilot test, whether people still want a responsible human present when things go wrong.

## Plan work with an agent

Jobs too large for one brief need a sequence. Name the outcome, the proof you will accept, and what comes later. Reverse goal setting works backward from a finished state: write what "done" looks like, then list the last step before that, then the step before that, until you reach today. That keeps agent plans from drifting into generic scaffolding.

Plan one narrow stage at a time, the same move as handing a single task without micromanaging it. Link where to read. Say what to achieve and leave the method open. When the same correction keeps returning, change the setup instead of repeating the correction.

## Outer loops and weekly arenas

An outer loop is how planned work keeps running after the first session. A named weekly arena is one shape: a fixed slot where you review what agents shipped, what stalled, and what rule or skill needs a permanent fix. The arena is not the work. It is the clock that makes the work visible.

Recurring passes belong in the thread or in a bot profile, not only in your memory. A start-of-day file review, a Thursday check of open pulls, or a scheduled fleet health scan each qualify as outer loops when the agent can run them without you retyping the prompt.

## ICS, prestudy, and thirty-day blocks

The ICS program is a phased study map: understanding before technique piles, dimensions of learning as the spine, and technique pages that restate the map folded away into the hubs. The program map is the course order for that same system. Prestudy, bear hunter, and spaced retrieval name a pipeline for turning information into usable structure. Prestudy sets up the material. Bear hunter encodes it. Retrieval schedules the return. The separate technique pages on the learning door keep the moves; this page holds how they sequence.

Thirty-day challenges are four-week practice plans, one per trainable dimension. Each track starts by noticing how that dimension already works, then changes one thing until a weak spot shows under ordinary study. The hub index listed mindset, self-management, self-regulation, deep processing, and retrieval tracks. Individual challenge pages stay on the learning door.
