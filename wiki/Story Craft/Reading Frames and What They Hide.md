# Reading Frames and What They Hide

A reading frame is the set of questions a reader brings to a character, such as a therapist's diagnostic checklist or a screenwriter's arc chart. Each frame makes some things about the character easy to see and leaves others invisible. Knowing which frame a critique uses tells a writer which of its verdicts to act on and which to set aside.

- Every frame arrives with its own list of what counts.
- A diagnostic checklist measures harm to real people.
- A frame that looks for improvement calls a flat arc no arc.
- A checklist reads a surprise as inconsistency.
- Name the frame before acting on its verdict.
- Two frames can disagree about charm, and both stay useful.

## A worked example

In an April 2023 episode of Cinema Therapy, a YouTube show, the therapist Jonathan Decker diagnoses Jack Sparrow from Pirates of the Caribbean with antisocial personality disorder. He goes through the criteria one at a time: no concern for other people's feelings, no remorse, broken promises and unpaid debts, impulsive action without a plan, lies, and using people and then dropping them. The second film gives Decker his clearest case. Jack offers his friend Will to Davy Jones in his own place, and when asked whether he can live with that, he says yes.

- The frame: a clinical checklist of behaviours.
- The remorse test: indifference to, or excuses for, hurting others.
- What it sees: a man who treats people as tools.
- What it scores: a person's harm to real people.

## What the frame hides

The same checklist, applied to a story, misses how the character is built. Decker's co-host notes that in the first film Jack wants his ship, the Black Pearl, at the start and at the end, and calls that zero growth. Read as a flat arc, where the character holds steady and the people around him change, the same fact is how the film is built, since Will is the one who changes. E. M. Forster's test for a round character is whether they can surprise the reader in a convincing way, and a checklist reads that surprise as inconsistency.

| Frame | Looks for | Sees in Jack |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clinical checklist | harm to other people | a disorder |
| Positive arc | improvement | no arc |
| Flat arc | change in the world around him | Will changes |
| Round character | a convincing surprise | yes, in film three |

- His crew rescues him in the third film, and nobody missed him.
- At the end he gives up immortality to save Will.
- The clinical frame calls this a buried wish for connection.
- The craft frame calls it a surprise that was set up.

## Charm

The two frames split hardest on charm. In the clinical reading, Jack's charm is how he gets away with harm, and the same charm is why real people with this pattern hurt others again and again. In the story reading, charm is how the film tells the audience to stay with him. This desk has not settled which reading is right, and both are kept.

- Charm as exemption: it lets harm pass.
- Charm as signal: it tells the audience whom to follow.

## How to use a frame

A list of criteria already contains a judgement, because it decides in advance what counts as good or bad. Before acting on a critique, name the frame and ask what it was built to measure. A character diagnosis on this wiki grades how a character is built on the page. A character's values show in the price they pay to keep them.

- Name the frame.
- Ask what it was built to score.
- Keep only the verdicts that answer the writer's question.
