# The Character Blueprint

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

A character blueprint is a first appearance that works as a template. Instead of one memorable moment, it sets up the pattern every later scene with that character will repeat and vary. The standard example is Jack Sparrow's entrance in the first Pirates of the Caribbean film. In about two minutes and with almost no dialogue, it builds him up as a grand captain, then reveals a sinking dinghy. He then steps ashore as if all of it were the plan. That gap between how he presents and what is real, and his refusal to admit the gap, drives the rest of the film.

## Core takeaways

- A character moment shows one trait. A blueprint shows the shape that later scenes keep varying.
- The grand half has to be played straight, so the reveal that undercuts it lands hard.
- A small act with no witnesses and nothing to gain can show values faster than dialogue.
- An entrance can also tell the audience what kind of film they are watching.
- The opening works as a promise of what spending time with this person will be like.

## The build

The film opens on a low angle looking up at him, standing high on what looks like a ship's mast. Low angles lend authority, so before he does anything the camera has called him important. The camera then cranes up into an over-the-shoulder view, which lifts the audience toward him and puts us inside his point of view. The shot is grand enough that it reads as how he sees himself, a legendary captain.

A cut shows his face for the first time, with a slow push-in. He gives almost nothing away, still and watchful, with wind in his hair and low golden light behind him. Even his leap from the mast is shot from below, holding the heroic frame to the last moment.

## The correction

Then his feet hit the water, and a wide shot shows the "ship" is a small battered dinghy. It has taken about 22 seconds and four shots, and the joke has also told the audience the film is a comedy. The build is what makes the joke land. Without the slow climb of grand shots first, the dinghy reveal would hit far softer.

## The dock scene

At the port, the crowd reacts before we see him. Workers and sailors stop and stare, standing in for the audience's own disbelief. He sails in upright on the mast, unbothered, while the boat sinks under him. At the last possible moment he steps onto the dock, timed like a magic trick. That step says more about how he treats failure than backstory could.

## The pattern the reveal sets up

The entrance sets the film's visual logic. Heroic framing and shabby reality will keep colliding, and he will bridge the gap by refusing to see it. Disaster will keep finding him, and he will step clear at the last instant, acting as if that was the plan. Every later scene with him is a variation on that shape.

## The action with no audience

While the dinghy sinks, he stops and salutes his dead fellow pirates. It confirms he is a pirate, and more usefully, a pirate who keeps a code of loyalty. Showing a value through a chosen act, rather than stating it, is one of the oldest tools in screenwriting. Nobody is watching, he gains nothing, and his own boat is going down.

## Whether the salute has anything at stake

The salute costs him nothing visible, so its weight comes from what it admits. The scene could have been played purely for a laugh. Instead it plays as recognition. He knows what he is and how the world treats his kind, and the salute marks a fate he has kept narrowly dodging. That depth arrives in about four seconds without a word.

## The dock master

His first lines extend the pattern into speech. He is the one in trouble, yet he starts bargaining at once. He offers a few coins and asks the harbor master to forget his name, and he leaves with the man's purse. Everything is negotiable, and charm is money. He arrived on a sinking boat and left richer than he came.

## What the first two minutes have established

By the end of the entrance, the audience already knows a great deal about him.

- He is a pirate with a code and real loyalty to his own kind.
- He is broke and has no proper ship.
- He treats authority as something to bargain with, not obey.
- He outlasts disaster rather than avoiding it.
- He somehow comes out ahead every time.
- The camera is on his side and will frame him as a hero even when the situation says otherwise.

The entrance also makes the audience a promise. Spend time with this person, and he will surprise and entertain you.

## The seven steps

The reusable moves form a sequence, not a formula:

- Decide the one gap the character lives in, between how they present and what is true.
- Build the presentation fully and without irony.
- Reveal the truth in a single cut or line.
- Show how the character responds to the gap, since that response is the template.
- Add one small private act that shows what they value.
- Let the first exchange with another person repeat the same pattern in speech.
- Keep the camera or narration on the character's side, so the audience is invited in before it is asked to judge.

## How to practice this

- Pick a character you are writing and name their gap in one sentence.
- Draft an entrance of under a page that builds the presentation, then corrects it in a single beat.
- Check the next three scenes with that character and mark where each varies the entrance's shape.

## Open questions

- How do I find the single gap a character lives in before I write their entrance?
- How do I check that later scenes vary the blueprint instead of repeating it?
- How do I build an entrance like this for a character whose presentation and reality match?
