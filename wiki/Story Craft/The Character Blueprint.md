# The Character Blueprint

A character blueprint is an introduction that shows a character so fully that every later scene plays as a variation on it. Most introductions give one moment, and the audience learns one trait. A blueprint gives the writer a pattern to vary for the rest of the story, and it tells the audience what time with this person will be like.

## Core takeaways

- A blueprint sets the pattern every later scene repeats with changes.
- The first two minutes can carry the whole character.
- A choice made with nothing to gain tells more than dialogue.
- Show the character's grand self-image first, then undercut it.
- The introduction also tells the audience what kind of story this is.
- End the introduction with the character a little ahead.

## The worked example

Jack Sparrow's entrance in Pirates of the Caribbean: The Curse of the Black Pearl (2003) runs close to nine minutes over several scenes. The first two minutes lay the foundation, with almost no dialogue. The camera first frames him as a great captain, then shows his real situation, and he behaves as if that situation were the plan. Everything he does later in the film is a version of that pattern.

- Low angle on a figure on a mast: a man in command.
- The camera cranes up over his shoulder: his view of himself.
- A push-in on his face in golden light, giving nothing away.
- He leaps down, and a wide shot shows a small, sinking dinghy.
- Four shots and 22 seconds tell the audience this is a comedy.
- He stops to salute three hanged pirates as he passes.
- At the dock, workers stop and stare before he is shown.
- The boat sinks under him as he sails into port.
- He steps onto the dock at the last possible second.
- He talks the dock master down, then walks off with his purse.

```
epic framing  -->  shabby reality  -->  acts as if planned
(low angle,        (sinking             (steps off dry,
 crane, light)      dinghy)              leaves richer)
```

## What each beat tells the audience

Each beat in the sequence adds one fact about the character, and the facts stack. The salute lasts about four seconds, with no one watching and nothing to gain, so it shows a code and a loyalty to his own kind. The dock step shows how he handles failure: he lets the boat sink and steps clear at the last moment. The bargain with the dock master shows that he treats a rule as an opening price and talks his way past it.

- The salute: a pirate with a code, aware of how pirates end.
- The staring workers: their disbelief stands in for the audience's.
- The dock step: failure handled by exact timing.
- "What do you say to three shillings, and we forget the name."
- He arrives on a sinking boat and leaves with more money.

## How to build one

The dinghy reveal is funny because the build-up before it was fully heroic. If the scene had opened on the close-up, the wide shot would have landed far softer. On the page the order is the same: set up how the character presents, then show the reality, then show how the character closes the gap between the two. After the introduction, check that a stranger could list what they now know about the character and about the story.

- Decide the one gap the character lives in.
- Stage how the character presents at full strength first.
- Undercut it with a plain fact of the situation.
- Show the character's own way of bridging the gap.
- Add one private choice that shows a value.
- Close on a small win that sets up later scenes.

After two minutes the film's audience knows a list of facts: a pirate with a code, broke and without a ship, quick with words, able to outlast disaster, and usually ahead at the end. They also know the film sits between the epic and the absurd, and that the camera will keep framing him as the hero.
