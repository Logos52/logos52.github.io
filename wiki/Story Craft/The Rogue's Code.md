# The Rogue's Code

A rogue's code is a list of things a thief will not do. It names people he will not rob, methods he will not use, or a claim he will not make about himself. It says nothing about what the stealing is for.

The earliest one written down in English is a ban in a printed ballad from around 1500: "loke ye do no husbonde harme / That tilleth with his ploughe". Do not rob the man who works a plough. The ban heads a list, spare this man and spare that one, with the ploughman at the front of it.

A thief and a rogue can take the same purse and land on opposite sides of an audience's judgement. The difference is not in the taking. An audience forgives the taking. It does not forgive a lie about the taking.

## Core takeaways

- A code is a list of refusals. The oldest one on record, in a ballad printed around 1500, names who not to rob, with the ploughman first.
- The modern rogue keeps one refusal: he will cheat you, and he will not claim to be better than a man who cheats you. That is honest dishonesty.
- The refusal covers what kind of man he is. Names, plans and whereabouts are free to lie about, and he lies about them constantly.
- Forgiveness is scored against the other characters, never against zero. Around him a trading company, a navy and men holding letters of marque do more harm while claiming legitimacy.
- A restraint only counts as a moral core once the character pays for it. In the 2003 pirate film and its sequel the restraint is stated five times and never costs him anything he wants.
- The mechanism is shown in stories only. In life the conduct is deplorable, and nothing here is a guide to judging people.

## What does not explain the difference

Two characters pick the same pocket. One of them the audience wants caught. The other they want to get away with it. Nothing in the act separates them: same purse, same fingers, same owner walking away lighter. Whatever is sorting them, it is not the theft.

The obvious answers do not survive the material. The size of the crime is not doing the sorting. The lead of a 2003 pirate film stands at a guardhouse and announces that he means to commandeer a ship, pick up a crew, and raid, pillage and plunder. That is grand theft declared in advance, and he keeps the audience anyway. Sympathy for the victim is not doing it either. The same figure cheats his allies and sells out a friend, and the audience stays with him.

Charm is the serious candidate and it stays live. The sourced reading of this figure says outright that part of why he gets away with things is that he is charming enough to be let off. [[wiki/Concepts/Charisma|Charisma]] puts the whole exemption there, in the pull a person exerts on the people judging him. The claim here is that it sits somewhere else. The disagreement is real and has never been tested.

What separates the two pickpockets is what each one carries alongside the act.

## What a code is

What a rogue carries has been on record for five centuries, and every entry has the same form. The gentleman thief is defined by manners, charm, courtesy, and the avoidance of force or intimidation in the stealing, and by the selectivity of his targets: the wealthy, or the rare and difficult object. Those are constraints of two kinds, one on method and one on target. The same entry lists his motives, and there are plenty of them: material wealth, the thrill of the act itself, correcting a moral wrong. The archetype wants things the way anyone wants things. It is identified by what it refuses.

The ballad is the oldest entry and the plainest. The plough-ban heads an actual list: spare the husbandman, spare the good yeoman, spare the knight or squire who will be a good fellow. The redistribution everyone remembers is in the same ballad, money handed to the next traveller "yf he be a por man." The two arrive differently. The giving is a disposition, something he does when the traveller turns out to be poor. The ban is the thing he hands his men as an instruction. That split is this page's reading, and the popular summaries run the other way, leading with the man who robs the rich to give to the poor. The ballad supports both. The code is the half his men are told to keep.

The other entries show the same form from other directions. An outlaw is a person declared outside the protection of the law, and a man with no legal standing has only the standing he imposes on himself. That last step is an inference, but a short one. The social bandit of the 1969 definition is the peasant outlaw whom lord and state count a criminal while his own people count him a hero. Historians have contested the larger thesis ever since, so it serves here as a definition and never as settled history. The trickster is the "boundary-crosser" who violates the principles of social and natural order, playfully disrupting normal life and then re-establishing it on a new basis.

Restraints tell a stranger what is off the table, and motives never do. A goal tells you what a person will chase. A restraint tells you what he will not do to you while he chases it, and only the second is something a stranger can rely on. Goals predict pursuit. Restraints predict what he will not do to the people in front of him, and trust runs entirely on the second. A figure with no legal standing builds his reputation out of prohibitions, which is why the oldest code on record is a list of who not to rob.

## Honest dishonesty

The modern rogue compresses the ballad's whole list into the one restraint that carries most of the weight: he will cheat you, and he will not claim to be better than a man who cheats you. This page calls it honest dishonesty. Both halves matter. The cheating is real and stays real, with no softening and no secretly-noble reveal, and no claim of virtue is attached to it anywhere.

The restraint has a precise edge, and the precision is most of its content. What he will not misrepresent is the kind of man he is. The particulars, meaning names, plans and whereabouts, are free to lie about, and he lies about them constantly. In the pirate film the proof sits inside a single exchange: a false name given moments before a complete and true confession of criminal intent. The category is delivered straight while the details are fiction.

The refusal of virtue and one other habit of his are a single move. He never acknowledges any gap between his presentation and his reality. The grand manner and the shabby facts collide constantly and he declines to notice. He also never accepts an upgrade to his category, even one offered warmly. Refusing the compliment and refusing to admit the gap are the same act: he keeps no second account of himself. The refusal is sharpest exactly where it would be cheapest to break, when righteousness arrives as a sincere compliment and he hands it back. [[wiki/Concepts/Good Faith|Good Faith]] reads honesty as a cooperation signal, where the disclosure that cost something is the one that can be believed. The rogue splits that signal in two, abandoning transactional honesty outright and keeping honesty of self-representation absolutely.

## The five moments in the films

The 2003 pirate film and its sequel state the restraint five times, and taken in order the five build into the whole of it. Nothing else about the plot is needed.

At sword's point in a blacksmith's shop, accused with "You cheated," he answers with the label: "Pirate." No denial, no justification. The accusation is received as a description.

Moments later in the same duel, told he ignored the rules of engagement and that in a fair fight he would be dead, he answers: "And that's not much incentive for me to fight fair then, is it?" The method is defended as effective. It is never defended as fair.

At a harbour guardhouse, ordered to state his business "and no lies," he gives a false name and then the truth entire: he means to commandeer a ship, pick up a crew, and raid, pillage and plunder. "I said no lies," one guard objects. The other reasons that if he were telling the truth he wouldn't have told them. The answer: "Unless, of course, he knew you wouldn't believe the truth…" The exchange is the evidence. A true confession of the category is unbelievable, because nobody expects the category to be stated. The false name sits in the same exchange, moments earlier, which is the edge of the restraint drawn exactly.

With an opponent hanging off a boom over open water and a sword in his own hand, he states the code's whole jurisprudence: "The only rules that really matter are these... What a man can do, and what a man can't do." Where the line is spoken from matters later: total advantage, nothing at risk for the speaker.

In the sequel, offered a sincere "you'll discover… that you're a good man," he refuses it: "All evidence to the contrary." The compliment is real, it costs nothing to accept, and he declines it anyway. The only price of the refusal is the credit itself.

The films also show the restraint working with nobody watching. He enters the first film on a small boat that is sinking under him, and spends about four seconds of the arrival saluting the remains of hanged pirates strung up at the harbour mouth. No audience, no dialogue, nothing to gain. The reading that fits is recognition: he knows what he is and what the world does to men of his kind, and the salute acknowledges a fate he has spent a career narrowly escaping. Minutes later he talks his way past a dock challenge with a small payment and a false name, and walks off richer than he arrived. Authority is treated as something to talk down, not something to obey. Heroic presentation and shabby reality collide over and over in those two minutes, and he bridges them by declining to admit the gap exists. [[wiki/Story Craft/The Character Blueprint|The Character Blueprint]] reads the same two minutes shot by shot, as the model of how an introduction sets every scene that follows. This page takes from them only the salute and the refusal.

The sequel shows the restraint with the sentiment stripped out. Challenged over a bargain he did not keep, he concedes the debt without argument and haggles the number. Asked to his face whether he can live with condemning a friend to a lifetime of servitude in his place, he says yes, and asks to seal the deal. The restraint is not a softening. The man who will not misrepresent his kind will sell a friend and say so out loud, and the second fact is what keeps the first from being a virtue in disguise. The people around him price him accordingly. Everyone who deals with him knows what he is and knows he will hurt them if it suits him, and they deal with him anyway. His self-description holds its value in-world, so the mechanism runs among the characters and not only in the audience.

A symptom-list reading files all of the above under indifference to rules, egocentrism, and absence of remorse, and it can do nothing else with the material, because a criterion list has no slot for a restraint. Why that frame goes blind at exactly this point is the argument of [[wiki/Story Craft/Reading Frames and What They Hide|Reading Frames and What They Hide]].

## Why the audience forgives him

Small takings earn no forgiveness here. The announced plan was to raid, pillage and plunder. The forgiveness comes from the fact that nothing is ever claimed about the taking.

Around the rogue, every antagonist does worse while claiming legitimacy: a trading company, a navy, and men holding letters of marque, which are government warrants to raid. The commissions are real, and the harm is larger. Everyone in that world is corrupt and only one of them is not lying about it. Nobody on screen says that line, but it is the world the films stage.

So the scoring is comparative. Nobody watching concludes that the rogue is good, and he does not have to be. The forgiveness is scored against the other characters, never against zero. The injury on the other side has a name too. A character who announces he will rob you leaves your knowledge of him usable: he can be priced, planned around, dealt with at your own risk. A character who robs you under a warrant has made your knowledge of him worthless, and worthless knowledge is the injury the story does not forgive. The audience is not adding up harms. It is asking which figure can be relied on to be what he says he is.

Fiction reached the same finding long before the pirate film, from the function side. The Spanish picaresque got there four and a half centuries earlier, and the founding novel is from 1554. Calling it the same finding is this page's join, so the definitions that follow are the genre's and the conclusion is the page's. The picaresque hero is a sympathetic outsider untouched by the false rules of society. The founding novel's protagonist is an alienated outsider whose ability to expose and ridicule the compromised people around him gives him a revolutionary stance. The genre's own definition sets him, always, in a corrupt society. Put the three together and the rogue has a job: he makes everyone else's compromise visible. He can only do that job if he is making no claim of his own. A picaro who insisted on his own virtue would show nothing about anyone else. The trickster definition closes the loop with its second half, disruption and then re-establishment on a new basis. The rogue skips the second half. He replaces nothing and repairs nothing. He makes the corruption legible and leaves it standing.

Two threads run on from here. The picaresque function is the social form of the flat arc in [[wiki/Story Craft/Arc Types|Arc Types]], a character who already holds his truth and turns it on the world around him, with the note that the truth held can be disreputable. And a whole form carrying one proposition across four and a half centuries is a controlling idea working at genre scale, the counterweight [[wiki/Story Craft/Premise and Controlling Idea|Premise and Controlling Idea]] keeps against the rule of one play, one premise.

## Stating the rule instead of hiding it

It is worth being exact about what kind of object this code is. Its nearest neighbour is the hidden law: the cynic who keeps one rule absolutely and conceals it, letting the world believe he has none, a figure who would rather be thought lawless than caught keeping the rule. Both figures keep a rule. The difference is what each does with the audience's knowledge of it. The hidden law conceals the rule and lets everyone misread him. The rogue states his rule from the first scene and refuses only the credit for keeping it.

Both figures belong to [[wiki/Story Craft/The Moral Core|The Moral Core]], where a law becomes real the way anything in fiction becomes real: the character pays for it, and the audience believes the price, never the speech. That standard matters here, because a code that is only announced is a line of dialogue.

## Five conditions for a rogue

For a writer building a rogue, five conditions have to hold and each one can be checked.

1. **The transgression is real and unminimised.** The audience sees the cheating and the story does not soften it. The bar is the debt scene: the claim conceded, the price haggled, the plain yes.
2. **A restraint exists and reads as a prohibition.** It shows in what he declines, the way the ballad's code shows in who goes unrobbed.
3. **Self-description is accurate about kind.** No claim of virtue stands, including one offered as a compliment. The particulars may be fiction. The category may not. The films hold only in this narrow form: the false name beside the true confession.
4. **The surrounding legitimacy does worse under a warrant**, on screen, at larger scale. The other characters are what the forgiveness is scored against.
5. **The restraint is charged at least once**, in a moment where declining to claim virtue costs the figure something he wants. This is the price rule from the moral core, used as it stands.

Here is the honest verdict on the films. Conditions one, two and four are satisfied plainly. Condition three holds only in the narrow form. Condition five is open. The code's grandest statement, what a man can do and what a man can't do, is delivered while the other man hangs from a boom and the speaker holds the sword: total advantage, nothing staked. Across the material the restraint is never charged. No scene exists in which claiming virtue, or concealing his kind, would have bought him the thing he was chasing, and he declined and paid. By the price rule, the films do not yet show a moral core. That is a limit on what they prove rather than a fault in them. They prove the restraint can be stated in a way nobody forgets. They do not prove the restraint was ever paid for.

## What the code protects

The ballad handed armed men a list of who not to rob, with the man behind the plough at its head. Five centuries later the code keeps the same shape with one entry left, and the entry has turned around. The old entries named victims to be spared. The last one names the claim its holder must not make about himself. What survived the narrowing is the function. A code tells strangers what is off the table, and the modern rogue's code protects the one piece of knowledge his world gets to keep: what kind of man he is. He may take the purse. His account of himself stays good.

Two limits hold all of this inside fiction. Transfer to real conduct is not established, and the sourced reading that enjoys this figure most says so itself: in life the conduct is deplorable, fun to watch and ruinous to be owed money by. A story can arrange a set of worse men holding warrants. A life is under no such obligation, and nothing here is a guide to judging people. The sharpest attack on the mechanism is also still standing. If the refusal of virtue is itself a bid for regard, the whole account collapses into performance.

What is left is the value itself, and it has not moved since the ploughman. A figure with no legal standing, no virtue to claim, and no legitimacy to invoke can still issue one guarantee, that what he says he is, he is. Audiences have priced that guarantee the same way for five centuries. The taking is forgiven. The lie about the taking is not.

## How to practice this

1. Take a rogue you are writing and look at his worst act. Check that the audience sees it and the story never softens it. Notice any secretly-noble reveal you added.
2. Write down what your rogue refuses to do. Check that the refusals show in what he declines on the page. The ballad's code shows in who goes unrobbed.
3. Read every line where he describes himself. He may lie about names, plans and whereabouts. Notice any line where he claims to be better than what he is.
4. List the people around him and what each one does. Notice whether any of them does more harm while claiming legitimacy.
5. Find the scene where refusing to claim virtue costs him something he wants. If there is no such scene, the restraint is only stated. A stated restraint is a line of dialogue.

## Open questions

- If the non-claim of virtue is itself a bid for regard, the mechanism collapses into performance. That is the failure mode [[wiki/Concepts/Anti-Marketing|Anti-Marketing]] names, where a flaw revealed early reads as honesty and starts doing the work of advertising, and the same flaw discovered later reads as concealment. The harder case here is a genuine harm disclosed. The sourced material sharpens the worry rather than settling it: figures of this kind are described as skilled at simulated sincerity, good at appearing sorry, good at performing what they do not have. Nothing on this page can close the question.
- The comparative mechanism has no stated floor. Forgiveness is scored against a field of men doing worse under warrants. What the audience does when the field is clean is unmeasured.
- Honest dishonesty may be a family rather than a single restraint: accurate self-naming, refusal of righteousness, refusal to invoke legitimacy. It already comes apart on the false name.
- Where does the exemption actually sit? [[wiki/Concepts/Charisma|Charisma]] locates it in charm. This page locates it in the non-claim of virtue. The disagreement is testable and has not been run. The prediction is that a charming figure who does claim virtue is not forgiven the same way.
- Whether any of this describes real conduct is not established. The mechanism is demonstrated in stories, where the field of worse men is arranged by an author. No claim on this page reaches people.
