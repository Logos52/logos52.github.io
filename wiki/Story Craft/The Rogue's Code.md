# The Rogue's Code

The rogue's code is the short private rule that an outlaw character keeps while breaking the public ones. Robin Hood, the gentleman thief and Jack Sparrow all steal, lie or cheat, and audiences still side with them because the rule they keep is plain and the powers around them are worse. A writer who gets the code right can make a lawbreaker the character readers trust most.

- The rogue breaks the law openly and says so.
- He keeps one private rule, and the audience sees him keep it.
- He tells the truth about what he is.
- The lawful powers around him are no cleaner.
- Readers forgive him by comparison with those powers.
- He refuses credit for the good he does.

## The oldest version

A Gest of Robyn Hode, printed around 1500 and later catalogued as Child ballad 117, gives Robin's code as a list. His men are to do no harm to the farmer who tills with his plough, to the good yeoman, or to a knight or squire who will be a good fellow. Bishops and archbishops they may beat and bind, and they are to keep the high sheriff of Nottingham in mind. The code names who is protected and who is fair game, so every robbery in the ballad can be checked against it.

- Protected: farmers, good yeomen, decent knights and squires.
- Fair game: rich churchmen and the sheriff.
- The outlaw's list is stricter than the law he breaks.

## How it works

The rogue tells the truth about himself while lying about everything else. When Jack Sparrow is asked his business in Port Royal, he says he means to take a ship, find a crew and plunder, and the guards do not believe him. When Will Turner says "You cheated", Jack answers "Pirate". His stated rule is what a man can do and what a man can't do, and he never claims a higher one.

- Say the crime out loud, because the audience trusts the honesty.
- Show the private rule once, when no one is watching.
- Jack salutes three hanged pirates while his own boat sinks.
- Surround him with worse powers that claim the law.
- In the Pirates films, a trading company and the navy hang men.
- Let him do the right thing late and deny any good motive.

```
lawful powers:  claim the law, break their own rules
rogue:          breaks the law, keeps one stated rule
audience:       compares the two and forgives the rogue
```

## Where it fails

The code stops working when the rogue's harm lands on people the audience cares about and the story does not charge him for it. In Dead Man's Chest (2006) Jack offers Will to the sea captain Davy Jones to pay his own debt. In the next film his crew rescue him, and when he asks whether anyone came because they missed him, nobody had. A clinical reading labels him with antisocial personality disorder and judges him as a real person, and inside the story the better test is how the other characters treat him and what he pays.

- A rogue with no rule at all is a villain.
- A rogue who harms friends for free loses the audience.
- A rogue who takes the credit becomes a plain hero.

## Related types

The rogue sits among several older character types that overlap with him. Eric Hobsbawm's social bandit is an outlaw the poor see as their champion, a thesis historians have contested. Lewis Hyde describes a trickster as a crosser of boundaries, and Paul Mattick describes a trickster's work as disrupting an order and then setting it back up.

- Gentleman thief: polite manners, a code, and stolen goods.
- Picaresque hero: a low-born rogue moving through a corrupt society.
- Trickster: crosses lines, disrupts order, restores it.
