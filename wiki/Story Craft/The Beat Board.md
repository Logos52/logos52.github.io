# The Beat Board

A beat is one change in a story, and a beat board is a wall or table of index cards, one card per beat, used to plan the structure before any scene is written. Television writers' rooms use a board to lay out a whole season, and novelists use the same method alone. Cards are cheap to move, cut and test, and a board shows gaps that a draft hides.

## Takeaways

- One beat per card, in about seven words.
- Neighbouring beats should connect with "but" or "therefore".
- Beats joined by "and then" have no cause between them.
- Each scene card should turn a value, better to worse or back.
- Mark each card with the initials of the characters in it.
- An empty card holds an open question, never filler.
- After drafting, outline the draft again and compare it to the board.

## How it works

The board works because a card is too small for prose. With room for only a short line, a writer has to say what changes in the beat, and the cards can then be moved around and tested as a sequence. In the Breaking Bad writers' room, described in Brett Martin's book Difficult Men, beats went on 3 by 5 inch cards in marker, with a row of cards for each of the season's 13 episodes. Matt Stone and Trey Parker, who write South Park, give the test for the sequence: if "and then" fits between two beats, the story fails there, and each join should be "but" or "therefore".

- Write the beat: who does what, and what changes.
- Place it in order on the board.
- Test each join between neighbouring cards.
  - "But": a complication.
  - "Therefore": a consequence.
  - "And then": no cause, so rework or cut.

```
[baker needs rent] --therefore--> [takes night job]
        |
      --but--> [falls asleep at the oven]
        |
  --therefore--> [loses the day shift]
```

## How to do it

Screenwriting guides give different card counts for the same method. Blake Snyder's Save the Cat uses forty cards in four rows of ten, one row for each quarter of a film. Alexandra Sokoloff suggests forty to sixty cards in four columns. Syd Field puts one scene on each card, then cuts cards before reordering them.

- Card: about seven words, a plain label with no dialogue.
- Board: under fifty cards for a film, on a table or wall.
- Initials on each card show who is present.
- A second colour marks a second storyline.
- Each scene card names the value that turns in it.

Robert McKee's rule that each scene turns a value gives a second test beside "but" and "therefore". Dwight Swain's scene and sequel adds a rhythm: a scene in which a goal meets a setback, then a sequel in which the character reacts and decides on the next goal.

## Keeping the board honest

Two habits keep the board true to the story as it is actually written. A card for a beat the story has not yet earned stays as a question, a placeholder that marks the slot, and nobody fills it with invented content to make the board look complete. After each writing session, a reverse outline of the draft, one short line per scene or paragraph, shows where the draft has drifted from the board. The board compresses the story, and the reverse outline checks that the compressed version is still true.

- Leave empty cards as questions.
- Reverse outline after each session, one line per unit.
- Compare the reverse outline to the board the same day.
- In software, bind each card to the scene it stands for.

## Where it fails

Card sorting from interface research, where users group thirty to fifty cards by topic, looks similar but tests how people group things, and its rules transfer to story order only loosely. A board with no reverse outline slowly stops matching the draft. A board with too many cards turns back into a draft in small pieces.

- Too many cards: the overview is lost.
- Cards full of dialogue: nothing to test.
- A board never checked against the draft.
