# The Beat Board

The value of putting a story on cards is that you can move them, and every rule about the format exists to protect that. One event per card, compressed to a line — and if it will not compress, you are holding two events or a decision you have been avoiding. The order then becomes something you try rather than something you defend. Maintenance decides whether the board stays useful: it describes the story only while it matches what has actually been written, and a board that has quietly drifted will confidently point you at the wrong problem.

## The card

A **beat** is one event-sized unit of the story: something happens that changes a situation. That unit occupies a single physical card, written by hand and squeezed to a **logline** — the few words that still say what occurs. Storage is the second job. Compression is the first. A pass that forces already-written beats down to card length should alter some of them. If every beat survives the squeeze at seven words with nothing changed, the board is only filing cabinet.

The rules exist so the cards can still be rearranged. That mobility is what a prose outline cannot do. The working ceiling is about seven words — a ceiling, not a statute, and it arrives through one feature-card practice as it circulates, not through a signed pamphlet. Labels stay generic. "Battle in swamp" can move. "Final showdown" has already nailed itself to a slot. A changed beat gets a new card. The old one is not patched. Software makes patching cheap, which is exactly what the rule is there to stop. On a laptop the move is: write a fresh card, throw the previous one away.

Under that same feature practice the whole story fits in fewer than about fifty cards. A second published sheet is forty on the nose. A novel port sits between forty and sixty. Those numbers belong to the practices that published them.

One hour-drama room used a three-by-five-foot corkboard, three-by-five index cards, and Sharpies, and would not switch to a whiteboard. The person running that room treated the squeeze-to-card as the thing that produced the breakthroughs. Theory, attributed. Not a trial.

One circulating practice spreads the cards flat on a table instead of pinning them. The stated reason is that taking a pin out and putting it back makes people leave the order alone. Plausible. Nobody timed it. Furniture, not a law.

**The card compresses first and stores second.**

## The board

Cards that small put the whole story in one look. That is why they are that small. A board can also run two tiers at once: a season row along the top, episode beats hanging under it. One season board ran thirteen cards across the top because that season had thirteen episodes. The last episode's slot held a single card that said "BOOM." A **placeholder card** is a named-but-unbuilt beat holding a slot. Holding a slot without having built the beat is a legal board object. [[wiki/Story Craft/Seeding and Payoff|Seeding and Payoff]] is the same discipline run the other way: a named-but-unbuilt beat reserves a place the story has not yet earned.

One feature sheet is forty cards in four rows of ten — Act 1, 2A, 2B, 3 — and those forty *contain* that sheet's fifteen named beats. They are not fifteen cards. Anatomy on that sheet: a location header, a one-line description, a plus-or-minus pair, angle-brackets for conflict. A novel port of the same idea runs forty to sixty scene cards in four columns, the surface split into eight sections. An older practice puts one scene on each three-by-five and publishes no total. That older count reaches this page secondhand; the flag is in Sources.

Initials in a corner let a glance show who has vanished from a stretch of episode. [[wiki/Story Craft/Spotlight Rotation|Spotlight Rotation]] is that scan made physical: a rotation failure is an initial missing from a run of cards. A second card color, reserved for one category — one practice reserves it for action — makes that category's distribution visible without reading.

## A check, not a generator

Every method named on this page assumes the cards already have events on them. Blank cards and a marker do not invent a story. Invention happens somewhere else — a sentence, a walk, a page of draft. The board is the check. Compression tests events. It does not supply them.

## Seams, turns, cull

One practice maps three acts across a wall, then fills the beats. The joint between neighbours has to read **but** or **therefore**. If "and then" is the honest connective, the sequence has failed. "But, because, therefore — that gives you the causation between each beat, and that's a story." [[wiki/Story Craft/Premise and Controlling Idea|Premise and Controlling Idea]] is that claim run at seam scale.

Writing the connective onto the board turns the joint into an object that can pass or fail. The seminar that gave the test does not call it a board object. That operationalization is this page's. A beat that will only take "and then" is cut, or rebuilt until it is a consequence of the one before it. Doing this at every joint should expose the places prose had sanded into mere succession. That is the checkable yield. When neighbouring cards belong to different plotlines, the legal joint may sit two cards down. Causation is tested along each line, not only along the wall.

A scene flips a value from one polarity to the other at a nameable **turning point**, or it is not a scene. The **value turn** is that flip: good-for-this becoming bad-for-this, or the reverse, at one discrete moment. One spreadsheet writes the rule as columns — value in play, polarity at open, polarity at close, the exact moment of the flip. No turning point, no value shift. No value shift, not a scene. The plus-or-minus pair on a feature card is the same test at card scale. One one-page variant — the **Foolscap** — plots each scene's one-line event above or below an axis by polarity, so the board reads as a zigzag.

Then the **cull**: take a card off and read the sequence without it. If the story still stands, the card goes. After that, the survivors are rearranged until each one reads as the consequence of the one before it. Necessity first, order second. A graceful layout of cards the story did not need is still a layout of cards the story did not need.

User-research card-sorting uses thirty to fifty cards, one item each, never the same wording on two cards. Matching labels make people sort by matching the words instead of the relationship underneath. An open sort lets the sorter invent and name the groups, and that is how a mental model is found. A closed sort files cards into categories already chosen, and that is how a structure is checked. Loose scene cards sorted open *may* surface the story's own shape. That is a plausible transfer, not a finding from the UX literature. UX sorting is about membership, not sequence. Dealing the same cards into a pre-printed four-by-ten grid is a closed sort in the same analogy. Useful. Not a finding.

## Handoff, growth, sync

**Scenes** run Goal → Conflict → Disaster. **Sequels** run Reaction → Dilemma → Decision. A **Motivation-Reaction Unit** is the still-smaller pair inside both: something happens, the character reacts. The Decision of one Sequel is the Goal of the next Scene. That handoff is the load-bearing joint. Each unit forwards a named object the next unit has to receive. A card that writes its Decision has already said what the following card must take. Two popularizers teach the pair. Their names live in Sources.

**Snowflake** is an optional ladder: each unit at one size becomes one unit of the next size down. A logline card is the squeezed form of the paragraph it will grow into. The ladder itself does not begin as cards. Joining it to the board is this page's move. That author's own clock, not a field average: an hour for a one-sentence summary; another hour to grow it into a five-sentence paragraph; about an hour per character for a one-page summary; several hours to explode each of those sentences into its own paragraph, which is the one-page synopsis; a day or two for character synopses; a week to grow each of those paragraphs into a full page, which is the four-page synopsis; a week for character bibles; up to a week for a scene-list spreadsheet; optional one- or two-page scene notes; then the draft. The middle rungs each take a week, so the ladder spends weeks of structure work before a draft line exists. One author's numbers. Not a norm. What the price buys is that no level is invented from nothing.

A two-tier season board has no published card cap. The fifty-card feature ceiling does not travel onto a season row. The seven-word rule is feature-side. Whether it holds on a season row has not been checked.

Drafting walks the manuscript a little farther from the board every session. That drift is why the second discipline exists. Sync is one of two mechanics: bind each card to the document it stands for, or rebuild a list of what was actually written and compare it to the plan. The class is any card bound to its document, or a reverse outline on paper. One software corkboard binds each card to the real document, so dragging a card reorders the binder. Order then syncs by the drag. Content still does not. The synopsis field is the living mini-outline and has to be rewritten as the scene changes.

A **reverse outline**, after a session, states each new paragraph or scene's actual function in about twelve to fifteen words, lists those notes in the order they now stand, and compares the list to the plan. Twelve to fifteen is this page's working count for "a sentence or so." It is not a published figure from the writing-center handout. If the comparison shows a split and the cards are not brought across, the board no longer describes the manuscript. Skip the sync and the board keeps describing the pre-draft intention; every diagnostic run on it is then reading a story that is gone. One spreadsheet practice updates after every session so the sheet describes the pages, not the intention.

Up front: forty-odd cards written by hand to logline standard, a table or corkboard, a rewrite instead of a patch every time a beat moves. Recurring: a twelve-to-fifteen-word line for each scene the session produced, every session, for the life of the draft. That is tomorrow if the board is kept.

The board is the wrong instrument for some writers. It locks structure before drafting and re-locks it after every session. A writer who finds the story by drafting gets the relationship inverted: the plan freezes before the discovery happens. For that writer the reverse outline is the piece worth keeping. The full board is a commitment made too early.

## What a true board is for

Seven movable words are cheap enough to rewrite. A card cheap enough to rewrite is cheap enough to keep true. Only a true board pays the tests back. Stop paying the recurring charge and the furniture remains. The diagnostics do not.
