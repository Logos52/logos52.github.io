# Stakes Without Mortality

Stakes without mortality are the ways a story stays tense when no one is at risk of dying: the risk is to a friendship, a trust, a skill or a sense of self. These stakes matter in stories with a loved cast, in gentle stories, and in long series, where killing a character is ruled out or would end the series. The tension comes from what the characters value, so the work starts with showing that value.

## Takeaways

- Tension is the gap between what a character expects and what happens.
- Show the value first, then put it at risk.
- A threat to something unvalued carries no weight.
- Moral stakes ask who gets hurt by a character's choice.
- A bond that can be lost must stay lost unless repaired.
- Escalate by deepening what is valued, instead of adding danger.

## How it works

Robert McKee describes a story's tension as the gap between the result a character expects and the result the world returns. A death is one very large gap, and a smaller loss works the same way as long as the audience cares about what is lost. The order matters: the story first shows a friendship, a craft or a promise working, and then lets it slip. The audience feels the loss because it has already watched the thing work.

- Plant: show the valued thing working.
- Gap: the character acts and gets a worse result than expected.
- Cost: something valued is damaged or lost.
- Repair: it takes effort and time, on the page.

```
value shown  ->  character acts  ->  result differs
   (friend)       (keeps secret)      (friend hurt)
                                           |
                     repair, or lasting cost
```

## Kinds of stakes

John Truby separates a character's psychological need, a flaw that hurts the character, from a moral need, a flaw in how the character treats other people. Moral stakes use the second kind: a choice that protects one person harms another, or an act of care hurts someone without meaning to. Games with a party of companions give a second model. In Dragon Age and Baldur's Gate 3 each companion's approval rises and falls with the player's choices, and a companion can walk out, so a relationship is at risk in every conversation.

- Relational: a trust, a friendship, a place in a group.
- Moral: who is harmed when a character acts.
- Identity: a skill or role the character has built a self around.
- Small wins count, because the audience knows what they cost.

## An example

The film Kiki's Delivery Service has no villain, and no one is near death until the last scenes. Kiki's setbacks are bad weather, a busy city, and a girl who is rude about a pie, and each has an ordinary reason behind it. The main loss is inside her: she stops being able to fly, and tells the baker who took her in that losing her magic means losing everything. The audience has watched her fly for most of the film, so the loss matters to them.

## Other shapes

The Chinese four-part form 起承轉合 builds a piece in four moves: set up, develop, turn, and bring together. The Japanese form kishōtenketsu is a reading of the same pattern. The turn creates interest through a change of angle, with no fight needed, which suits a story whose stakes are quiet.

- 起: introduce.
- 承: develop.
- 轉: turn to something new.
- 合: bring the parts together.
