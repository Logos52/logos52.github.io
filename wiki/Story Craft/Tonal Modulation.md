# Tonal Modulation

Tonal modulation is the planned alternation of heavy and light moments in a story, so that grief, fear and laughter each get room. A light moment after a heavy one lets the audience rest and makes the next heavy beat land harder. A joke that arrives too early cuts off a feeling the audience was still having.

## Core takeaways

- Comedy goes after the emotional peak.
- A light beat rests the audience before the next heavy beat.
- Mixing tones is old: tragicomedy was defended in 1601.
- Deciding tone and deciding who leads a scene are one plan.
- A small vocabulary pushes comedy onto situation and repetition.
- A relaxed listener takes in more of a new language.

## How it works

In Macbeth, the murder of King Duncan is followed straight away by a drunk porter joking at the castle gate about being the gatekeeper of hell. The joke comes after the murder scene has finished its work, so the audience has already felt the horror, and the knocking at the gate, which he keeps answering, keeps the danger present under the laughter. Battista Guarini, defending the mixed form in 1601, described tragicomedy as taking tragedy's gravity and comedy's laughter while holding back the tragic ending. Shakespeare and Guarini give a writer the same rule about timing: the light beat follows the peak of a heavy one.

- Let the heavy beat finish before any joke.
- Keep a thread of the danger inside the light scene.
- Follow a big scene with a smaller, quieter one.
- Alternate large set-piece episodes with contained, low-cost ones.
- Small wins can carry as much feeling as big ones.

```
weight
  ^        crest
  |        /\          next crest, higher
  |       /  \   joke   /\
  |      /    \  ____  /  \
  |_____/      \/    \/    \____
                    time -->
```

## Planning it across a story

Tone is scheduled across a whole story along with who carries each scene. Low points and the beats that set up stakes are the fixed heavy moments, and the light moments are placed around them. Rotating which character leads a scene and varying the tone of scenes are the same scheduling problem, because a character tends to bring their own tone with them.

- Mark the heavy beats first.
- Place a light scene after each one.
- Check that no two heavy beats sit side by side unbroken.
- Give comic scenes to characters whose voice suits them.

## For stories that teach a language

Stephen Krashen's affective-filter hypothesis holds that anxious learners take in less of a new language, and his later work adds that compelling stories help input stick, though both remain hypotheses. Teachers such as Beniko Mason use a low-anxiety rule in story-listening classes. A story for learners with few words cannot rely on wordplay, so its humour comes from situations, repeated patterns and timing.

- Keep the overall mood safe enough for a relaxed listener.
- Use running gags and repeated situations for humour.
- Keep sad beats short and follow each with a lighter scene.
