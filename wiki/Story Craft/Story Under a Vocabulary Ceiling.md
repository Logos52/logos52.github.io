# Story Under a Vocabulary Ceiling

A story under a vocabulary ceiling is fiction written for language learners, where every sentence uses only words the reader already knows. A reader at the start has no words for feelings or ideas. The parts of a plot, a want, an obstacle, a reversal and a change, are events, and simple words can show an event.

## Core takeaways

- Readers need about 98% of words known to follow a story alone.
- A full story arc can run on action words only.
- Show feeling through a choice, an object, or a thing left undone.
- Put the character's want early and let the deeper need wait.
- Seed late payoffs in the words the reader has at the start.
- Every new place or character costs new words.
- Keep known-word coverage highest at a scene's peak.

## How it works

Reading research puts a number on how many of the words a reader must already know. Hu and Nation tested 66 university students in 2000 on fiction where 80, 90, 95 or 100 percent of the words were known, and found that following the story alone needs about 98 percent known. Batia Laufer's earlier work put a bare minimum at about 95 percent. Below that, the reader spends effort working out words and loses the story, so a writer holds most of each page inside the known words and adds new ones slowly.

- 98% known: the reader follows the story alone.
- 95% known: a bare minimum, with effort.
- Under 95%: working out words replaces reading.
- The scene's peak needs the highest coverage.

Language input the learner can understand, called comprehensible input, is necessary for learning, and on its own it is not enough. Nguyen and Doan made that point in 2025, and they do not argue that input should stop being understandable.

## Story at low vocabulary

The ceiling changes how a story shows the inside of a character. T. S. Eliot's objective correlative, a chosen object that stands for a feeling, becomes the main tool: a letter kept unopened, a gift given back, a seat left empty. As the reader learns more words across a series, the story can say more about what the characters feel, so the reader's vocabulary sets the pace at which secrets come out. The word the reader is about to learn and the secret the story is about to tell can arrive in the same scene.

```
book 1: action words   -> want, deeds, objects
book 2: more words     -> reasons start to show
book 3: feeling words  -> need named, secret told
```

- Want: stated early, in action.
- Need: shown in objects, named once the words exist.
- Premise: one "X leads to Y" line holds the series together.
- Dan Harmon's story circle, a loop of steps, fits scene and season.

## What it costs

Every new element brings words the reader does not have yet. A new place needs its nouns, and a new character needs a name and a way of speaking. A scene set in one place with two people, like a television bottle episode, gives the reader a rest.

- Add places and characters slowly.
- Reuse known places for new events.
- Plant a late payoff in early words, or it cannot be planted.
- Omission works only when the writer knows what is left out.
