# Character Voice

Character voice is the set of habits in a character's speech that lets a reader tell who is talking without a name attached. When every character talks the same way, the reader has to track the speaker tags, and the dialogue stops showing who these people are.

## Takeaways

- Cover the names: a reader should still know who is speaking.
- Voice comes from word choice, filler words, sentence length and grammar.
- Each line carries what is said and what is held back.
- A character's usual way of talking shows the old hurt underneath.
- A break in that usual pattern tells the reader something changed.
- With few words to work with, voice lives in structure and silence.

## What makes a voice

Sol Stein gives a checklist for telling one speaker from another. Each item on it is something a writer can set on purpose, line by line. A voice built from a few of the items, used every time the character speaks, is enough for a reader to learn it. E. M. Forster noted that a minor character can be held together by one memorable note repeated, and a voice is often that note.

- Vocabulary: which words this person knows and prefers.
- Throwaway words: the fillers and tics they use.
- Phrasing: tight and clipped, or loose and running on.
- Grammar: formal, broken, regional or careful.

A script like Chinese has no spelling to bend, so an accent cannot be shown by misspelling words. The voice then has to come from the other items.

## Said, unsaid, unsayable

Robert McKee splits every line of dialogue into three layers, and a writer can check a line against each of them. The words out loud are the top layer. Under them sits what the character knows and is keeping back, and under that sits what drives the character without the character knowing it. A strong voice keeps the two lower layers pressing on the words that get spoken.

- The said: the words spoken.
- The unsaid: what the character chooses not to say.
- The unsayable: what the character cannot see in themselves.

## Voice and the wound

This desk's working rule ties a character's usual way of speaking to their inner wound, the old hurt that shapes what they believe. A person who was let down might speak in short orders and never ask for anything. That usual way is held fixed on purpose, so that the moment it breaks carries meaning. When the guarded character asks for help for the first time, the change in the shape of the line tells the reader more than any explanation would.

- Pick a default way of speaking from the wound.
- Keep it steady across scenes.
- Break it once, at the moment that matters.

## When words are scarce

In a story written for readers who know few words, word choice and grammar leave little room for difference between speakers. Voice then comes from how much a character says, how long they wait before answering, and what they leave out. When even a sentence has no room for a mark of its own, voice moves into actions and objects.

- Length: one character speaks in fragments, another in full sentences.
- Silence: who does not answer, and when.
- Order: who speaks first in a group.
