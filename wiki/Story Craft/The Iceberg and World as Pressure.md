# The Iceberg and World as Pressure

The iceberg is Ernest Hemingway's rule that a writer can leave out what they know and the reader will still feel it. World as pressure is the companion rule for setting: a story world shows itself through what it stops the characters from doing. Together they let a writer build a large world and put only a small part of it on the page, which keeps scenes short and makes the parts shown carry weight.

## Takeaways

- Leave out only what you know in full.
- About one-eighth of the world belongs on the page.
- A world's limits matter more than its powers.
- A rule can solve a problem only if the reader knows it.
- Expand what the world already has before adding something new.
- One to three concrete details can carry a whole place.

## The iceberg

In Death in the Afternoon (1932), Hemingway compared prose to an iceberg, which moves with dignity because only one-eighth of it shows above the water. A writer who knows something and leaves it out gives the reader a sense of the missing part. A writer who leaves something out because they never knew it leaves hollow places the reader can feel. His story Hills Like White Elephants is the standard example: a couple argue about an operation that is never named.

- Know the backstory, the rules and the history in full.
- Put on the page only what the scene needs.
- The reader senses the rest through consistent small choices.
- Gaps left from ignorance read as holes.

```
  on the page     one-eighth: what the scene needs
  ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
  below           history, rules, costs, backstory
                  known to the writer, felt by reader
```

## World as pressure

Brandon Sanderson's laws of magic apply to any story world with rules, magic or not. His Second Law says limits are more interesting than powers, and he names three kinds: limits on what can be done, weaknesses an enemy can use, and costs paid for each use. Superman's weakness to kryptonite and his moral code make him more interesting than his strength does. A world presses on its characters through these limits, so the setting decides what the characters can try.

- First Law: a rule resolves a problem only if readers understand it.
- Second Law: limits, weaknesses and costs matter more than powers.
- Third Law: expand what exists before adding anything new.
- Zeroth Law: when in doubt, choose what is awesome.
- A known rule paid off later is plant and payoff.

## Concrete detail

A few exact details do more than a long description. Anton Chekhov's letter of 10 May 1886 to his brother Alexander told him to seize on a few concrete details, and the famous line about light on broken glass is a later paraphrase. Ursula K. Le Guin gave each place in her Earthsea novels one to three details about work or trade, such as the sheep of Gont and the silk of Lorbanery. Robert McKee adds that a scene works through the gap between what a character expects and what happens, and a world with firm limits is what opens that gap.

- Pick details that show how people in a place make a living.
- A cost the reader already knows makes a later scene hit harder.
- A small vocabulary makes the iceberg rule stricter.
