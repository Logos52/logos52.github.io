# Foils and Pairings

A foil is a character placed beside another so that the difference between them makes each one easier to see. The word comes from jewellery, where a thin leaf of metal set behind a gem makes the stone look brighter. Pairing characters this way lets a writer show a trait by contrast instead of explaining it.

## Core takeaways

- A foil shows a trait by standing next to its opposite.
- The strongest pairs share a situation and make different choices.
- The pair is the smallest unit of a cast.
- A strange character needs someone normal to react to them.
- The contrast can be staged with objects when words are scarce.
- Both characters in a pair need scenes between the big moments.

## How a pair works

The contrast is clearest when the two characters start from the same place. In Hamlet, both Hamlet and Laertes lose a father, and both swear revenge. Hamlet delays and Laertes strikes at once, so each one's choice makes the other's easier to see. If the two had faced different problems, the audience would have had nothing to compare.

```
same loss:      father killed
same vow:       revenge
different act:  Hamlet waits  |  Laertes acts
result:         each choice is easier to read
```

- Give both characters the same pressure.
- Let them answer it in opposite ways.
- Show the shared start before they go different ways.
- Naming the difference late, or never, can be the payoff.

Shakespeare used the jeweller's image himself. In 1 Henry IV, Prince Hal says his reform will shine like bright metal on a dull ground, and look better for having a foil to set it off.

## The straight man

A foil does not have to be a rival. In Pirates of the Caribbean, from 2003, Will Turner and Elizabeth Swann meet Jack Sparrow with confusion and anger. Without their reactions, his strangeness would read as the normal behaviour of that world. Will also carries the film's change, while Jack wants the same ship at the start and at the end.

- The foil reacts, so the audience knows what is unusual.
- The character who changes can stand beside one who does not.

## Pairs across a series

The Captain America films pair Steve Rogers with Tony Stark across several films. Steve keeps his values while the world changes around him, and Tony keeps remaking himself to meet the future. When the two finally fight in Civil War, neither is a villain, and the audience can take either side. A pair built over that much time can carry a whole story's central disagreement.

- One perseveres and one reinvents.
- Neither side is written as wrong.
- A whole cast can be read as several such pairs linked together.
