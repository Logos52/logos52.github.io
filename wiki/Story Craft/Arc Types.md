# Arc Types

A character arc is the change a main character goes through between the first page and the last, measured by what the character believes. Naming the arc type before writing settles what the ending has to prove. It also stops a writer from giving a good character a fake flaw because the textbooks expect one.

- Most arcs turn on a false belief, the Lie, and a Truth.
- Positive change: the character gives up the Lie and takes the Truth.
- Flat arc: the character holds the Truth and the world changes.
- Three negative arcs end with the character worse off or disillusioned.
- All five types share one skeleton of plot points.
- A flat arc needs doubt about the cost of holding the Truth.

## The five types

K. M. Weiland sorts arcs by what the character believes at the start and what the character believes at the end. The start belief is called the Lie, a false idea about the self or the world that has worked well enough so far. The Truth is the idea that would serve the character better. The plot keeps putting pressure on the Lie, and each type is a different answer to what that pressure does.

| Type | Start | End |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Positive change | believes a Lie | takes the Truth |
| Flat | holds the Truth | the world changes |
| Disillusionment | believes a comforting Lie | accepts a hard Truth |
| Fall | believes a Lie | rejects the Truth for a worse Lie |
| Corruption | sees the Truth | rejects it for a Lie |

- Iron Man is positive change: Tony Stark learns he needs others.
- Anakin Skywalker is negative change: he keeps his Lie and gets worse.
- Disillusionment is the lightest negative arc.
- It ends before the character builds a new identity.
- Training Day ends in disillusionment.

## The shared skeleton

The same plot points appear in every type, and the arc type decides what each one has to show. Take the midpoint, the plot point halfway through the story. In a positive arc it is where the character first sees the Truth. In a flat arc it is where the character proves the Truth works. A writer who knows the type knows what each point has to do. Other systems put their own names on the same points, among them the screenwriting book Save the Cat.

```
hook -> inciting event -> first plot point
     -> midpoint -> third plot point -> climax

positive: sees Truth at midpoint, rejects Lie at third
flat:     proves Truth at midpoint, Lie seems to win at third
```

- Save the Cat puts All Is Lost just before 75 percent.
- Its Dark Night of the Soul is the low mood after that.
- Aristotle wanted reversal and recognition in one stroke.
- His word hamartia means a misjudgement in the modern reading.
- John Yorke treats comedy and tragedy as one five-act shape.

## The flat arc

The flat arc is the hardest type to write, because the main character does not learn anything. The 2014 film Captain America: The Winter Soldier is the usual example. Steve Rogers is already good when the story starts, and the filmmakers gave him no flaw, did not hide him behind spectacle, and did not force him into a compromise. They made a political thriller in the 1970s style instead, where his own agency hunts him, so the world pushes hard to make him give up what he believes.

- He doubts whether holding the Truth is worth its cost.
- He never doubts the Truth itself.
- At the climax he drops his shield rather than kill his friend.
- Natasha Romanoff changes: she publishes every file, her own included.
- Sam Wilson joins him without weighing the risk.
- The people around a flat character are the ones who change.

Some critics still call Steve Rogers boring or naive. The film answers by raising what holding the Truth costs him.

## Other ways to sort arcs

Weiland's five names are one system among several. September C. Fawkes uses two questions: does the character change or hold steady, and does the story end well or badly for them. E. M. Forster's older split between flat and round characters measures how deep a character goes, which is separate from whether the character changes. This desk uses the five names on an ensemble story where nobody dies and the vocabulary is kept small, and that use is a choice of this setup rather than something any film showed.

- Fawkes: change or steadfast, crossed with positive or negative.
- Forster: flat and round describe how deep a character goes.
- Vogler names the lowest point Ordeal, Campbell the Belly of the Whale.
