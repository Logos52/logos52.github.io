# Arc Types

Arc type is decided at one moment. That moment is the crux: the point where a character's old belief about themselves and the thing they have come to see cannot both be kept, and the character has to act. What the character does there is what names the arc.

The five shapes in use are not separate plots. They run on the same underlying parts, the same false belief and the same accumulating pressure. They differ only in the last choice at the crux.

That changes how you plan. The arc is not chosen at outline stage and decorated afterward. Everything before the crux exists to make the crux cost something.

## Core takeaways

- Five types: positive change, flat, disillusionment, fall, corruption. The character's choice at the crux is what sorts them.
- Six parts are shared by all five: the Lie, the Ghost, the Want, the Need, the Truth, and the Normal World.
- The five names work as a check run on a draft, not as a plan picked in week one. Typing an arc before the Lie is known produces schematic figures.
- A flat arc means the character already holds the Truth and turns it on the world. The doubt is about the cost of holding the Truth, never about the Truth.
- A fall or a corruption reads as tragic only when the Truth is offered repeatedly and refused where the reader can see it.
- Under a vocabulary ceiling, arc types get rationed across the cast: the flat arc anchors the early books, negative arcs go to secondary characters whose decline shows externally.

## The six parts every arc shares

The skeleton is six pieces.

The Lie is the false belief about self or world that governs the character at the start. It is shown as behaviour and never spoken as a creed. A Lie can be *carry everyone alone, take no hand*, shown as refused help, not as a sentence the character would sign. [[wiki/Story Craft/The Wound and the Lie|The Wound and the Lie]] is the paired unit every type inherits and then diverges from at the crux.

The Ghost is the backstory injury that installed the Lie. It is also called the Wound. Render it as a concrete object introduced early and explained late: cracked hands, a kept letter, a scar the reader meets before anyone accounts for it. [[wiki/Story Craft/Interiority Through Action and Object|Interiority Through Action and Object]] is the concrete carrier when Lie and Truth are only stateable by a narrator.

The Want is the external, nameable goal the Lie produces. A monthly envelope, a seat, a letter: objects a beginner-tier vocabulary can hold in one hand. That one-hand rule is a house constraint, not a craft law.

The Need is the internal truth the Want stands in for. A Need can be *let herself be carried*; another can be *presence, not provision*. In a positive change arc the Need shows up late and usually spends the Want.

The Truth is the worldview the story is actually arguing. Characters on a change arc walk toward it. Characters on a flat arc already stand on it. Characters on a negative arc lose it or turn it down. A presence-love Truth can be one a character mostly holds early. That is a flat arc, not a Lie the same character must overthrow. The Lie on a flat arc belongs to the *world*.

The Normal World is the opening state governed by the Lie.

## The five types and where each one ends

Type is the row the character's last choice lands on. Someone scanning a draft can type it from this view.

| Type | At the crux | End-state |
|---|---|---|
| Positive change | Rejects the Lie, takes the Truth, usually loses the Want | Whole, changed |
| Flat | Already holds the Truth; turns it on the world | World and cast changed, self steady |
| Disillusionment | Reaches the Truth; the Truth is bleak | Awake, still standing |
| Fall | Refuses the Truth; the Lie destroys them | Worse, undone |
| Corruption | Starts on the Truth; abandons it for the Lie | Morally worse |

Positive change is the 1989 delivery-service film, and [[wiki/Story Craft/The Change Arc|The Change Arc]] walks that spine beat by beat. Flat is the 2011–2016 soldier-superhero trilogy: the world changes, a cast converts by watching, and doubt is the test. Disillusionment is the only negative that ends with the character still *consciously holding* the Truth. A companion recut moved the old picture off this row; the type stays, the exemplar does not. Fall carries the load-bearing phrase *could have changed and refused*. No completed fall sits in the project canon, and no invented one stands in for it. Corruption is positive change run backward. Fall resembles Aristotelian tragedy; it is a cousin, not the same thing.

A competing four-type grid, change or steadfast crossed with positive or negative, will meet a reader elsewhere and look like a missing row. The five here are sorted by the choice at the climax. That grid sorts by direction and agency. The two cross. Neither replaces the other. A public essay on the soldier-superhero trilogy uses three types, not five; the three negatives here are three versions of that one negative choice.

## Writing the crux twice

The table is for pairing. Design a positive arc and a negative arc on one skeleton by writing the crux twice: same Lie, same Truth, opposite last move. Comedy and tragedy share one five-act shape. Tragedy is taking the wrong lesson at the crisis, or seeing the right one and not acting. That inversion is the pairing instruction.

No two members of a cast need end the same. That is what the five types are for. A house constraint the five types happen to serve is that nobody has to die for them to be distinct. That constraint is not what sets type, and it is not a craft law.

## What each type looks like on the page

In a positive change arc the Want dramatizes the Lie. Visible cost accumulates: hands worsening, an envelope that never fills the hole. When the Need is taken, the Want that protected the character is given up. The resolution stays *changed, not restored*: the burden eases, it does not vanish. *Small wins are big.* The arc can close with the crisis still partly open, and it earns that close because the reader already watched what it cost.

Isolation as a Lie, the group as a Truth, a first crack from someone already holding that Truth, then acting from it by hosting and transmitting, is the same positive shape with a different payload. One steadfast Truth-holder who converts a cast gives every side character a B-plot that can take its own turn in a later book. In the soldier-superhero trilogy one convert dumps the files and another says he is in. Match the vocabulary ramp to those payoffs by giving each convert their crisis only once the language exists to hold it.

A Want can be escape from an assigned life. A Lie can be *the assigned life can be outrun*. Those are types. They do not dictate a bleak-accepted ending. The conscious hold is what separates disillusionment from the two heavier negatives: the character is awake inside a Truth that hurts, and still standing.

*Could have changed and refused* is what makes a fall tragic instead of merely sad. It requires the Truth offered repeatedly and turned down in view of the reader. A reachable picture is a thane who sees the cost of the next room and walks in anyway, or a son who takes the first hit and does not walk back. A near-fall can supply the descent and an off-ramp without the ending: the Truth being refused is *own the responsibility*, and the off-ramp is taken rather than the fall. A near-fall becomes a true fall when the refused offer is repeated until the refusal has taken everything and the way back is closed on the page. No character in the project canon completes that shut door.

A betrayal beat, confidence weaponized and a group splitting, is a partial corruption with both sides written real. A full sustained corruption arc does not exist in the project canon, and the betrayal character is unnamed. One suggestion, not canon: plant a concrete small-compromise scene early that only re-reads as the first step of the decline once the later betrayal lands. Beginner action-only vocabulary cannot state corruption, so the seed has to be an act. That scene is not written out.

## The low point and catharsis

Pity and fear build to a recognition and a reversal that discharge as catharsis. [[wiki/Story Craft/The Low Point and Catharsis|The Low Point and Catharsis]] is how the low point makes the crux cost something without deciding its type. Peripeteia and anagnorisis landing at one stroke have the strongest effect. The power rides on internal stakes: identity, love, belief, never mortal peril. Catharsis without death is the standing rule. The "whiff of death" is the loss of a relationship, a place, or a long-planted Ghost object. Recognition under a vocabulary ceiling is one character finally naming aloud a Truth planted books earlier.

## Rationing arcs across a vocabulary ceiling

The vocabulary curve is also the reveal curve. Arc type gets rationed across the cast rather than loaded into one book, and [[wiki/Story Craft/Story Under a Vocabulary Ceiling|Story Under a Vocabulary Ceiling]] is why the rationing and the front-loaded cores exist. Anchor the early books on the flat-arc character who already holds the Truth, because steadiness reads through repeated concrete action at beginner vocabulary. Put negative arcs on secondary characters whose decline shows externally, so the payoff can wait until abstract-emotion vocabulary exists. Split Want from Need across the ramp: the Want is a nameable object early, the Need stays unnamed until later. The Moment of Truth is the first scene in which the character can say the Need aloud, and [[wiki/Story Craft/The Change Arc|The Change Arc]] owns that spine. Front-load the cores to the stretch where most readers leave. That is a design choice, not a measured attrition number.

## Where the beats fall

First Plot Point around a quarter, Moment of Truth around half, and low point around three-quarters are scaffolding conventions. They are model-dependent, not laws. One beat-sheet places All Is Lost slightly before the three-quarter mark. All Is Lost is an event. Dark Night of the Soul is a reaction valley. Crisis is a knowledge turning point. Ordeal, Approach to the Inmost Cave, Reward, and Belly of the Whale are separate again. They resemble each other. They do not coincide unless a given draft makes them. One model says Crisis need not sit on the three-quarter low point at all.

## A flat arc is not a flat character

A *flat arc* here is a dimensional character who holds the Truth and changes the world. A *flat character* is built around a single idea, with no interiority and no capacity to change. Those are different axes. Fusing them is the most common error in secondary-arc writing. A round character can run a flat arc. A household cat who is a motif is a genuine no-arc grace note. A diagnostician does not "correct" that cat.

## Hamartia, and where these terms come from

Hamartia is an error or misjudgement in action, discovered late. It is not a labeled fatal flaw announced in advance. The nineteenth-century "tragic flaw" reading is the mis-write. Vocabulary for the five names comes from one craft book. The psychology of weakness-and-Need and the value-swinging gap come from two others. Those two do not independently generate the five names.

## Typing comes after the draft

**The five names are useful as a check run on a draft, not as a plan picked in week one.** The taxonomy is applied after the fact. Typing an arc before the Lie is known produces schematic figures.

## What the method costs

Distributing five types across one cast invites ensemble sprawl. Stagger the spotlight so one character's crisis lands per installment, and [[wiki/Story Craft/Spotlight Rotation|Spotlight Rotation]] is that defense. Without a genuine off-ramp repeatedly refused, a fall or corruption reads as arbitrary and merely sad. A Truth-holder who never doubts and faces no real opposition reads as a passive superhero. The misconception is that a flat arc means no internal conflict. The critical detail is doubt about the *ability* to uphold the Truth, or about whether the *cost* of upholding it is too high.

The steadfast flat-arc anchor costs the most inventive external opposition, because the drama cannot come from the Truth being in question. It can and must come from doubt about the cost of holding it.

Under a vocabulary ceiling, negative-arc interiority cannot be paid for until later. An early fall or corruption must be carried by external object and action, and if the seed is never re-cued it reads as nothing. Typing an arc spends the freedom to discover it. A character who surprises the draft into a different crux forces a rewrite backward. In this project the method also costs front-loading discipline and plant-to-payoff bookkeeping. Each payoff is planted one or two readings earlier, at a lower vocabulary tier, and drawn out by a curious friend: a second character who pulls a confession into a quiet scene, so the later crisis lands on material already planted.

## When to stop and rewrite

Quit when Lie and Truth are only stateable by the narrator. Find the concrete carrier and delete the statement; [[wiki/Story Craft/Interiority Through Action and Object|Interiority Through Action and Object]] is that carrier.

Quit when the low point raises a bigger external threat and the internal loss cannot be named. Cut the threat and threaten identity, love, or belief.

Quit when a negative ending feels sad rather than tragic. Write the offered-and-refused scene.

Quit when a flat-arc protagonist never doubts and meets no resistance. Give the world genuine opposition, or move the arc to a supporting character.

## What to check in a finished draft

A planted Ghost object should discharge its payoff without exposition. On any negative arc, a reader can point to the specific scene where the Truth was offered and refused. A steadfast protagonist should read as consistent through repeated concrete actions a reader can list. If the only answer to what the character stands to lose is their life, internal stakes are missing. The recognition should land within the beat that follows the plant, on the page, not one scene later after being told.

## What the choice at the crux is for

Type is still set at the crux. The setting of it is a check on a draft. A steadfast Truth costs doubt about the price of holding it, never doubt about the Truth. No two members of a cast need end the same. That is what the choice at the crux is for. It is not a promise that no one dies.

## How to practice this

1. Write the six parts for one character before you type the arc: Lie, Ghost, Want, Need, Truth, Normal World. Notice whether the Lie is shown as behaviour and never spoken as a creed.
2. Type a finished draft against the crux table instead of picking a type in week one. Notice whether any character typed before the Lie was known reads as a schematic figure.
3. Write one crux twice: same Lie, same Truth, opposite last move. Notice which version costs the character the Want.
4. On every negative arc, point to the scene where the Truth was offered and refused. If no such scene exists, the ending reads as sad rather than tragic.
5. List the repeated concrete actions that show your steadfast character holding the Truth. Then check that the doubt is about the cost of holding it, not about the Truth.
6. Ask what each character stands to lose. If the only answer is their life, the internal stakes are missing.

## Open questions

Whether disillusionment can stay a named fifth type in a no-death ensemble once the clean exemplar has moved, or whether the lightest negative is always one axis of a growth arc.

How the pairing of a positive and a negative (the crux written twice) is checked when the two cruxes land in different books.

Whether the four-type grid's "negative steadfast" is a missing row or just this page's fall under another name.
