# Setting as Character

Setting as character means writing a place so that it acts on the people in it: it makes demands, blocks plans and shapes how they live. A place written this way gives a story pressure and texture without any extra plot. A place written as scenery stays in the background, and any other place could stand in for it without changing the story.

- One small, exact detail shows a place better than a description.
- Culture comes from how people in a place earn a living.
- Deepen the places you have before adding new ones.
- A place should make demands on the characters or block their plans.
- Leave out only what you know, since readers notice a hollow gap.

## How it works

A place becomes active when it opens a gap between what a character expects and what happens. Robert McKee describes that gap as the source of tension, and a setting can open it as well as a villain can: a harbour that freezes over, a town that distrusts outsiders, a rent that keeps rising. The characters react to the place from the inside, and the reader learns about the place and the people at the same time. Details carry all of this, so each one is chosen for what it tells about the people.

- The place presses on the character.
  - The character adapts, resists, or leaves.
  - Each choice shows who the character is.
- Details are chosen for what they say about people.
- Customs follow from work, weather and money.

Anton Chekhov gave the rule for details in a letter to his brother in 1886: to show a moonlit night, describe the glint of broken bottle glass on a mill dam. The line often quoted as his, "don't tell me the moon is shining", is a later paraphrase of that letter.

## How to do it

Start from what the people in a place do for a living, and let the customs, clothes and speech follow from that. Ursula K. Le Guin built the islands of Earthsea this way, so each island's culture follows its trade, such as the silk dyers of Lorbanery. Brandon Sanderson's third law of magic applies the same way to places: expand what you already have before you add something new. For each place already built, ask what follows from it before inventing another.

- Name the work people do there, and derive customs from it.
- Pick one exact detail per scene that tells something about people.
- Ask "what happens when" of a place already built.
- Link places to each other through trade, travel or old quarrels.
- Reuse a place in a new situation before building a new one.

## Where it fails

Ernest Hemingway held that a writer can leave things out only when he knows them, and that a reader senses the gap when he does not. A setting sketched with no knowledge underneath reads thin, however much is left out. The opposite fault is a setting explained at length that never touches any choice a character makes. In a story for language learners there is a further cost, since each new place brings new words and so takes reading time.

- Hollow omission: the writer never worked out what is left out.
- Scenery: the place could be swapped with no change to the story.
- Too many places: each new one gets no depth.
- Each new place in a learners' story costs new words.
