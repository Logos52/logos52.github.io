# Seeding and Payoff

A seed is a small detail a story shows early, and the payoff is the later scene that uses it. Anton Chekhov set down a rule in a letter in 1889: a loaded rifle shown on stage has to go off, or it should not be on stage. A writer who keeps track of seeds can make a late turn feel earned, and can cut any detail that promises something the story never uses.

## Core takeaways

- An audience expects a use for any detail the story lingers on.
- Cut a detail the story never uses, or give it a use.
- A big payoff needs its seed placed well before it.
- The longer the gap, the more noticeable the seed must be.
- The audience should recognise the payoff as something already seen.
- Show the seed in an object or action, and never explain it.

## How it works

An audience treats anything the story dwells on as a cause whose effect has not arrived yet. The film scholar Kristin Thompson calls this a dangling cause: a setup left open, which the viewer carries forward while watching. When the payoff arrives, the viewer connects it to the earlier detail, so the scene surprises them and still feels fair. The seed has to stay in memory across the whole gap, which is why it is shown vividly or shown more than once.

- Seed: a detail gets attention and has no use yet.
- Gap: the audience holds an open question.
  - Short gap: one clear showing is enough.
  - Long gap: show it again, or make it striking.
- Payoff: the detail is used, and the audience connects it.

```
seed            gap                   payoff
 |------------------------------------->|
 gun on wall    audience remembers       gun is fired
 (shown)        (question stays open)    (connected)
```

The same mechanism is what makes a threat matter. Robert McKee describes tension as the gap between what a character expects and what actually happens. A story that first shows the audience a friendship, a home or a promise gives a later threat to it weight. A threat to something the audience never saw valued does not move them.

## An example

Chekhov's own play The Seagull follows his rule. In the second act the young writer Treplev brings in a gull he has shot and lays it at the feet of the woman he loves. In the last act a shot is heard offstage. The audience already knows Treplev owns a gun and has used it, so nobody has to explain what the shot means.

- Act two: the gun and the dead bird are seen.
- Act four: a shot offstage, and the audience understands it.

## How to do it

Keep a running list of every seed and the scene that pays it off. Any seed with no match on the list is cut or given a payoff. A big turn late in the story gets its seed one or two chapters or episodes earlier, placed while the scene is busy with something else so it does not draw attention. In a story written for language learners, a seed for a late payoff has to use the words the reader already has at the start.

- List each seed beside its payoff.
- Cut or pay off any seed left without a match.
- Plant the seed while the scene is busy with something else.
- Use an object or an action a scene can show again.
- Bring it back once, slightly changed, so it stays in memory.
- If an earlier seed is cut, check the payoff that needed it.

## Where it fails

Two opposite faults are common. A seed given too much attention lets the audience predict the payoff, so the late scene has no surprise left in it. A story that opens many mysteries and never closes them leaves the audience holding questions that go nowhere, a fault often called the mystery box.

- Seed too loud: the payoff is guessed early.
- Seed forgotten across a long gap: the payoff seems sudden.
- Seed with no payoff: the audience waits for nothing.
- Payoff with no seed: the ending looks invented.
