# Seeding and Payoff

A seed is something shown early and deliberately left unused. Leaving it unused is what makes it a promise rather than scenery.

Two conditions decide whether it can be collected later. The reader has to still be holding it, which in practice means a real object they saw more than once rather than a fact mentioned in passing. And it has to act on something they already care about, because a payoff that threatens a value the reader never acquired arrives as an event rather than as a consequence. Most of the work is bookkeeping rather than inspiration: knowing what is planted and what each plant is owed.

## Core takeaways

- A plant is a conspicuous element shown early and left unused, carrying the promise that it will be used later. The payoff is that use.
- A seed survives a gap only if it was presented vividly or redundantly enough to be recoverable. The longer the gap, the more salient or repeated it has to be.
- At the first vocabulary floor a plant cannot be stated. It can only be shown as an object and shown again.
- A payoff works only if the value it threatens was established first. Otherwise the later firing is information.
- The house rhythm is PLANT → SPARK → PAYOFF, with the spark drawn out one or two readings before the arc beat.
- A fixed recurring cast requires an explicit setup-to-payoff table per character, and that ledger is real overhead.

## What a plant is

A plant is a conspicuous element shown early and left unused, carrying a promise it will be used later. Once the reader can see it, the story owes them its use. The payoff is that use.

The rule is older than the slogans built around it. An 1889 letter to A. S. Lazarev, Aleksandr Lazarev-Gruzinsky, the same addressee Motif and Symbol names, says one must never place a loaded rifle on the stage if it is not going to go off. Wording varies by translation. That is the loose attested form. The sharper "first act, then the second or third" phrasing is secondhand: Gurlyand, 11 July 1904, then popularized by Shchukin in 1911. Both reported the line. Neither is a letter. This page is the wing's source of record for the three. Do not fuse them into one quotation.

## How a seed survives a gap

A serial has a name for the same obligation held across an interruption. A dangling cause is information given no immediate effect, suspended until resolution many installments later. It survives only if it was presented vividly or redundantly enough to be recoverable. The longer the gap, the more salient or repeated the seed has to be.

Two carriers do that work, and they are not interchangeable. The runner is a low-scene-count thread: a sentence or two per lesson, paying off across the run. Writers'-room shorthand calls it a C-story. The repeated object is the other carrier, and for a graded reader it is the reliable one. Redundancy is the method here, not sloppiness. [[wiki/Story Craft/Motif and Symbol|Motif and Symbol]] covers that object as a return-with-variation, and the web that keeps one object from standing for two people.

A plant hides when it reads as texture: a prop, a habit, a gag. Only the payoff reveals that it was structural. Reading as texture is what lets it survive without being solved, and it was structural the whole time.

## Why an early plant can only be shown

How much can be revealed is set by how much vocabulary the reader has. An early plant can state only what happened, not why. At the first floor there are characters for objects and actions, and almost none for reasons. The plant cannot be stated. It can only be shown as an object, and shown again. [[wiki/Story Craft/Interiority Through Action and Object|Interiority Through Action and Object]] covers that show-only discipline: the seed has to be a thing that can be re-shown, never a stated theme.

A late payoff can only be seeded in the vocabulary available at the start. [[wiki/Story Craft/Story Under a Vocabulary Ceiling|Story Under a Vocabulary Ceiling]] gives the reason. The first-floor noun is the only noun the later scene is allowed to use.

A payoff lands when it fires on a value the reader already feels. The value has to be established first. If it was not established, the later firing is information, and information is not a payoff.

## The plant, spark, payoff rhythm

The house rhythm is PLANT → SPARK → PAYOFF. The object is shown. A small confession is drawn out by a curious friend one or two readings before the arc beat. The crisis spends it. The one-to-two-readings window is this system's default. It is not a finding and it is not from the 1889 letter. [[wiki/Story Craft/Companion Arcs and Party Banter|Companion Arcs and Party Banter]] covers the curious friend as a draw-out: the spark is often a question in downtime, not a speech at the peak.

The gap between what a character expected and what the world returned converts a plant into felt tension with no body count. [[wiki/Story Craft/Stakes Without Mortality|Stakes Without Mortality]] covers that conversion on the stakes side. The distance between what one character gives and what the other needed is the payoff, because both values were planted separately. Plant what one character gives, and plant what the other needs. The payoff happens where the two meet.

## The ledger and what it costs

A fixed recurring cast remembers objects across books, so an explicit setup-to-payoff table per character is mandatory. Moving a payoff reopens its seeds. The ledger is real overhead. A seed occupies a speaker-cap slot. A plant laid early commits the arc. Those three costs arrive with the benefit.

An unfired gun is a conspicuous plant that never pays. An un-seeded payoff is a resolution with no plant, which reads as a god in the machine. Every big beat is seeded one to two readings before it lands.

## How seeding fails, and what to do

Seeding fails when accumulation is mistaken for structure. Open loops are cheap to lay and expensive to close. Mystery-box bloat is the named form: so many guns that none reads as about to fire. The opposite cost is over-telegraphing. A plant salient enough to survive four books may be solved early. Both poles are live, and neither wins by default.

In a beloved fixed cast the reader presumes safe, a planted threat can arrive inert. The seed only works if the value was made to matter first. The false-stakes mechanism lives on Stakes Without Mortality. The seed-side rule stays here: plant the bond's worth before you threaten the bond.

A self-contained single reading owes no dangling causes. The whole method is for a gap. With no gap, there is no seed.

If a payoff feels random, add a more redundant plant. Never enlarge the payoff. If the reader calls the plant an hour early, thin the salience or bury it in a gag. If a book ends with more open guns than fired ones, stop planting and use a book to fire the ones already laid. If a threatened bond is shrugged at, plant the bond's worth first.

## How to check the work

The checkable tests are recognition tests. At the payoff the reader recognizes the object with no reminder, so the landing is recognition rather than information. On PLANT → SPARK, the reader can trace the value back to an earlier reading. Across a book, guns fired should track guns laid. A widening gap between the two is the early bloat signal.

## What a landing looks like

A kept promise is something the reader already recognizes. There is no reminder. The object walks in and the value it threatens is already the reader's. That is a payoff. New information in the same slot is a different tool, and it will not do this job.

## How to practice this

1. Build a setup-to-payoff table for each recurring character. Notice which plants still have no payoff listed against them.
2. Count the guns laid and the guns fired in your last book. Notice whether the gap between the two counts is widening.
3. Take a payoff that feels random and add a more redundant plant. Do not enlarge the payoff itself. Notice whether the reader now recognizes the object with no reminder.
4. Show your seed as an object, then show that same object again later. Notice whether you stated it as a theme instead of showing it.
5. Plant what the bond is worth before you threaten the bond. Notice whether the threat still reads as inert.
6. Place the spark one or two readings before the arc beat. Notice whether the reader can trace the value back to an earlier reading.
