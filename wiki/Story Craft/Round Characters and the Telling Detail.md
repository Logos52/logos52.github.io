# Round Characters and the Telling Detail

A round character is one who can surprise the reader and still feel true. A telling detail is one small, specific thing a character does or owns that shows who they are faster than a description could. Together they settle how much depth each character in a cast needs and how to show that depth in a few lines.

## Core takeaways

- A round character can surprise the reader convincingly.
- A character who never surprises is flat.
- A surprise that does not convince is a flat character in disguise.
- True character shows in a choice under pressure.
- Higher stakes make a choice reveal more about the character.
- One telling detail can set up a whole character.

## Forster's test

E. M. Forster split characters into flat and round in 1927. A flat character is built around one idea and says or does the same kind of thing each time, which makes them easy to recognise and useful in small roles. A round character can do something the reader did not expect, and the reader accepts it once it has happened. Forster held that only round characters can carry tragedy for any length of time.

```
never surprises          -> flat
surprises, unconvincing  -> flat, pretending
surprises, convincing    -> round
```

- Most of a cast can stay flat.
- Spend roundness on the few who carry the story.
- Give a round character one contradiction.
- Show the contradiction in action before anyone names it.
- Plant the surprise early so it convinces later.

A contradiction convinces when it rests on the character's inner structure: an old wound, a false belief, what they want, and what they actually need.

## Surface and choice

Robert McKee separates characterization from true character. Characterization is the surface anyone could observe: looks, job, habits, speech. True character is what the person chooses when something is at stake, and the higher the stake, the more the choice tells the reader. A reader who still needs a name tag to follow a character's lines has run into a voice problem, and more backstory will not fix it.

- Characterization: what anyone could observe.
- True character: the choice under pressure.
- Raise the stakes before the choice that matters.

## The telling detail

The opening of Pirates of the Caribbean, from 2003, introduces Jack Sparrow in 22 seconds and four shots. The camera frames him like a hero, standing on a mast, then cuts wide to show that his boat is a small dinghy and it is sinking. While it sinks, he stops to salute the bodies of dead pirates, which shows in about four seconds that he is a pirate with a code. Every later scene with him plays a variation on this opening.

- Pick a detail the character chooses, with nobody watching.
- Keep it short and concrete.
- Build later scenes as variations on it.
