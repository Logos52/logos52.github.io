# Structure for the Ear

Structure for the ear is the craft of building a story that is only heard, as in a radio play, an audio drama or a narrative podcast. A listener has no page to look back at and no faces to tell the speakers apart. A writer who plans for that keeps the cast small, marks every change of scene with sound, and varies the scenes so attention does not drop.

## Takeaways

- A character exists for the listener only when speaking or named.
- Keep a half-hour play to six characters or fewer.
- Each line should tell the listener who is speaking.
- A scene can be a single line or a single sound.
- Vary scene length, number of voices, pace and place.
- Use few sound effects, since silence also carries meaning.
- Time the script by reading it aloud against a clock.

## How it works

A listener builds the scene from sound alone, so a character exists for the listener only while that character speaks or another character names them. With more than six characters in a half-hour play, listeners start to confuse them. Audio has no speech tags, so each speaker's lines need a voice the listener can place before anyone says the name.

- Present but silent means absent for the listener.
- Too many voices: listeners lose track of who is who.
- Distinct speech patterns do the work of name tags.

## Keeping attention

Variety holds a listener, and four things can change from scene to scene: the length of a sequence, the number of people speaking, the pace of the dialogue, and where the action happens. A noisy scene with many voices sits well beside a quiet scene of one person thinking aloud, and an indoor scene beside an outdoor one. Silence can carry suspense, worry or calm, and a pause lets listeners take in what they just heard.

```
scene:   market   ->  bedroom  ->  road
voices:  many         one          two
pace:    fast         slow         medium
sound:   loud         quiet        wind
```

- Change the length, the voices, the pace or the place between scenes.
- Sound effects: a door for function, a distant dog for mood.
- Too many effects become tedious.

## Sound as place

Audio producers build a place in layers. An ambient bed, a steady background sound such as traffic or rain, sets the location, and foreground sounds go on top of it. Each voice gets the acoustics of the room it is in: echo for a hard interior, dry sound outdoors. Moving a background sound to the foreground shifts the listener's point of view.

- Lay the background wash first, then the foreground.
- Match each voice's acoustics to its room.
- Bring a background sound forward to shift perspective.

## Openings, transitions and comedy

Narrative podcasts often start with a cold open, a scene before the title that sets a mood, a place, a stake or a question, and then run in three acts. Listeners drop off most in the first minutes, so the opening carries more weight. Transitions between scenes are short: a few seconds of sound or a few words. Audio comedy tends to run as premise, escalation and exit, and a pause can be the joke, as in Jack Benny's radio show.

- Cold open: mood, scene, stakes or a question.
- Transition: a sound of a few seconds, or a few words.
- Comedy: set up, escalate, then leave the scene.
- A BBC African Performance play runs 28 minutes without credits.
- A word count is a poor guide to running time.
