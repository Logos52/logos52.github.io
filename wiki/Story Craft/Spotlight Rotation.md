# Spotlight Rotation

Spotlight rotation is the scheduling of which cast member carries each episode, chapter or scene in a story with several leads. Television writers' rooms do it by weighting each episode's storylines as A, B and C. Rotating on purpose keeps every lead in the audience's memory and stops one character from taking every big moment.

## Takeaways

- The cast list says who, and rotation decides when each one leads.
- A, B and C mark how much weight a storyline gets.
- Each lead needs a dedicated turn of their own.
- A small recurring thread keeps an off-duty character present.
- A bottle episode is one place and few people, and costs little.
- If two characters could swap lines, the spotlight is wasted.

## How it works

An hour-long drama usually runs two or three storylines in each episode. The A story gets the most time and the biggest turn, the B story gets less, and the C story is often a runner, a small thread of a scene or two that comes back across episodes. The letters mark weight, never the order the scenes appear in. Across a season the writers move each lead through A, B and C, so everyone gets turns at the centre and turns in the background.

- A story: the main turn of the episode.
- B story: a second line, often a pair of characters.
- C story, or runner: a short thread repeated over many episodes.
- Bottle episode: one set, a few actors, a slower pace.

```
          ep1   ep2   ep3   ep4
Ana       A     C     B     A
Ben       B     A     C     C
Cho       C     B     A     B
```

The Breaking Bad episode "Fly" is a standard bottle: two characters locked in a lab, chasing a fly. Bottles save a television show money. In a story for language learners they also give the reader a rest, since one place and two people bring few new words.

## How to do it

Start from a chart of the cast that sets what each character stands for, called the character web, and plan who leads which stretch. E. M. Forster's split between round and flat characters matters here: a round character can surprise and can carry a whole episode, while a flat one works best in a runner. Games with party members give a useful model in the loyalty missions of Mass Effect 2, where each companion gets one mission about their own problem. A quiet scene with a character, such as talk at a campfire, plants what a later action scene pays off, so the audience recognises the payoff when it comes.

- Plan the lead for each stretch before writing scenes.
- Give each lead one dedicated turn, like a loyalty mission.
- Keep off-duty leads alive with a runner of one or two lines.
- Put a quiet scene before the big scene that uses it.
- Do not stack sad turns for the same character back to back.
- If two leads need the same recognition, give it to one.

## Where it fails

Most failures show up as absence. A lead missing from a long stretch fades from the audience's mind, and a later payoff for that lead lands with no build-up. On a beat board, a wall of cards with one scene per card and character initials on each, the gap shows as a run of cards with no mark for that character.

- One lead takes every A story.
- A lead disappears for a long stretch.
- Two leads could swap lines with no change.
- A late payoff for a character who had no runner.
