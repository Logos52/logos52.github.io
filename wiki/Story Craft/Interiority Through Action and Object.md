# Interiority Through Action and Object

Interiority is what a character thinks and feels inside. The technique here is to show it through what the character does and what they handle, instead of through words that name the feeling. It matters most when the writer cannot use many words, as in a story for people learning the language, and it makes any scene stronger, because readers trust what they see a character do.

## Core takeaways

- Show a feeling through an action or an object.
- Readers fill in what a clear action leaves out.
- Leave things out only if you know what they are.
- Use an object again later to show how the character changed.
- A character's choice with no one watching reveals the most.
- Learners need about 98 percent of words known to read for pleasure.

## How it works

T. S. Eliot's rule for expressing an emotion in art is to find a set of objects, a situation or a chain of events that will produce that emotion in the reader. Ernest Hemingway compared a story to an iceberg with one eighth above the water: most of the meaning can stay under the surface, as long as the writer knows what is down there. Readers complete what is left out, the way a comics reader fills in the action between two panels. The result is stronger than a stated feeling, because the reader did part of the work.

```
stated:  "She was afraid he would leave."
shown:   She puts his coat back on the hook.
         reader fills in: fear, wanting him to stay
```

- Pick one object tied to the feeling.
- Let the character handle it at a moment of pressure.
- Do not name the feeling in the same scene.
- Show the gap between what the character expected and what happened.

In Hemingway's story Hills Like White Elephants, a couple argues about an operation that is never named, and the reader understands it from what the two say around it.

## Action with no audience

What a character does when nobody is watching tells the reader who they are. In the first two minutes of Pirates of the Caribbean, from 2003, Jack Sparrow's boat is sinking, and he stops to salute the bodies of hanged pirates. That shows he has a code, without a line of dialogue. He then steps onto the dock at the last second as the boat goes under, which shows how he handles failure better than any backstory could.

- Choose an action that costs the character something or gains them nothing.
- Keep it short.
- A few seconds on screen is enough.

## Objects across a story

An object works best when it comes back. A writer plants it in a quiet scene, shows it again at a turn, and uses it at the crisis. The low point of the story is where such an object can break. A theme stated in words cannot be shown again this way, so the thing planted has to be a behaviour or an object.

- Plant the object in downtime.
- Bring it back when the character changes.
- Let the crisis spend or break it.
- An object can carry a belief the character has no word for.

## Why vocabulary matters

Research on reading in a second language measures coverage, the share of words on a page that the reader already knows. Comprehension rises steadily as coverage rises, and the main study, by Hu and Nation in 2000, tested 66 readers. A story for learners therefore has few words for feelings, and interiority has to move into actions and objects.

- Hu and Nation: about 98 percent for reading without help.
- Laufer: about 95 percent as a floor for adequate comprehension.
- Schmitt, Jiang and Grabe, 2011: no sudden cliff, a steady rise.
- Kremmel and colleagues, 2023, repeated the study and found the same.
