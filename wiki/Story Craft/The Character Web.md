# The Character Web

A character web is a cast designed together instead of one character at a time. One question sits underneath all of them, and each person is built as a different answer to that question. The answers oppose each other, so the meaning of the story comes from the collisions between them rather than from any one person's speech or any one person's plot.

The answers are visible long before the question is ever stated. An umbrella packed for someone who did not ask for it and an envelope sent home every month are two positions on the same matter. A reader who watches both will hold the question without it being named.

## Core takeaways

- A character web is a cast built together: one question underneath, and each character a different answer to it.
- The controlling idea is that question in one sentence: a value joined to a cause by *when*, *if*, or *because*.
- Moral complexity needs at least four value systems: a hero, a main opponent, and at least two more, each attacking a different face of the question.
- The theme comes from the collision of the characters' moral arguments, not from a speech given to one character.
- At A1 a stance is played through action and object, because no thesis line is available yet.
- Two-part opposition, one hero and one villain, is the primary failure. It reduces the argument to a slogan.

## The one question the cast answers

The work starts from a single **controlling idea**: a value joined to a cause by *when*, *if*, or *because*, said in one sentence. [[wiki/Story Craft/Premise and Controlling Idea|Premise and Controlling Idea]] is that sentence, the theme the web argues. That page and this one disagree on how the authorship spine is cast, and the disagreement is left standing. This page keeps the doctrine: one question, four corners as the minimum, and a dual-spine test. It works the love-spine as the live web.

The house through-line on that same spine is the sentence the love-web argues: love given in a language the beloved cannot receive reads as no love at all, until each learns to receive the other's. Every recurring character is a **stance** on that sentence, one defensible answer played in behavior. A stance that can only be announced, never played, becomes a speech. Preventing that speech is why this method exists.

[沈文](/tsumugu/cast/shen-wen.html)'s stance is presence. He shows up. He packs the umbrella nobody asked for. [阮草](/tsumugu/cast/ruan-cao.html)'s stance is provision, as one expression of chosen care: the envelope home every month. Neither is wrong. The series never lets either win. That constraint keeps the question open long enough to argue for the length of a series. If one stance were allowed to defeat the other, the argument would reduce to the two-part slogan that the minimum exists to prevent.

```
Question: love given in a language the other cannot receive.
Presence: show up, pack the umbrella.
Provision: send the envelope, refuse the hand.
Neither wins. The question stays open.
```

## Four corners, the minimum for moral complexity

Moral complexity has a minimum: a hero, a main opponent, and at least two more, each a whole value system attacking a different face of the central question. That minimum is **four corners**. Two-part opposition, one hero and one villain, is the primary failure, and the tests below look for it. The second spine, *who authors a life you did not choose?*, is cast to this minimum as doctrine. The names that fill those corners are not settled here.

## One web, not two

The test that this is one web and not two is whether the central characters answer *both* spine questions at once. The same character has to answer love and authorship. Otherwise the reader is watching two casts under one title. The presence-lead answers presence on love and authorship on the second spine: he builds the small one, gathers the armored one, and lets the provision-lead adopt him. The provision-lead answers provision on love and authorship on the second spine: a life she did not choose, and a found family she builds anyway. When a character sits on only one axis and never touches the other, the cast has split into two subcasts under one title.

## The theme comes from collisions

Every relationship drawn on the web is also a position in the argument. Two characters in collision argue the question. Two characters running in parallel do not.

Over the story, each major character makes, in dialogue, a moral argument justifying what they do to reach the goal. The author's vision lands in the collision of those arguments, not in a speech someone is handed. The theme is earned when the reader watches the arguments collide, not when a character states it. A dispute over money stages this as form: provision against presence, with neither character given the winning argument. The standard is that no one is right, no one is wrong, and each argues from a value they hold. That is a steelman rule for the writing. It is not a claim that every scene already passes.

The theme's full statement waits for a wordless reconciliation that says the controlling idea without a thesis line. Holding the statement back until that beat is the rule. The unpublished climax is not written here.

## Who can carry a corner

Which members can carry that wait is a round-versus-flat decision. [[wiki/Story Craft/Round Characters and the Telling Detail|Round Characters and the Telling Detail]] owns that distinction: the capacity to surprise convincingly, not a different book's flat *arc*. Two leads are built to take the pressure of the question. [將軍](/tsumugu/cast/general.html) the café cat is fixed contrast, a running gag with no arc, and that is correct casting. A flat member holds a fixed reading of the question that the round members are still working out. Two adults, [蘇老師](/tsumugu/cast/teacher-su.html) and [吳老闆](/tsumugu/cast/boss-wu.html), are held deliberately light: full stances, low spotlight, adult-generation echoes.

Scene and dialogue have to come *from* the character. A surname pasted onto generic prose is not a stance.

[[wiki/Story Craft/Foils and Pairings|Foils and Pairings]] is the two-character case of the same web: one pair, shared ground, opposite method.

## Playing a stance at A1

At A1 the reader has words for actions only, so a stance that was designed as an answer to the question is *played* through action and object. There is no thesis line available yet. The umbrella and the envelope have to hold the argument until the words arrive. This project's first book sits at that level, about A1, not the later B1 of the language exams. Assign each recurring member a fixed position and let vocabulary-limited behavior carry it.

[[wiki/Story Craft/Interiority Through Action and Object|Interiority Through Action and Object]] is how that playing is done.

A silent plant can do the work. A photo from home surfaces, and the subject changes before anyone explains it. The image is an unexplained picture he will not discuss. It is not a live grandmother-Want. The stance itself, presence-hunger and a family he never had, is fully present in action and object, and it is legible at A1 whether or not that photo is the vehicle.

Front-load the handful of Han for the theme's key value words, so climactic dialogue is readable when the argument comes to a head. The order in which vocabulary is taught sets the order in which the argument can be stated out loud. The web lets the debt build through behavior at A1 and settle in dialogue once the vocabulary ceiling lifts.

## How the web fails

Two-part opposition, one hero and one villain with no third or fourth corner, collapses the argument into a slogan. That is the primary failure.

```
Weak: one hero, one villain, a slogan.
Strong: four value-systems, no clean winner, a question a reader can name.
```

A **name-swap ensemble** is the next failure: characters who sound identical and hold the same value, so the web argues nothing.

A character conceived only to fill a corner reads as schematic, an argument rather than a person. One corner on the second spine stays thin here, *what making is for*, mend versus make, and it stays thin rather than being dressed with a fresh invented detail.

A tight two-hander or a single-protagonist story should not use the method. It pays the cost of four corners and ends up with a debate panel.

The price is that cost. Four whole value-systems have to be kept live. Spotlight has to be rationed, so that the book and not any one room contains the web. [[wiki/Story Craft/Spotlight Rotation|Spotlight Rotation]] covers when each member anchors a scene. This page covers who they are.

## When to merge, re-cast, or redistribute

Change the cast when the evidence says so.

- A justifying line moves into another mouth with no loss. Both characters hold the same answer. Merge or re-cast.
- A scene needs all four corners in the room to make its point. Redistribute. The book holds the web, not the room.
- A test reader picks a clean winner in a value-dispute. A corner has collapsed into villainy.
- A reader partway through cannot name the one question. The tracks were parallel.

## What should be checkable

- State the one question and place two or three characters as opposed answers.
- A dispute reads as two people both right.
- A wordless beat lands the idea without a thesis line.
- An early object registers as a live absence to an A1 reader.

The companion pairing-grid, fifteen duets and three finer engines, is a different object. It is not imported here.

## The reader has to be able to name the question

The ensemble is still one argument. If a reader cannot name the question, the tracks were parallel. An umbrella and an envelope are already two answers. The test is whether anyone watching them can say what the two are answering.

## How to practice this

1. Write your one question as a single sentence. Join a value to a cause by *when*, *if*, or *because*. Notice whether it still fits in one sentence.
2. Give each recurring character one answer to that question, played in behavior. Notice any stance you can only announce in a speech.
3. Count the whole value systems in your cast. Notice whether you have fewer than four corners.
4. Move a character's justifying line into another character's mouth. If nothing is lost, both hold the same answer. Merge or re-cast.
5. Write a dispute where neither character is given the winning argument. Notice whether a test reader still picks a clean winner.
6. Ask a reader partway through to name the one question. Notice whether they can say what the characters are answering.
