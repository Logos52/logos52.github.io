# Premise and Controlling Idea

A **premise** is one causal sentence the story sets out to prove: `[trait] leads to [end]`, true inside this work only. The **controlling idea** is the same spine at the final act: **value** the climax leaves plus **cause**. If either needs two sentences, the spine is unclear or two stories are bundled.

Story structure carries the argument because whoever wins at the climax proves the premise, while a speech explaining the moral gives the proof away before events earn it.

## Ending shapes and opposition

- Idealistic endings move from negative to positive, pessimistic endings move from positive to negative, and ironic endings hold both, making ironic endings the hardest to carry across an ensemble.
- A **counter-idea** must be argued with equal force against the main thesis, or the story reads like propaganda.
- A writer sets four characters (the hero, the main opponent, and two secondary opponents) against one another so each acts on a different value rather than echoing the rest ([[wiki/Story Craft/The Character Web|The Character Web]]).

## Premise vs controlling idea

A premise states what a character trait drives inside a story, such as poverty driving crime, frugality ending in waste, or ruthless ambition ending in self-destruction. A controlling idea names the value left standing at the climax and why it won, such as justice prevailing when someone risks comfort, or love dying when pride outlasts need.

## Locked and open for Tsumugu

**Founding pair locked:** provision without presence starves the love it means to prove. Climax controlling idea: bond survives **changed** because presence replaces the failed fix and love is chosen again, not restored.

At the climax, the struggle between provision and presence leads to the realization that the burden can be shared, which forces a choice for presence over fixing and leaves the bond changed.

**Series-level sentence unlocked.** Candidate question (not picked): how a person meets a life they did not choose. Subordinates: the initial pair sentence, and the care-over-capability thread. Map honestly, do not flatten.

## Moral core (what a character will pay for)

Separate from story premise: a character's **moral core** is the short list of rules they pay for, not recite. Forged by events, it is tested by charging the rule against what they love most. A speech stating the core is a **receipt** only if scenes already paid, and otherwise it is a soapbox.

**Eight diagnostic shapes** (name after writing choice under pressure): Steadfast, Idealist, Oathkeeper, Steward, Tender, Maker, Redeemer, Hidden Law (concealed rule, distinct from an honest-dishonesty rogue who states the rule while refusing virtue credit, as covered in the retired merged pages).

Villains use the same loop, and belief rides on the price paid rather than the morality of the law.

House cores (compression, not verified by cast cards): 沈文 freedom and presence, 阮草 keep-alive-by-love, 星野 author assigned life, 金多恩 mend honestly, 阿迪 old inside new, 白龍 speak on drift, 小圖 care not capability. Use one shape per lead, while support may run on wants.

The loop fails when the test hits something they do not love, when the law is costume, or when the price never lands.

## Series scale without flattening

A multi-book run honestly carries **dominant sentence + subordinates**. Repeating the first book's premise in every sequel adds no new argument across the series. Subordinates stay named: care versus capability, assigned life versus authored life, presence inside constraint versus escape with proof. Each thread gets its own controlling idea at its own climax, and the series sentence, when chosen, should predict how those climaxes rhyme rather than replace them.

## Costs and quit signals

Stated premise enforces cut: scenes that work but do not argue get removed.

Quit signals: no one-sentence compression, a climax pulling multiple values, a character preaching the moral, two cast members giving the same answer, or an object explained in prose.

Checkable: after the climax, a reader states what value won and why unaided, and removing a character breaks the argument or proves they carried nothing.

Skip apparatus when discovering a draft, writing mood or slice without argument, or working while the series spine is still open (this project's series sentence is a valid skip).

## Check against objects

After a climax, list every load-bearing object (mushroom, chair, envelope, photo). If removing one does not change what value won, either the object was scenery or the controlling idea was never argued through action. For Tsumugu, the object test is the earliest falsifier. It fails at B1 before theme-words exist.

## How to practice this

1. State the premise in one causal sentence, and write the controlling idea as value plus cause.
2. Map the climax through battle, self-revelation, moral decision, and new equilibrium.
3. List four corners with distinct answers.
4. Find object naming, then cut naming.
5. Name one moral core, and locate the forge event and priced test.
6. Check reader recall after the climax.
