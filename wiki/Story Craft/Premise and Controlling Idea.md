# Premise and Controlling Idea

A premise is the one-sentence claim a story proves through what happens in it. Writing that sentence down before drafting gives every later choice a test, since a scene that does not help prove or challenge the claim can be cut. Lajos Egri, Robert McKee and John Truby each give a version of the same tool.

## Core takeaways

- State the story's claim in one sentence before drafting.
- The claim names a value and the cause that decides it.
- One story proves one premise.
- The story has to argue the opposite side at full strength.
- Each main character gives a different answer to the same question.
- The ending shows the answer through what the characters do.

## Three versions

Egri, writing in 1946, put the premise in the form of a trait that leads to an end. McKee calls it the controlling idea and builds it from a value, such as justice or love, and the cause that makes that value win or lose. Truby treats theme as an argument the story makes through what the characters do. All three tie the idea to cause and effect in the plot, so the sentence can be checked against the scenes.

| Writer | Form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Egri | trait leads to end | ruthless ambition leads to its own destruction (Macbeth) |
| McKee | value + cause | justice wins because the hero is more violent than the criminals |
| Truby | argument through action | the climax proves which way of living works |

- Write the sentence with a cause in it.
- Check each scene against it.
- If a scene proves nothing, cut or change it.

## Three ending shapes

McKee sorts endings by what happens to the value named in the premise. The value can win, it can lose, or it can do both at once. In the mixed case the hero gets what they wanted and pays for it, or loses it and gains something else in its place.

- Idealist: the value wins.
- Pessimist: the value loses.
- Ironic: a win with a price, or a loss with a gain.

## The counter-idea

A story that only shows its own side reads like a sermon. McKee's rule is that the opposite idea must get its strongest case, so that the audience feels the ending could have gone the other way. Truby builds this in with what he calls four-corner opposition: the hero and three opponents each hold a different answer to the story's central question. The climax then settles which answer holds.

```
          hero
           |
opponent --+-- opponent
           |
       opponent

each corner: a different answer to one question
```

- Give each opponent a real reason.
- Let the counter-idea win for a while.
- Save the hero's self-revelation and moral decision for the end.

## Delivering the idea

The premise is almost never spoken aloud in a good story. It reaches the audience through repeated objects and images, and through the choices the characters make at the climax. A final object only carries the idea if it was planted earlier, so cutting the early plants makes the ending fall flat.

- Repeat an object tied to the idea.
- Keep the early plants the final object depends on.
