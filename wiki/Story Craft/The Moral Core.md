# The Moral Core

A moral core is the one rule a character keeps even when keeping it costs them something they love. Robert McKee's point is that true character shows in choices made under pressure, and the moral core is what those choices keep returning to. A writer who knows a character's core can decide what that character does in any scene and can price each choice so the audience feels it.

## Takeaways

- A core is one rule, said in a few plain words.
- The audience learns the core from what the character chooses.
- Each test of the core should cost more than the last.
- The best test sets the core against something the character loves.
- A core can be formed before the story or during it.
- Villains can have cores too, spent on the wrong cause.

## How it works

John Truby's rule is that a story makes its moral argument through events. So the writer stages the core as a choice with a price, and raises the price each time. Early tests cost comfort or status, later tests cost a friend, a home or a life, and the last one often costs the thing the core was protecting. Where the core is formed also sets the shape of the arc: a core the character already has makes a steady character who changes others, and a core formed on the page makes a change arc.

- State the core as a short rule the character would say.
- Stage a small test early, paid in comfort or pride.
- Raise the price at each later test.
- Put the core against family, love or duty near the end.
- End with the character paying in full, or breaking.

```
test 1        test 2          test 3            last test
comfort  -->  reputation -->  a friendship -->  life or love
      the same rule, a higher price each time
```

## Examples from published stories

Captain America shows the rising price in its clearest form. In The First Avenger (2011) the doctor who picks Steve Rogers asks whether he wants to kill Nazis, and he answers that he does not want to kill anyone and does not like bullies. The core is set before the serum makes him strong. In Civil War (2016) he holds his line against a friend and against the team that had become his family, and at the end he drops the shield that made him Captain America.

| Character | Core | Price |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Steve Rogers | Stand up to bullies, compromise only where you can | His team, then the shield |
| Tony Stark | Make his life count, as a dying man asked | Carries the bomb through the portal himself |
| Luke Skywalker | Nobody is beyond saving | Throws his weapon away |
| Obi-Wan Kenobi | Duty to the galaxy over love | Twenty years in the desert |
| Ser Duncan the Tall | Protect the weak | Risks his life in a trial by seven |
| Jon Snow | Keep his oath to protect the realm | Killed by his own men for letting outsiders in |
| Ned Stark | Honour | Bends once to save his daughters, and it buys nothing |
| Maximus | Strength and honour, in service to Rome | His life |
| Luthen Rael | Win the rebellion at any cost | His whole life, for a win he will not see |

## Where it goes wrong

The common failure is a core stated in dialogue and never paid for on screen. A core that bends without cost is another, because the audience stops believing it was ever a core. A mood or a trait cannot stand in for a core either, since a core comes from an event in the character's past, such as a death, a betrayal or a promise.

- Trace every core back to an event in the character's past.
- Make every bend of the core cost something visible.
- Give villains cores too, and let them pay for them.
- Thrawn keeps a code of honour and serves someone else's evil.
- Tywin Lannister puts his house above everyone in it, even his son.
