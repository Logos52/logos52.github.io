# The Change Arc

A change arc is the path of a character who starts out believing something false about themselves or the world and ends by acting on the truth. It is the most common arc in fiction, and K. M. Weiland's work on character arcs gives it a standard set of parts. A writer who knows the parts can place the key turns and check that the ending follows from the start.

## Core takeaways

- The character begins holding a false belief, called the Lie.
- A past hurt, the Ghost, taught them the Lie.
- The Want is what they chase, the Need is what they lack.
- At the midpoint they glimpse the truth.
- In the third act the Lie costs them the most.
- At the climax they act on the truth.
- Seed each big turn one or two chapters or episodes earlier.

## How it works

The Lie shapes what the character wants, and the want drives the plot. John Truby calls the past event behind the Lie the ghost, and he separates two kinds of need: a psychological need, a flaw that hurts the character, and a moral need, a flaw in how they treat other people. Each turn of the plot tests the Lie, and the character's choice at each turn moves them toward the truth or away from it. Weiland puts it as a loop: the character drives the plot, and the plot shapes the character.

- Ghost: a past event, never a mood.
- Lie: the belief the ghost left behind.
- Want: the goal the Lie points at.
- Need: what would fix the Lie.
- Midpoint: a glimpse of the truth, not yet acted on.
- Third-act low: the Lie's worst cost.
- Climax: the choice that proves the truth.

```
Lie ----- midpoint ----- low point ----- climax
 |        (glimpse)      (Lie costs      (acts on
want       truth          the most)       truth)
drives
plot
```

Robert McKee adds that each scene swings a value, and that the climax proves the controlling idea, the one sentence the whole story argues. Aristotle's hamartia, a misjudgement the hero discovers late, is an early form of the Lie.

## Examples

Two public stories show the shape. In Kiki's Delivery Service a young witch's gift for flying becomes her job, her Want is to keep the delivery service going, and her Need is a sense of self that does not depend on her output. In Pride and Prejudice Elizabeth Bennet refuses Darcy's first proposal on the strength of her misjudgement of him, and accepts the second after she has learned the truth about him and about herself.

- Kiki's Want: keep the delivery service going.
- Kiki's Need: a self that does not depend on the work.
- Elizabeth: refuses while holding the Lie, accepts after learning the truth.

## Terms that get confused

A flat arc and a flat character are different things. E. M. Forster's flat character is simple and does not surprise. Weiland's flat arc belongs to a character who already holds the truth and changes the world around them. The negative arc is the change arc reversed, so the character ends worse off than they began.

- Flat character: simple, and never surprises.
- Flat arc: the truth held from the start, and others change.
- Negative arc: the character moves further into the Lie.
- Weiland sorts arcs into five types: one positive, one flat, three negative.

## Where it fails

A change arc fails when its turns are not prepared. If the midpoint glimpse or the climax choice comes with no earlier seed, the change looks invented. In a large cast, two characters reaching the same recognition at the same point split the attention, so one gets it and the other waits. Under a small vocabulary, objects and actions carry the Lie until the words for it exist.

- Unseeded turn: the change looks sudden.
- Two recognitions at once: give the spotlight to one.
- Lie stated in words too early: show it in action instead.
