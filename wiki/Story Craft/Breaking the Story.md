# Breaking the Story

Breaking the story is the step where what happens in an episode gets decided completely, before a line of script exists. It runs on cards rather than prose, one event each, moved around until the order holds. What it produces is structure rather than sentences.

It carries its own name because it carries its own calendar, its own deliverables, and its own approvals, the same as any other stage of production. What that buys is a draft that no longer has to decide the events while it is writing them, which is where drafts usually stall.

## Core takeaways

- Breaking decides every event in an episode before any script page exists. The script then writes the scenes the outline already named.
- A beat is a scene stated as function plus location: a slug line and one or two sentences of what occurs.
- The letters A, B, C, and D say how much a strand weighs. They never say when it plays.
- The lock is the approval after which the executing writer may not add, delete, or substantially alter scenes. It binds because crew is already spending against the outline.
- One showrunner put about three-quarters of the labor before the first script page, at two to three weeks of a full room per episode. That is one room's accounting, not a measured constant.
- Multi-cam is the one named exception to deciding everything in the break. Writers rewrite while actors rehearse, that night, and again the next day.

## What a beat is

The unit of the decision is a **beat**: a scene stated as function plus location, written as a slug line and one or two sentences of what occurs. Some rooms use the word for something smaller than a scene. That smaller use is room-specific.

Dialogue is the last thing to exist. Writing is structure, and dialogue is the small part added at the end. An hour-drama script runs about forty-five pages, inside a common range of forty-two to fifty-five. The outline that precedes it is eight to fourteen pages of the same beats, each expanded to a paragraph, and only there do key snippets of dialogue appear. The two lengths show the labor split: ten to fourteen pages of decided function against forty-five pages of spoken execution.

## The documents, in order

The documents harden in a fixed order.

A **story area** is two to three pages of prose that precede the beat sheet. It is the episode said as a short argument, not yet as scenes.

The **beat sheet** is the short list of those scenes: slug line, act and scene number, one or two sentences, three to four pages for the whole episode. That page count is one source's default, not a finding.

The **outline** expands each beat to a paragraph. One source's default sits around ten pages. One hour-drama room sent ten to fourteen. The eight-to-fourteen range covers both.

Notes then come from a senior writer, a showrunner, a studio, and a network, still before the script. That chain is a fact about a network pipeline. It is not an org chart a solo writer is supposed to hire.

The script that follows is execution of a decision already made. Nothing in the later documents is allowed to invent a new scene. They can only write the ones the outline already named.

## Letters and strands

The letters A, B, C, and D rank how much a strand weighs, meaning its importance and its screen time. They never rank the order it plays. An A-story can open in scene four. A C-story can open the hour. [[wiki/Story Craft/Spotlight Rotation|Spotlight Rotation]] uses the same letters at season scale, for weight and never for order.

**D**, where it exists, is a **runner**: a light thread glimpsed a few times, not a full plot. [[wiki/Story Craft/Seeding and Payoff|Seeding and Payoff]] is the mechanism that turns a complete strand into those spaced glimpses. The letter here only names the weight.

Each strand is broken alone to a complete beginning, middle, and end, then braided in a separate pass. The **blend**, the braid, is that second pass. It is not a way of inventing the strands. A writer breaking without a room can use this unit: one strand, complete, then braid.

Analysts reconstruct one long-running multi-cam as three strands, the cast paired off, each pair a mini three-act inside the episode. That reconstruction is an analyst's. It is not that show's writers confirming how the room broke. The hedge stays attached to the claim, and it returns below as an open question rather than as a finding.

The blend pass interleaves the strands so the episode cuts away from each one at a point that pulls the viewer forward, with a twist or escalation at each act break. There is no test for where that cutaway point is. A room proverb says cut away at a question, not an answer. It is a proverb rather than a measured rule, and it does not close the gap.

## Cold opens and act counts

A **cold open** is the detachable teaser before the titles. It is built as one joke or game run the whole way to a twist, it is often unrelated to the episode, and it is routinely swapped between episodes for time. One dress-alike gag was written for one episode and traded to another. The open can move because nothing in the hour that follows depends on it.

Multi-cam shape, per union format notes, is cold open, two acts, and a **tag**, the short closer after the last act. One published multi-cam script ran forty-two pages: a page and a half of teaser, Act One about fifteen and a half pages across four scenes, Act Two about twenty pages across six scenes, and a page-and-a-half tag. Those numbers are a worked format, not a law.

Two single-cam rooms keep the detachable open and then split differently. One room uses a cold open plus three acts plus a tag. Another uses a cold open plus four acts. The detachable open is the constant across them. The act count varies from room to room.

## The lock

The **lock** is the approval after which the executing writer may not add, delete, or substantially alter scenes. It binds because the outline has already gone to crew for pre-production, before the script exists. Locations are being booked. Days are being scheduled. Crew is already spending against those pages. That is a production fact. It is not a taste fact, and it is not a feeling that the room is done.

After lock, a failing beat escalates to the head writer or showrunner. It is never unilaterally re-broken. The first try is to make the existing scene work. If it will not work, escalate. The lock bars a writer who can see the fix from making it alone, so being able to see the fix does not grant permission to make it. By then every instrument that can re-open the story sits outside the scripting writer's hands. [[wiki/Story Craft/The Beat Board|The Beat Board]] is the instrument those hands no longer hold: how a beat is written onto a card, the causality tests, and the board as authority.

The escalation path has no stated resolution here, meaning it is not settled whether the board re-opens or the outline is patched. That stays an open question. The rule a writer can actually follow is smaller: do not add, delete, or substantially alter scenes on an approved outline.

## Testing beats at the board

There are two tests and two instruments. At the board a failing card comes off. At the table read the question is whether the story tracks.

Every beat answers three questions: is it indispensable to the plot, is it interesting on its own terms, and does it set up the scene that follows. A no on any one takes the card off. Bad ideas stay in circulation on purpose, because sometimes a bad idea leads to a good one. The three questions are not a purge of everything unfinished. They test a card that is claiming a slot.

One hour-drama room's loop was to ask "where is this person at?" and then to ask the same question for the next major character. The room moved only after every major character had been asked. The useful extraction is per-person, not per-plot. A plot that is complete while a major character has not been asked is not a finished break.

A slow board is normal. A time-lapse of one break covers twelve days with almost nothing on the corkboard for days. A near-empty corkboard for those days looks the same as a stalled room until cards go up, so an empty board on its own does not tell you which one you have. [[wiki/Story Craft/The Beat Board|The Beat Board]] owns the card-level tests that decide which cards stay.

## The table read

A **table read** is actors reading the script aloud. It is watched for whether the story tracks: whether attitudes are right, and whether steps are missing, rushed, or repetitive.

Laugh data at a table is unreliable in both directions. A laugh can come for a joke that will fail once it is performed. Silence can come for a joke that will land once the actors have it. Fix the story immediately. Give jokes a chance to work once they are performed.

Multi-cam is the named exception to the rule that what happens is decided in the break and nowhere downstream. Writers rewrite while actors rehearse, watch the run-through, rewrite that night, and rewrite again the next day. The break still decided the hour. The rewrite cycle is allowed to patch what the table and the floor expose.

## What breaking costs

The seventy-five percent figure is one showrunner's accounting of one process, not a measured constant across rooms. That room ran two to three weeks per episode, with all writers breaking every episode together. Two to three weeks is the high end of the same interview cluster. The all-together half is the confirmed half. "Seven writers" is a snapshot, and headcount fluctuated across seasons.

The cost, stated as that room's: two to three weeks of a full room per episode, about three-quarters of the labor before a script page, and days of nothing visible. The scripting that follows was called "kind of carefree" compared to the breaking.

When the break is done, any writer in the room can execute the episode. Scripting carries about a quarter of the labor. Once the board is done, every plot beat is determined. That is the room's own logic for why the later pages can be handed to whoever is free.

If progress is measured in script pages, a room that is breaking will look idle for as long as the break runs.

## Breaking without a room

What happens in the episode is decided in the break and nowhere downstream, apart from the multi-cam rewrite cycle named above. Whether seventy-five percent holds in a divided room, or for a writer breaking alone, is not established. A writer breaking alone cannot hire that room and should not treat its calendar as a personal quota. What transfers is the unit, one strand broken complete and then braided, and the lock, the outline finished before pages.

The quit signal is the calendar, not silence. When the break runs past its budget with the board still empty, shrink the unit and break one strand alone to a complete beginning, middle, and end. An empty board inside the budget is still working. An empty board past the budget is the point at which to shrink the unit.

## How to practice this

1. Break one strand alone to a complete beginning, middle, and end. Braid it with the others in a second pass. Notice whether the braid invented any event the strand did not already have.
2. Write the story area first, then the beat sheet, then the outline. Notice whether any new scene appears after the outline has named them all.
3. Test each card with the three questions. Take the card off if the answer is no to any one. Notice how many cards leave the board.
4. Ask "where is this person at?" for every major character before moving on. Notice whether a plot you thought was finished still has an unasked character.
5. Set a budget in days for the break. Keep working while the board is empty inside that budget. Shrink the unit to one strand when the budget runs out.
6. At a table read, listen for attitudes, missing steps, rushed steps, and repetition. Fix story problems immediately. Leave the jokes alone until the actors have performed them.
