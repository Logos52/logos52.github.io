# Breaking the Story

Breaking the story is what a TV writers' room calls working out an episode's events one at a time before anyone writes script pages. Each event is called a beat, and a beat is one event that changes the situation. Doing the break first means structure problems get found on a board of cards, where moving a card is cheap, instead of in a finished draft.

## Core takeaways

- The room breaks the story together, then one writer scripts it.
- Structure is most of the writing work.
- A break moves from area to beats to outline to script.
- An episode's stories, called strands, are lettered A, B, C by weight.
- Each strand gets written whole, then blended into the episode.
- A table reading shows whether each strand still tracks.

## The pipeline

Most rooms produce the same documents in the same order, and each one is narrower and more exact than the one before. The story area is a short statement of what the episode is about and who it happens to. The beat sheet lists the events in order. The outline turns those beats into scenes written as prose, and once the producers and the network have given notes on it, one writer turns the outline into a script.

```
story area -> beat sheet -> outline -> notes -> script
   (room)       (room)       (room/writer)    (writer)
```

- Each beat goes on a card on a board.
- The board is the record the room trusts.
- A card has to be caused by the card before it.
- The outline is locked before scripting starts.
- Breaking Bad sent outlines of 10 to 14 pages to the crew.

## The room

Vince Gilligan described the Breaking Bad room as doing most of the hard work in the break. Once the room had agreed the structure, the writer could script with little worry, because every scene already had a reason to exist. This desk's rough reading of his account is that about three quarters of the work happens in the break. Nobody measured that figure, and it stands for how the room described itself.

- The whole room works on the break together.
- Bad ideas are kept, since they often lead to good ones.
- Gilligan's summary: writing is structure.

## Strands and letters

An episode usually runs two or three stories at once. The A story gets the most weight and screen time, the B story less, and the C story is often a runner, a small gag or thread that comes back a few times. The letter says how much of the episode a strand gets. The order the strands appear in on screen is decided later, when they are blended.

- Write each strand complete, start to end.
- Then blend: cut the strands into scenes and alternate them.
- A blended runner shows up as short, spaced glimpses.
- A runner can be one joke or game played the whole episode.
- A cold open, the scene before the titles, can move between episodes.
- Outside analysts read Friends episodes as three blended strands.

## Testing the break

The break gets tested again once there is a script. At a table reading the cast reads the whole episode aloud. The writers listen for whether each strand still makes sense on its own and for where the laughs land. Multi-camera sitcoms, which are shot like a play in front of an audience, get rewritten every day of production week from what the readings show.

- The story-tracks test asks whether each strand can be followed alone.
- Laughs at the table are data for the rewrite.
- Multi-camera sitcoms work to a tight page budget per episode.
