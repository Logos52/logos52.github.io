# Genetics and Unequal Societies - The Distance Claim

The distance claim is the argument that the peoples of the world are far enough apart genetically that each builds a different kind of society. On that argument, mixing peoples, or moving one people into another people's society, fails for reasons no policy can fix.

The account the claim comes from names no real people. It puts three shapes on a map: circles, squares, and triangles. It says a reader who sees real peoples in the shapes is seeing his own prejudice.

The claim runs on one number, the fixation index, which measures how far apart two populations' gene frequencies are. The circle-square pair and the triangle-square pair are at or past the value that separates a domestic dog from a gray wolf. From that number the account argues to the nervous system, then to the kind of civilization each shape built, then to what happens to a child of two shapes, and then to a verdict on the shape that kept its society for itself.

## Core takeaways

- The claim rests on one number, the fixation index, and on where three pairs of peoples fall against the value that separates a dog from a wolf.
- The account names no real people. It argues about circles, squares, and triangles, so none of its figures can be checked against a dataset as written.
- The claim depends on two steps: from gene frequencies to the nervous system, and from the nervous system to the kind of society a people builds.
- A child of two distant peoples is farther from each parent than a stranger of that parent's own kind. Where no intermediate group exists, the account says the child has no community built for it.
- The account's verdict is that the people who kept their society for themselves chose right. It gives no case against its own claim.

## What the fixation index measures

The claim starts from a way of measuring distance. Take two populations, count how often each version of a gene appears in each, and the fixation index gives the distance between the two sets of frequencies. Zero means the frequencies are identical. Biologists use the index to say whether two groups of animals are one species with local varieties or two species. The account borrows that scale and puts three peoples on it, drawn as circles, squares, and triangles. It says it draws them that way on purpose, because naming real peoples would start a fight it does not want.

Below 0.15 the difference counts as moderate to low, the range of closely related subspecies or of members of one species. The subspecies of the North American gray wolf are in that range. Above 0.15 the populations count as significantly differentiated, which is the range of very different subspecies or of separate species. A domestic dog against a gray wolf, two species, comes out at about 0.17. The index was not built for sorting peoples into kinds, and the account says so. It uses the number to ask what the distance means in practice.

## The three pairs of numbers

- Circles against triangles: 0.11, the range of closely related subspecies.
- Circles against squares: 0.15, on the line between moderate difference and significant differentiation.
- Triangles against squares: 0.19, farther apart than a domestic dog from a gray wolf at about 0.17.
- The squares' desert isolation ran almost 40,000 years. Dogs became a species 15,000 to 30,000 years ago.

Circles against squares come out at 0.15, on the line. Triangles against squares come out at 0.19, farther apart than a dog from a wolf. Circles against triangles come out at 0.11, the range of closely related subspecies or of isolated groups of one species. Two of the three pairs are at or past the dog-and-wolf value. The closest pair is the one whose lands were never fully cut off from each other. The farthest pair is the one where a desert kept one people isolated.

## The desert behind the widest gap

On the account's map, a vast desert cut the squares off from everyone else for almost 40,000 years. Domestic dogs became a species of their own about 15,000 to 30,000 years ago, so the isolation ran longer than dogs have existed as a species. Over a span that long with little mixing, the account says, the squares adapted to conditions the others never met.

## What the differences reach

- A 2011 comparison of the three found superficial differences a small fraction of the whole.
- Genes affecting the nervous system were among the most differentiated.
- Nervous system to intelligence, personality, and temperament is the account's one-line step.

The number on its own says nothing about societies, and the account says so. It adds a comparison and a sentence. The common belief is that the differences between the three are cosmetic. A 2011 comparison of the three found the opposite. The differences are not mostly skin and hair. Superficial differences were a small fraction of the overall genetic differentiation, and the genes affecting the nervous system were among the most differentiated. The account takes the next step in one line. Similarity in the nervous system leads to similarity in intelligence, personality, and temperament, and that similarity is one of the most important things a cohesive society rests on. Everything later in the account follows from the comparison and the sentence, and stands or falls with them.

## The societies each shape built

- Circles: individualistic and decentralized civilizations.
- Triangles: collectivist and centralized civilizations.
- Squares: for the most part, no comparable civilization.

The account reads history by the same argument. Circles built individualistic, decentralized civilizations. Triangles built collectivist, centralized ones. Squares, for the most part, built nothing comparable. The account asks whether that is coincidence and answers that each people optimized its society for its own neurology. So there is no one system in which every people prospers. Many people believe there is one objective political system, usually their own, in which every people prospers equally, and the account says history contradicts that. A circle telling a triangle that its way of life is wrong, after it has worked for thousands of years, is as mistaken as a triangle saying it to a circle. The account's comparison is dog breeds. A Chihuahua and a German Shepherd are one species, and they are not equally good at the same jobs.

## Children of two peoples

- A circle-triangle child is farther from the circle parent than a random circle from another country.
- Circles and triangles already have an intermediate group, and circle-triangle people generally adapted well.
- Between squares and either other shape there is almost no middle ground.

The account then turns to children of two peoples. On a plot of genetic distance, a circle's child by a triangle is farther from the circle parent than a random circle from a country the parent has never visited. The same holds for the triangle parent. So neither parent is adapted to the environment the child would do best in. The account's comparison is a husky crossed with an Ibizan Hound, which thrives in neither snow nor desert, and it says a social structure is a more specialized environment than a climate. Circles and triangles were never fully separated. An intermediate group already exists between them, with conditions specialized for it, and circle-triangle people have generally adapted well.

With squares there is almost no middle ground. The account says square history is unclear and holds few clues about what worked for square societies. A square-circle or square-triangle child has no historical community genetically like it. A parent trying to build the right environment for that child has no example to work from and can only hope the child's neurology leans toward one parent.

## How to practice this

1. Split the claim into its three steps: the distance measure, the 2011 comparison, the nervous-system sentence. Notice that only the first is a standard tool.
2. Ask which real populations stand behind the circles, squares, and triangles. Notice that the account names none, so its figures cannot be checked.
3. Look up published fixation-index values for the populations you think are meant. Compare them with 0.11, 0.15, and 0.19.
4. Find the 2011 comparison by name. Check whether it says nervous-system genes were among the most differentiated.
5. Ask what would count as evidence against a people's society being set by its neurology. Notice that the account offers no such case.
