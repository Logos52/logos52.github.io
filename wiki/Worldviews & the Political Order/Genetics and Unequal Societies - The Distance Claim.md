# Genetics and Unequal Societies - The Distance Claim

## Reasoning

### Abstract

The distance claim is the argument that the peoples of the world sit far enough apart genetically that each builds a different kind of society. Mixing them, or moving one people into another's society, fails for reasons no policy can fix. The account this page takes it from names no real people. It puts three shapes on a map, circles, squares, and triangles. It says that any parallel a viewer draws is the viewer's own prejudice. The engine of the claim is one number, the fixation index, which measures how far apart two populations' gene frequencies sit. The circle-square pair and the triangle-square pair sit at or past the line that separates a domestic dog from a gray wolf. From that number the account walks to the nervous system, then to the kinds of civilization each shape built. Then it walks to what happens to a child of two shapes, and to a verdict on the shape that keeps its society for itself.

### Core takeaways

- The claim rests on one number, the fixation index, and on where three pairs of peoples sit against the line that separates a dog from a wolf.
- The account names no real people. It argues in circles, squares, and triangles, so nothing on this page can be checked against a dataset as written.
- The step that carries the claim is from gene frequencies to the nervous system, and from the nervous system to the kind of society a people builds.
- A child of two distant peoples sits farther from each parent than a stranger of the parent's own kind, and where no intermediate group exists, the account says the child has no community built for it.
- The account's verdict is that the people who kept their society for themselves chose right, and it offers no case against itself.

### Essay

The claim starts from a way of measuring distance. Take two populations, count how often each version of a gene appears in each, and the fixation index says how far apart the two sets of frequencies sit. Biologists use it to say whether two groups of animals are one species with local varieties or two species. The account borrows that scale and lays three peoples on it, drawn as circles, squares, and triangles. It says it draws them that way on purpose. Naming real peoples would be a fight it does not want. The reader who sees real peoples in the shapes is told the seeing is his own.

The circle-square pair and the triangle-square pair sit at or past the line where the scale stops calling two groups one kind. The pair that sits closest together is the one whose lands were never fully cut off from each other. The pair that sits farthest apart is the one where a desert kept one people isolated for a span of time longer than dogs have been dogs. On the account, that isolation is where the distance came from. A people adapting alone for that long adapts to conditions nobody else met.

The number alone would say nothing about societies, and the account knows it. So it takes a comparison and a sentence. The first is a comparison that found the differences between the three are not mostly skin and hair. The genes behind the nervous system were among the most different. The second step is a sentence. A shared nervous system means shared intelligence, personality, and temperament, and those are what a cohesive society is made of. Everything after that follows from the comparison and the sentence, and stands or falls with them.

What follows is a reading of history. One shape built loose, decentralized civilizations. One built tight, centralized ones. One built nothing comparable. The account says that is not chance. Each people built the society its own neurology runs best in. So there is no one system in which every people prospers. A people that tells another its way of life is wrong is making the same mistake the other would make in reverse. Then it turns to children of two peoples. Such a child sits farther from each parent than a stranger of the parent's own kind, so neither parent is fitted to the world the child would do best in. Where an intermediate people already exists, the child has somewhere to land. Where none exists, the parent builds blind.

The account ends on a verdict rather than a test. The people who tried to run mixed societies got results that pleased nobody. The people who declined to try kept their society tuned to themselves. They were called callous, and the account says the results make that hard to argue with. Nothing on the page is offered against the claim. The premises are the comparison and the sentence. A reader who wants to check them needs the real populations behind the shapes, the comparison by name, and the thresholds on the scale. The account gives none of those.

### Conclusion

So the distance claim is a chain of three links. The first is a measure of genetic distance. The second is a comparison that puts the nervous system among the most differentiated parts. The third is a sentence that turns the nervous system into the kind of society a people builds. The first link is a standard tool. The second is one study, unnamed. The third is asserted. The page carries the chain as the account gives it. It holds no position of its own, because none is on record here yet. What a check needs is written under the open questions.

The fixation index is a measure of how similar the gene frequencies of two populations are. Below 0.15 the difference counts as moderate to low, the range of closely related subspecies or members of one species. The subspecies of the North American gray wolf sit there. Above 0.15 the populations count as significantly differentiated, which is the range of very different subspecies or of separate species. A domestic dog against a gray wolf, two species, comes out at about 0.17. On the account's map, a vast desert cut the squares off from everyone else for almost 40,000 years. Domestic dogs became a species of their own about 15,000 to 30,000 years ago, so over a span that long with little mixing, the squares adapted to conditions the others never met.

The account reads history through the same lens. Circles built individualistic, decentralized civilizations. Triangles built collectivist, centralized ones. Squares, for the most part, built nothing comparable. It asks whether that is coincidence and answers that each people optimized its society for its own neurology. Many people treat politics as a deity, one objective system, usually their own, in which every people prospers equally. History contradicts that. A circle telling a triangle that its way of life is wrong, after it has worked for thousands of years, is as mistaken as a triangle saying it to a circle. A Chihuahua and a German Shepherd are one species, and they are not equally good at the same jobs.

The sharpest part of the claim is about children. On a plot of genetic distance, a circle's child by a triangle sits farther from the circle parent than a random circle from a country the parent has never visited. The same holds for the triangle parent. So neither parent is adapted to the environment the child would do best in. A husky crossed with an Ibizan Hound thrives in neither snow nor desert, and a social structure is a more specialized environment than a climate. Circles and triangles were never fully separated, though. An intermediate group already exists between them, with conditions specialized for it, and circle-triangle people have generally adapted well.

With squares the middle ground is slim, a no man's land. Square history is blurry and holds few clues about what worked for their societies. A square-circle or square-triangle child has no historical community genetically like it. A parent trying to build the right environment for that child acts blindly and can only hope the child's neurology leans toward one parent.
