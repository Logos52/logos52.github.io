# How to read these arguments

This group is the toolkit for the rest of [[wiki/Worldviews & the Political Order/Worldviews & the Political Order|Worldviews & the Political Order]]. Every later page makes claims about power, prosperity, groups, guilt, immigration, or sex. Those claims only deserve a verdict after two checks: whether the conclusion follows from what was said, and whether the starting points are true. The folded logic pages and the old argument-validation hub now live here. [[wiki/Worldviews & the Political Order/Per Capita|Per capita]] rates belong in the same pass, because most political fights smuggle raw totals past readers who never divide by population size.

## Valid form and true premises

An argument can be valid without being sound. It is valid when its form rules out any case in which the premises are true and the conclusion is false. It is sound only when it is valid and every premise is actually true. Checking an argument therefore takes two separate questions: whether the conclusion follows, and whether the starting points hold.

Validity is about form alone. Elizabeth owns a Honda or a Saturn. She does not own the Honda. Therefore she owns the Saturn. If the first two lines are right, the last one has to be right, whatever car she really owns. The premises do not have to be true for that form to be valid. A valid argument with a false premise is valid but unsound.

Truth is still worth checking, because the closer someone's picture of a business, a channel, or a relationship is to the truth, the better that person does at it. Political arguments fail in both ways at once: bad form, false premises, or both. Group 1 keeps the two questions separate so you do not treat a slick chain as proof, or dismiss a sound chain because you dislike its conclusion.

## Hidden warrants and loaded questions

Many arguments hide a premise called the warrant, which links the evidence to the claim. Hearing barking and howling supports "a dog is nearby" only because we already know dogs bark and howl. That background link is the warrant.

Questions can smuggle warrants the same way. "How did your views on Harry Potter change given JK Rowling's bigoted opinions?" assumes the opinions are bigoted before any evidence is named. In one classroom case, a student walked through the presupposition with his teacher and changed his mind once he saw he did not have the evidence for it. Finding the hidden link is half of reading politics: the headline stat, the moral label, and the "everyone knows" line each carry warrants that should be named before you argue about the claim.

## Steelman, then disagree

A fair critic restates the other side so clearly that its holder would welcome the wording, lists points of agreement, and only then offers a rebuttal. The medieval article form did something similar: Aquinas's articles on whether God exists state the problem of evil before the answer. Being offended is no part of the test. If you cannot state the opposing view in words its holder accepts, you are not yet arguing with the view that is actually in play.

The old argument-validation hub folded here added one more layer. Misleading numbers, misleading words, misleading pictures of the speaker, and misleading presentations of your own case each get their own tests, but they all rest on the same two questions. Validity depends on form. Truth depends on the world. Everything else is detail about how people dodge one or the other.

## Rates before totals

Raw counts deceive whenever group sizes differ. [[wiki/Worldviews & the Political Order/Per Capita|Per capita]] is the shared tool for that mistake in this section: divide the count by the people it refers to before you compare places, groups, or years. Market pages, crime headlines, welfare debates, and empire arguments all reuse the same arithmetic. Read that page once, then apply it whenever a later group quotes a big number without a denominator.
