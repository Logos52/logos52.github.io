# How to read these arguments


The two pages on reasoning tools come first in the section's reading order, and both give tools for checking an argument before reading the pages that make one.

## Validity and Truth

# Validity and Truth An argument can be valid without being sound. It is valid when its form rules out any case in which the premises are true and the conclusion is false. It is sound only when it is valid and every premise is actually true. Checking an argument therefore takes two separate questions, whether the conclusion follows and whether the starting points hold. Because validity is about form, a valid argument with a false premise is valid but unsound. Truth is still worth checking, since the closer someone's picture is to the truth, the better that person does. Many arguments hide a premise called the warrant, which links the evidence to the point being made, and a question can hide a premise in the same way. Testing an argument fairly means restating the other side so well that its holder would welcome the wording, then listing points of agreement, and only then offering a rebuttal. Being offended is no part of that test.

## Argument Validation

# Argument Validation An argument is valid when its form rules out true premises alongside a false conclusion. It is sound only when it is valid and every premise is actually true. Checking an argument therefore takes two separate questions, whether the conclusion follows and whether the starting points hold. The first question needs only the argument's form, while the second needs the world, since a valid argument with a false premise is valid but unsound. Many arguments also hide a premise called the warrant, which links the evidence to the point being made, so finding that hidden link is part of the check. Each test works on any argument, whatever it is about. Together they cover misleading numbers and words, the person making the argument, your own judgment and the way you present your own case. The two questions call for different work. Validity depends on form alone. Take the argument that Elizabeth owns one of two cars, a Honda or a Saturn, that she does not own the Honda and that she therefore owns the Saturn. If the first two lines are right the last one has to be right, whatever car she really owns. Truth is still worth checking, because the closer someone's picture of a business, a channel or a relationship is to the truth, the better that person does at it. A fair test also gets the other side right before attacking it. The critic first restates the opposing position so well that its holder would welcome the wording, then lists points of agreement, and only then offers a rebuttal.
