# Mass Immigration - Cohesion

Large-scale immigration is a question of who is admitted and whether they can share a settlement. Selection on capacity, skill, and self-sufficiency is the criterion; assimilation follows from selecting well. Volume without that test is the case under examination.

Twelve defenses circulate. Three of them are slogans, six are claims about money, one is about crime, one is a moral appeal, and one is a statement that resistance is pointless. A thirteenth defends the arrangement without claiming any benefit from it, on the ground that no outsider has standing to object. They fail in different ways, and two of them partly hold. The money arguments are the ones that can be made in public. They are not the ones that decide the question.

## The slogans

Diversity is a strength, this is a nation of immigrants, no human is illegal.

None of the three is an argument, and the first two invert cleanly, which is the tell. Unity is equally sayable and equally unsupported; the diversity that does demonstrable work is diversity of ideas, which is not what the slogan is deployed to protect. The historical claim is true and answers a question nobody asked — the migrations it refers to were selected, in practice if not always on paper, for people who could enter an existing settlement, which is the case the present argument is not making. The third states a fact about persons to settle a question about jurisdictions, and every country on earth maintains the distinction it denies.

## The money

Here the defenses cluster, because wages and ledgers are easier to argue than whether a settlement still holds.

**Jobs nobody local will take.** There is no work a citizen refuses, only a wage, and the wage is set by what the marginal worker will accept. Entry-level wages in construction and IT sit at twenty dollars an hour and stay there, because an employer facing a demand for more can hire three foreign labourers for what one citizen costs. The claim concedes its own mechanism: an argument that the labour is needed because the locals won't take the pay is an argument that the pay is the point.

**More paid in tax than taken out.** This holds for selected high-skill migration and inverts for the low-skill flows that make up the volume. It also prices only the direct transfer and leaves out schooling, policing, housing, and the administration of a population that arrives needing all three.

**Higher GDP.** Adding people to a country raises its total output, and raising total output is not the claim anyone actually cares about. The measure that tracks whether individuals are better off is output per person, and the direction of that one is the whole dispute.

**Rescuing the pension system.** A scheme that pays a growing base of recipients out of a shrinking base of contributors has a structural problem, and importing contributors postpones the arithmetic rather than fixing it — at the cost of a larger obligation later, carried by people who did not design it.

**More entrepreneurial.** The category is doing the work. It counts anyone who has registered as self-employed alongside anyone who has built a firm, and the two are not the same economic act.

**Vital for technology.** This one partly holds, and it is the page's own criterion arriving as someone else's defense. Recruiting the best available people from anywhere is a coherent national strategy and a ruthless one, and it has nothing to do with volume. Admit on capacity, skill, and self-sufficiency; assimilation follows from selecting well. That is the opposite of the position the defense is offered to protect.

Read together, the money defenses describe a real transfer from six directions. Labour costs fall, output rises in aggregate, and the public balance absorbs what the private one saves. The transfer is true and secondary. What it never reaches is whether the people admitted can live under the same rules, or whether the settlement that made the wage argument possible is still there after the volume arrives.

Underneath the six sits a defense that claims no benefit at all: a firm hiring a foreign worker has made a private contract, and a third party has no standing to object. The framing is clean, and it holds only where both sides pay the full cost of what they agreed to. Housing, schooling, policing, and the upkeep of order around a newly arrived population are carried by the public rather than by the employer, which is what makes the wage cheap in the first place. A firm that could not run the same operation while paying those costs is running a subsidised one, and a subsidy is not a private matter.

## Crime

That immigrants commit fewer crimes than natives is a comparison engineered before it is made. Second-generation figures, which run markedly higher, sit outside it — the selection effect does not transmit, and practices do — deportation removes part of the population that would otherwise show up in the recidivism count, and the whole native population is set against a subset selected for the qualities that got it admitted. Separate the high-skill entrants from the mass inflows and the remaining gap is small enough to be an artefact of the sorting.

The claim is not false so much as unmeasured. What it reports is the effect of the selection, presented as an effect of the immigration.

## The moral claim

The obligation argument is the one with real weight, and it is also the one that has a boundary its advocates rarely state. Helping people in danger is a good, and the good is served best by placement where the person can enter an existing settlement — which is usually nearer, and already running a compatible one, than the destinations under discussion. That refugees are frequently not accepted by the countries closest to them is a fact about those countries and an argument that somebody should ask them why.

Where it fails is in the leap from an obligation to strangers in general to an unlimited one on a particular set of states. A state that cannot name a limit on whom it must absorb has stopped deciding who it is for. That is a cohesion failure before it is a fiscal one.

## The fatalism

That the flows cannot be stopped is the only defense that is straightforwardly false rather than incomplete. Countries with severe demographic pressure and every incentive to import labour have controlled their borders continuously and still do. The argument's function is not to persuade but to exhaust, and it is worth naming as such because an argument aimed at demoralising is not aimed at being right.

## What the sorting shows

Two defenses survive contact in reduced form. Recruiting exceptional people is sound and *is* the selection criterion, not a reason to abandon it. Sheltering people in danger is sound and argues for placement rather than for one region absorbing what the nearer ones decline.

Neither supports volume without a test. The money arguments describe a transfer that is real and not the load-bearing objection. A country can get the wage arithmetic right and still lose the ability to live as one.

## Open questions

Where a selected intake assimilates and an unselected one does not, what would count as a measure of the settlement rather than of the wage?

Where the nearer countries decline to accept refugees, does that transfer an obligation to more distant ones?
