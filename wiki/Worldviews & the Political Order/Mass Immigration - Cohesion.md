# Mass Immigration - Cohesion

Large-scale immigration is a question of who is admitted and whether the people admitted can share one settlement. The criterion is selection on capacity, skill, and self-sufficiency. Assimilation follows from selecting well.

Twelve defenses of immigration at volume, admitted without that test, circulate. Three are slogans. Six are claims about money. One is about crime. One is a moral appeal. One says that resistance is pointless. A thirteenth defends the arrangement without claiming any benefit from it: no outsider has standing to object to a private hire. The twelve fail in different ways, and two of them partly hold. The money arguments are the ones that can be made in public. They are not the ones that decide the question.

## Core takeaways

- Twelve defenses circulate: three slogans, six claims about money, one about crime, one moral appeal, and one saying resistance is pointless. A thirteenth says a firm's hire is a private contract that no third party may object to.
- Two defenses hold in a narrower form: recruiting exceptional people, and sheltering people in danger. Neither supports volume without a test.
- The six money defenses describe a real transfer. Labour costs fall, total output rises, and the public balance absorbs what the private balance saves.
- The crime comparison sets the whole native population against a subset selected for the qualities that got it admitted. Second-generation figures, which run markedly higher, sit outside it.
- The criterion is selection on capacity, skill, and self-sufficiency. Assimilation follows from selecting well.
- That the flows cannot be stopped is false. Countries with severe demographic pressure and every incentive to import labour have controlled their borders continuously.

## The three slogans

The three slogans are: diversity is a strength, this is a nation of immigrants, and no human is illegal.

None of the three is an argument. The first two invert. Unity is equally sayable and equally unsupported, and a statement whose opposite is equally sayable carries no argument. The diversity that does demonstrable work is diversity of ideas, which is not what the first slogan is used to protect.

The historical claim is true. The migrations it refers to were selected, in practice if not always on paper, for people who could enter an existing settlement. That is not the case being made for immigration at volume.

The third slogan states a fact about persons in order to settle a question about jurisdictions. Every country on earth maintains the distinction that slogan denies.

## The six money arguments

Six of the defenses are about money. Wages and public accounts are easier to argue about than whether a settlement still holds.

**Jobs nobody local will take.** Citizens do not refuse the work. They refuse the wage, and the wage is set by what the marginal worker will accept. Entry-level wages in construction and IT sit at twenty dollars an hour and stay there, because an employer facing a demand for more can hire three foreign labourers for what one citizen costs. The claim states its own mechanism. If the labour is needed because locals will not take the pay, the pay is the point.

**More paid in tax than taken out.** This holds for selected high-skill migration. It inverts for the low-skill flows that make up the volume. The figure prices only the direct transfer. It leaves out schooling, policing, housing, and the administration of a population that arrives needing all three.

**Higher GDP.** Adding people to a country raises its total output. Total output is not the number that says whether individuals are better off. That number is output per person, and the direction of that one is the whole dispute.

**Rescuing the pension system.** A scheme that pays a growing base of recipients out of a shrinking base of contributors has a structural problem. Importing contributors postpones the arithmetic rather than fixing it. It leaves a larger obligation later, carried by people who did not design it.

**More entrepreneurial.** The category is too broad. It counts anyone who has registered as self-employed alongside anyone who has built a firm. Those are not the same economic act.

**Vital for technology.** This one partly holds. Recruiting the best available people from anywhere is a coherent national strategy, and a ruthless one, and it has nothing to do with volume. It restates the criterion: admit on capacity, skill, and self-sufficiency, and assimilation follows from selecting well. That criterion is the opposite of the position this defense is offered to protect.

Taken together, the six money defenses describe a real transfer from six directions. Labour costs fall. Total output rises. The public balance absorbs what the private balance saves. The transfer is true and it is secondary. None of the six reaches the question of whether the people admitted can live under the same rules, or whether the settlement that made the wage argument possible is still there after the volume arrives.

## The private contract defense

Underneath the six sits a defense that claims no benefit at all. A firm hiring a foreign worker has made a private contract, and a third party has no standing to object.

That holds only where both sides pay the full cost of what they agreed to. Housing, schooling, policing, and the upkeep of order around a newly arrived population are carried by the public rather than by the employer, and that is what makes the wage cheap in the first place. A firm that could not run the same operation while paying those costs is running a subsidised one. A subsidy is not a private matter.

## The crime comparison

The claim is that immigrants commit fewer crimes than natives. The comparison is engineered before it is made.

Second-generation figures run markedly higher and sit outside the comparison. The selection effect does not transmit to the second generation, and practices do. Deportation removes part of the population that would otherwise show up in the recidivism count. The whole native population is set against a subset selected for the qualities that got it admitted. Separate the high-skill entrants from the mass inflows and the remaining gap is small enough to be an artefact of the sorting.

The claim is not false. It is unmeasured. What it reports is the effect of the selection, presented as an effect of the immigration.

## The moral obligation

The obligation argument carries real weight, and it has a boundary its advocates rarely state. Helping people in danger is a good. That good is served best by placement where the person can enter an existing settlement. Such places are usually nearer than the destinations under discussion, and are already running a compatible settlement. Refugees are frequently not accepted by the countries closest to them. That is a fact about those countries, and it is a reason to ask those countries why.

The argument fails at the leap from an obligation to strangers in general to an unlimited obligation on a particular set of states. A state that cannot name a limit on whom it must absorb has stopped deciding who it is for. That is a cohesion failure before it is a fiscal one.

## The claim that the flows cannot be stopped

That the flows cannot be stopped is the only defense that is straightforwardly false rather than incomplete. Countries with severe demographic pressure and every incentive to import labour have controlled their borders continuously and still do. The function of the argument is to exhaust rather than to persuade. That function is worth naming, because it means the defense is not offered on the ground that it is correct.

## What holds after the sorting

Two defenses hold in a narrower form. Recruiting exceptional people is sound, and it is the selection criterion rather than a reason to abandon it. Sheltering people in danger is sound, and it argues for placement rather than for one region absorbing what the nearer ones decline.

Neither supports volume without a test. The money arguments describe a transfer that is real and is not the objection the question rests on. A country can get the wage arithmetic right and still lose the ability to live as one settlement.

## How to practice this

1. Take a slogan you hear about immigration and say its opposite out loud. Notice whether the opposite is equally sayable. If it is, the slogan is not an argument.
2. When someone cites higher output, ask whether the number is total output or output per person. Notice that total output rises whenever people are added.
3. When a crime comparison is offered, ask which two populations are being compared. Check whether a selected subset is set against a whole population.
4. When a wage is called cheap, list who pays for the housing, schooling, and policing around the worker. Notice whether the employer carries those costs or the public does.
5. When a tax-contribution figure is offered, ask whether it covers high-skill or low-skill arrivals. Notice whether schooling, policing, and housing are priced into the figure.
6. When someone says the flows cannot be stopped, check whether countries with every incentive to import labour control their borders. They do, continuously.

## Open questions

Where a selected intake assimilates and an unselected one does not, what would count as a measure of the settlement rather than of the wage?

Where the nearer countries decline to accept refugees, does that transfer an obligation to more distant ones?
