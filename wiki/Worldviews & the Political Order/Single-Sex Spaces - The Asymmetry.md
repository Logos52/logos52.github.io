# Single-Sex Spaces - The Asymmetry

The asymmetry, as the Front Page video describes it, is that American law spent about 40 years opening men-only clubs while leaving women-only spaces legal, and nothing replaced what men lost. The video, [The Hypocrisy Of Male VS Women Spaces](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Kcy9rQjMglU) traces how courts used public accommodation laws to integrate men's clubs and why carve-outs protect women's spaces. It then follows the gap into men's friendships, work and deaths. The video says it is not taking a side, and it frames its conclusion as measured by outcomes: the balance between equal access and privacy, safety and past disadvantage went further than almost anyone intended.

## What men used to have

The video says fraternal orders were once a normal part of a man's life. A journalist's 1897 count put membership at 5.4 million, about one in five adult men, and the Freemasons alone reached roughly 4 million American members in the 1950s. The video explains their pull with a practical example, calling them "the welfare state before the welfare state existed." Before Social Security, a roofer who broke his back got rent money from his lodge, and a miner who died of tuberculosis got his funeral paid and his widow a small stipend.

## How the doors were opened

The video separates two causes. On the demand side, younger men stopped joining, and it cites Robert Putnam's *Bowling Alone* for the claim that losing these institutions explains about half the drop in civic engagement after 1960. On the supply side, the courts acted. The video lists the cases in sequence:

- In 1984, *Roberts v. United States Jaycees* held that the Jaycees were too big, commercial and open to count as an intimate or expressive association, so Minnesota's public accommodations law applied. Sandra Day O'Connor's concurrence, which treated the group as a business in all but name, is the part the video says dismantled the rest.
- In 1987, the same logic ended Rotary International's men-only rule, and Lions and Kiwanis followed.
- In 1988, a New York City law forcing large private clubs to admit women was upheld.
- In 1990, the New Jersey Supreme Court ruled that Princeton's eating clubs were public accommodations, after an 11-year suit by Sally Frank.
- In 1996, *United States v. Virginia* ended men-only admission at the Virginia Military Institute.

The video says Augusta National, Skull and Bones and the Boy Scouts gave way under pressure rather than court order. It adds that Scouting America's membership fell from roughly 6 million in 1972 to about 1 million today, while the Girl Scouts stayed girls-only.

## Why women's spaces stayed legal

The video gives the three rationales courts and legislators wrote into carve-outs. Privacy covers locker rooms, restrooms and shelters, and it applies to men's spaces too. Safety covers domestic violence shelters, since women are roughly 70 to 80% of intimate partner violence victims. Religious accommodation covers women-only gyms for some Muslim and Orthodox Jewish women.

Its argument is that those three reasons do not stretch to cover everything now women-only. A women-only executive club for vice presidents and C-suite officers is not a domestic violence shelter, it says. In its view The Wing was an elite networking platform with a sex-restricted door, the same pattern the Jaycees lost over in 1984. The video says The Wing was forced to drop its policy after a complaint under New York City's human rights law and later closed, but the video says most women-only spaces never faced that treatment. It notes that the right calls this a double standard and the left calls it a corrective.

## What the gap costs men

The video ties the loss of institutions to these outcomes:

- In 1990, 55% of men said they had at least six close friends, against 27% in 2021, and men with no close friends went from 3% to 15%.
- Almost 80% of American suicides are male, and almost 39,000 men killed themselves in 2023.
- The Surgeon General's 2023 advisory put the mortality risk of chronic loneliness at roughly that of smoking 15 cigarettes a day.

## Attempts to rebuild

The video's best example is men's sheds, which began in Australia in the early 1990s. A nonprofit stocks a building with tools and a kitchen, and men come to build things, talk and have coffee. Researchers think it works because men socialize shoulder to shoulder through shared activity instead of face to face. F3 Nation's free dawn workouts follow a similar idea. The video's verdict is that these lack scale, since 3,000 sheds worldwide is "a rounding error" next to the Masons in 1950.
