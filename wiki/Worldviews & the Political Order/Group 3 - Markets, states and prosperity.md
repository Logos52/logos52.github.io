# Markets, states and prosperity


The markets group asks why some economies grow and others fail, and it reads from the general argument about planning to the country cases.

## Socialism - Why Planned Economies Fail

# Socialism - Why Planned Economies Fail The calculation problem is the argument that a state which owns every factory and farm cannot plan well. Nobody buys or sells those things, so no prices form to tell planners what anything is worth. As a result, planned economies run short of some goods while piling up others. Socialism has a second problem, separate from prices, which is that it puts more effort into dividing wealth than into making it. A society with too many takers and not enough makers falls apart. Venezuela shows how far that can go, since it became so busy taking, dividing and reallocating that people starved in the streets. Attention focused on taking is attention not focused on building. Put plainly, "if you don't make stuff, there is no stuff." Without prices, planners set quotas, so factories meet the quota and not the need. The economy then ends up with too much of one thing and too little of another. During the Great Leap Forward, between 36 million and more than 45 million people died between 1958 and 1962. Each step toward a toll like that looks harmless on its own. Over the last century socialism killed between 65 and 100 million people. That is a higher death toll than the Black Death. The common replies are that real socialism has never been tried and that the Nordic countries show it working, yet Denmark calls itself a market economy.

## Socialism as Moral Correction

# Socialism as Moral Correction Socialism is often defended as a moral correction for real wrongs such as inequality, exploitation, predatory industries and market crashes. Several of those wrongs are real, but most of them come from the state's abuse of markets, so the socialist answer of a larger bureaucracy makes them worse. The whole debate over economic models also distracts from a corrupt financial system, because no model works while those financial abuses continue. Oligarchy, predatory healthcare firms and market crashes all trace back to the alliance between the state and big business, which is why the cure is more competition with less government picking of winners. The other moral arguments treat wealth as a fixed pie, but wealth grows in long-term games where people build it together. Equality pursued by force can only cut people down, and the charge of exploitation leaves out the owner's work and risk. Socialism also cannot reduce scarcity, because it distributes resources without acquiring new ones.

## China - The Temple and the Monks

# China - The Temple and the Monks China looks rich because its state takes the wealth that people produce at every level and spends it on visible projects. Households, meanwhile, keep one of the smallest shares of national output of any modern economy. A Chinese saying sums it up: "The temple is rich, but the monks are poor." Visitors see the temple, with its highways, skyscrapers and bullet trains, but most of the people who paid for it cannot afford to use it. The state can take that wealth because it owns the land, the banks and the big industries. Through those banks, ordinary deposits pay for projects such as the bullet train network, which most households cannot afford to ride. Leaders keep the system because giving households a larger share would leave less for trains, the army and preparing to invade Taiwan, which makes the obstacle political. That system also builds ghost cities and ships its surplus abroad, where producers without such subsidies cannot compete. China's real growth came from markets, so it got there by moving away from the system it still officially says it follows.

## Financial Slavery

# Financial Slavery Some modern lending works like legal slavery, because the lender profits when the borrower fails and relies on the state to stop the borrower from getting out. The argument is usually put as satire, meant to help people spot and avoid predatory loans. Honest finance runs the other way, since the lender shares the borrower's risk and wins only when the borrower succeeds. Once a lender removes that risk, the goal shifts from repayment to keeping the borrower paying compound interest for life. Usury breaks the link between lender and borrower, because a lender who expects the borrower to fail profits from the failure through interest on the interest. The arrangement holds only when the state stops the borrower from walking away, as with student debt that cannot be cleared through bankruptcy. A whole country can fall into the same trap when it borrows every year to cover its gap and its interest, and a central bank deepens the trap by printing the money it lends. In the United States the national debt stood at $39 trillion in April 2026, and interest alone now costs more than the military.

## Europe - The Slow Agony

# Europe - The Slow Agony Europe has fallen steadily behind the United States, and the cause lies in its own choices more than in any outside shock. It traded output for leisure, bet its industry on Russian gas, kept 27 national markets instead of building one and regulated faster than it built. Those choices were affordable while trade grew, Russian energy flowed and America paid for Europe's security, but that world has ended. The gap shows in incomes, output and stock returns, and around 70% of the gap in output per person comes from lower productivity. Behind it sit costly energy after the loss of Russian gas, internal barriers between 27 national markets, rules that only the largest firms can carry and fewer hours worked. The danger is now urgent because the workforce is shrinking, so growth must come from productivity. Most of the known remedies, though, need all 27 governments to agree.

## Britain - Poorer Than Mississippi

# Britain - Poorer Than Mississippi A [Front Page video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ITFz2aDjKxY) argues that Britain is a large economy with a falling place on income per person, and the IEA poll it builds on suggests Britons, on average, overestimate where Britain stands. A poll for the Institute of Economic Affairs, a London think tank, asked UK voters where Britain would rank among US states on GDP per person. On average they put it 7th, and the [IEA reports](https://iea.org.uk/media/brits-want-growth-and-back-less-red-tape-cheaper-energy-and-lower-taxes-to-get-it-finds-landmark-iea-research/) using IMF data, that it actually comes last, below every one of the 50 states. The video argues that the gap comes from slow, compounding choices, with each refused planning permission, tax rise, closed factory and departing millionaire or young doctor adding to it.

## South Africa is a Warning to the West

# South Africa is a Warning to the West The Moon video [South Africa Is a Warning To The World](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YKGGUexEhUw) argues that South Africa went from Africa's strongest economy to a place of crime and blackouts in about 20 years. Its explanation is that the rulers captured the state and drained it. When public services failed, the rich bought private replacements, and the video says the West is starting down the same path more slowly. Its closing line is that the West "just has to keep on following in its footsteps, one failure at a time."

## East Asian Exams - The Arms Race

# East Asian Exams - The Arms Race In South Korea and China, one exam taken by teenagers decides, according to a video by The Invisible Hand, which university a student attends. The university then decides the job, the social standing and even the marriage that follow. Because the exam ranks students against each other and not against a fixed standard, any extra effort by one family forces every other family to match it just to keep its place. The video argues that arms race explains the cram schools, the cost of raising a child, the low birth rate and the growing pile of graduates with nowhere to go. Each family acts rationally, and the sum of those choices is ruinous for everyone.

## America's Industrial Revival - The Freight Signal

# America's Industrial Revival - The Freight Signal The figures and the freight map are the speaker's claims from the talk, not independently checked facts. A flatbed truck is a simple deck, about 53 feet long and 11.5 feet wide, with no walls and no roof. It is a poor way to move most cargo. Thieves can reach what it carries, weather soaks it, and it cannot keep produce at the right temperature. The interesting fact is that booking one has become extremely hard. Companies are choosing to pay much more to move cargo this way, including trucker rates that doubled in about 6 months. Talk can be spun. Paying that price cannot. That gap between speech and action is revealed preference, and freight data lives in that world. Other stories do not cover the move. A crackdown on commercial licenses appears to be only a small slice. Fuel is not enough either. Oil running over 100 dollars a barrel accounted for about 24 cents, or 8 percent, of the change being explained. Front-loading imports before tariffs arrive would heat the coasts first, the way the map has worked for about 3 decades.
