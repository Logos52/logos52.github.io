# China - The Temple and the Monks

China looks rich because its state takes the wealth that people produce at every level and spends it on visible projects. Households, meanwhile, keep one of the smallest shares of national output of any modern economy. A Chinese saying sums it up: "The temple is rich, but the monks are poor." Visitors see the temple, with its highways, skyscrapers and bullet trains, but most of the people who paid for it cannot afford to use it.

The state can take that wealth because it owns the land, the banks and the big industries. Through those banks, ordinary deposits pay for projects such as the bullet train network, which most households cannot afford to ride. Leaders keep the system because giving households a larger share would leave less for trains, the army and preparing to invade Taiwan, which makes the obstacle political. That system also builds ghost cities and ships its surplus abroad, where producers without such subsidies cannot compete. China's real growth came from markets, so it got there by moving away from the system it still officially says it follows.

## How the wealth reaches the state

The state can take that wealth because it still owns most of what produces it. No farmer owns his plot of land, and state banks hold 99.5% of all bank assets in China. Energy, most raw materials and the big industries are also under direct or indirect state control. Private wealth gives no protection either, because a billionaire's assets can pass to the state overnight, and people who cross the party can disappear for years.

Bank ownership is what turns ordinary savings into state projects. The bullet train network is in debt by about 900 billion US dollars and will never make money. Most people cannot afford its tickets, so they ride the slow ordinary trains, yet their deposits in state banks fund the fast ones. The money that builds the trains is money those households do not spend.

## Why the state keeps it

Leaders could rebalance the economy toward consumption, but doing so would mean handing households a larger share of national output. The state would then have less for trains, for building up the army and for preparing to invade Taiwan. The obstacle is therefore political, so the shift will not happen. One speaker on a tech podcast reads China's Communist Party as closer to a capitalist fascist organization than a communist one. Fascist here means a nation uniting behind a common cause, with communism as a veneer over a leadership that wants above all to stay in charge.

The same system keeps building whether or not anyone needs the result. Ghost cities rise for populations that do not exist, partly because nobody can count. A village may report 100 people when it has only 80 people, so that it can collect benefits for 20 people who do not exist. Once infrastructure and real estate stopped carrying growth, exports took over, because nobody inside China has the money to buy what the factories make.

## What it means for everyone else

Local governments hand factories free land, tax subsidies and energy below cost, so rival counties overproduce and sell below cost. Joining the World Trade Organization in 2001 let them ship that surplus abroad, where garment makers in Bangladesh and others without such subsidies cannot compete. Matching China on its terms would mean banning unions, nationalizing industry, ending free speech and keeping a workforce with no rights. The system depends on the workers staying poor, because they do the work and are not paid for it.

The growth China did achieve came from markets. Of the roughly 1.5 billion people lifted out of extreme poverty in a single generation, China accounts for about 800 million. It got there by moving away from the system it still officially says it follows.

## Open questions

- How do party leaders weigh keeping power against raising household spending?
- How do local subsidies for land, tax and energy push counties toward overproduction?
- How do ordinary families see their place in a system where their deposits fund projects they cannot afford to use?
