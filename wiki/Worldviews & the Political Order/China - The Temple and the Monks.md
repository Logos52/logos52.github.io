# China - The Temple and the Monks

## Reasoning

### Abstract

The temple and the monks is a Chinese saying about a country where the temple is rich and the monks are poor, and it is the shortest true account of how China's success is made. The state sets a growth figure, 5% of GDP, and every province, city, and village must hit it. So a dug hole counts. The concrete that fills it counts. The empty house on top counts, and the bigger empty house that replaces it counts too. The state owns every plot of land, 99.5% of bank assets, the energy, and the big industry. A billionaire owns his fortune until the state decides otherwise. Wealth flows from everyone toward the state and not back to the people, so the household share of the economy is among the lowest of any modern economy. The bullet trains run empty on the savings of people who cannot afford to ride them. The factories export because nobody at home can buy what they make. The machine that does this was built over a century by a party that was one in twelve thousand. It took the country with Soviet guns and American restraint. It starved at least 45 million people to overtake Moscow. It purged everyone so that one man could sleep.

### Core takeaways

- GDP in China is a quota, not a measurement. A dug hole, the concrete that fills it, and an empty tower on top all count. Output counts whether or not anyone buys it.
- The state owns every plot of land, 99.5% of bank assets, the energy, and the big industry, so wealth flows to the state and the household share of the economy is among the lowest in history.
- The bullet trains and the ghost cities are paid for by the savings of people who cannot afford to use them. The temple is rich and the monks are poor.
- Communism does not underproduce. It makes too much of this and too little of that. China exports the surplus because nobody at home can buy it. Competing with that means becoming it.
- The party that runs the machine was one in twelve thousand in 1936. It won with a Soviet army, an American arms embargo on its enemy, barrier troops, human waves, and starved cities.
- It starved at least 45 million people between 1958 and 1962 by making food a weapon, and then purged everyone so that one man could sleep. The same work goes on in Beijing today.

### Essay

Start with what a visitor sees. Highways, towers, a bullet train that leaves on time and arrives on time, shopping malls with luxury shops the size of supermarkets. Then ask who paid for it and who uses it. The train is empty because ordinary people ride the slow one. The towers are empty because the cities were built for people who are not there. The highway turns into a two-lane road at the city limit and a dirt track after that. Nobody sleeps on the street, because anyone who would is sent back to the countryside. The image is the product, and it is built and guarded with the same care as anything else the state makes.

Underneath the image is one rule. The state announces a growth figure and every official must hit it. When growth is a quota, anything that can be counted gets built, whether or not anyone needs it. The measure counts what comes out rather than what went in. So the country digs holes and fills them, builds and demolishes, and lays rail across a continent. Each step goes into the figure. The debt that pays for it has passed three times the size of the economy. The engines that carried the debt, infrastructure and then real estate, have run out, and what remains is selling abroad.

Selling abroad is forced by what the state keeps. The founding principle never changed: the means of production belong to the state. No one owns land. The banks are the state's. Energy and heavy industry are the state's. A fortune is held at the state's pleasure. So what everyone produces flows upward and does not come back down, and households keep a smaller share of the economy than in almost any country on record. People who keep so little cannot buy what the factories make. The factories are fed free land, subsidized tax, and energy below cost by local governments competing to hit their own quota. So they sell below cost to the rest of the world. That is why the mismatch, too much of one thing and not enough of another, is old and permanent. Matching it would mean stripping one's own workers of their pay and their rights.

The machine that does this was not chosen by the people it runs. In the 1930s the communist party was a few tens of thousands in a country of half a billion, about as popular as a band of outlaws, with nothing to promise because there were no serfs and no lords to take land from. Wherever it held a strip of poor mountain land it squeezed the villagers until they stopped producing, and its own leaders wrote that down. It survived on money and advisers from Moscow. It also survived on a story told by a visiting journalist that made it the underdog and the democrat.

It won because two foreign powers made it possible. One sent a million soldiers into the richest region of the country, held it, and handed the countryside and the weapons to the party. The other, believing the party was not really communist, forced the government it had called a great ally to stop fighting at the moment the party was nearest to extinction, and then cut off that government's arms. Grain and men were taken from villages at gunpoint. Soldiers were driven forward by troops who shot anyone who turned back. Unarmed civilians were sent ahead to absorb the fire. Cities were starved for months until they surrendered. The party took the country in 1949.

In power it did what communist regimes do, and then more. Terror against enemies real and imagined. Every shop and factory handed over with a smile, because the state was now the only employer. Every field and tool collectivized. Then the great leap, an attempt to turn every man and woman into a foot soldier and overtake Moscow. It removed every reason to work, so people were whipped to work. Villages lied about their harvests, and inspectors took the grain the lies promised. Food became a weapon that the weak did not earn. Tens of millions starved. The man in charge knew. He purged the people who told him and pushed harder. Then he purged the countryside, then the cities, then the army, until by his own account there was no one left to threaten him. That is the origin of the temple and the monks. A state built to keep everything, by a man who needed to keep everything, still keeps everything.

### Conclusion

So the success is real in the way a temple is real. The towers stand, the trains run, and the surplus is a trillion dollars. What is faked is the meaning. Growth is a quota. Output goes unbought. Wealth never reaches the people who made it. The image is kept clean by deporting anyone who would spoil it. The monks pay for the temple and cannot enter it. The account's advice is the oldest one, to study the history. Overcapacity, the managed image, and politics in command are not new, and very little about the country is. No position on China is held on this page, because none is on record here yet.

In a market economy GDP is a measurement of what happened. In China it is a quota, a command from above. The mayor of a city, the head of a province, and the head of a village must each show 5% growth whether or not anyone has money to buy anything. So they can do anything that counts. Dig a hole and it is GDP. Fill it with concrete and it is GDP. Build a house on top and it is GDP. Destroy the house and build a bigger one, still empty, and that is GDP too. The measure counts what comes out and not what went in, and it counts output whether or not anyone buys it. That is the second Stalinist feature, after the quota itself.

The debt that pays for it has passed 280% of GDP and now runs 300% to 320%. In 2008 the United States poured money into its economy, and the People's Republic poured in far more as a share of its own. For a time the debt looked huge but growth looked bigger, so the debt could be outgrown. That was never the case. The debt has accumulated steadily, and the engines that carried it have gone one by one. Infrastructure was needed in the 1980s after decades of neglect and is now overbuilt to meet the quota. Real estate became a scheme where one built to hit the quota and sold to the next, and ordinary people trying to get a foothold lost when it went down. What is left is exports.

### Who owns what

- Not one person in China owns a plot of land.
- 99.5% of bank assets belong to state banks.
- The household share of GDP is the lowest, or among the lowest, of any modern economy in world history.

The chairman took a year to see the use of that message, then held his troops back, let the central government fight the Japanese, and moved into territory once the Japanese had cleared it.

Even in 1940 the party was a minority. It won the way East Germany and Poland became communist: an army arrived. In August 1945 the Soviet Union sent 1 million troops into Manchuria, the richest region and the strategic gateway to China. It held the region for months and stripped it. Then it handed the countryside to the communists, along with weapons by the shipload, training, and officers sent to Moscow. The United States had made the Republic of China one of the four great allies. It then forced the central government to seek a coalition with the communists, on the belief that they were agrarian reformers and not real communists. In April 1946 the Soviets withdrew, and the central government pushed the communists to the Siberian border. In May 1946 the American envoy ordered a halt on pain of ending weapons deliveries. Later he imposed an arms embargo on the central government. So the communists were helped by Moscow and protected by Washington at the moment they were nearest extinction. From there they took grain and men from Manchurian villages at gunpoint. They used barrier troops that shot any soldier who turned back. They sent unarmed civilians forward in human waves to absorb fire. They starved cities into surrender. The siege of Changchun lasted 8 months and killed 160,000 civilians, and after it the commanders of other cities, Beijing among them, surrendered rather than face the same. In 1949 the red flag went up over the Forbidden City. When an American general in Korea later complained that these troops were impossible to fight, the head of the central government, by then on Taiwan, asked how he had been expected to fight them in 1948 with no help.

### The Great Leap and the Cultural Revolution

- At least 45 million died between 1958 and 1962, and some Chinese historians say 50 million, in a population of about 800 million.
- One prefecture of 8 million lost 2.4 million. The head of Sichuan reported 9 to 12 million starved and called it a good thing.
- The Cultural Revolution ran from 1966. The general who besieged Changchun died in 1971. A tree older than about 70 years is hard to find.

From 1950 to 1951 the party eliminated real and imagined enemies in a campaign of terror. By 1955 and 1956 every industrialist and shopkeeper had handed his deeds to the state with a smile and become a state employee, because the state was now the only employer. By 1956 and 1957 every farmer's land and tools had been collectivized. That was not enough. The chairman wanted what every communist leader wanted: to take the grain straight from the field into the state granary, sell it abroad, and buy whole industries with the currency. He had one advantage, people. If every man and woman in the countryside became a foot soldier working day and night, China could overtake the Soviet Union, not just England. The man who had brought a quarter of humanity into the communist camp would then lead the camp. So from 1958 every cup and tool was collectivized, people slept in dormitories and children in kindergartens, and the incentive to work vanished, as it had in the party's mountain slivers in the 1930s. Farmers who would not work were whipped. Every village competed to report more grain than it had, and the inspectors took the grain the reports promised. Food became a weapon. Work points earned in the field bought the ration, so the pregnant, the sick, the old, and the undesirable were cut off from the food chain as punishment. The account's minimum, from the archives, is 45 million dead between 1958 and 1962, and some Chinese historians say 50 million, about the toll of the Second World War worldwide. The head of Sichuan, a province the size of France, reported 9 to 12 million starved and told the chairman it was a good thing, because the survivors were the core and the dead were the slackers and riffraff. The sparrows were killed with gongs and drums so they could not land, and the locusts they had eaten thrived. Backyard furnaces smelted pots, pans, and door handles to meet the steel quota. The trees went for fuel, so a tree in China older than about 70 years is hard to find.

The chairman knew, because he ran a machine built to find out everything about everyone. When party members raised the famine in the summer of 1959, he read it as a threat and purged them, and pushed harder. Then he said what Moscow's dictator had said, that the further the revolution goes the more counter-revolutionaries there are, and purged the countryside from 1962 to 1966. The Cultural Revolution from 1966 purged the cities. It was not about culture. It asked ordinary people, for the first time in any communist regime, to denounce party members, and they did. Then from 1968 to 1971 the army purged the people. With the death in 1971 of the general who had besieged Changchun, the army was purged too. The test of loyalty was to denounce someone close, a friend, a colleague, a family member. Millions were purged, the party's number two was tortured to death in a dungeon, and hundreds of millions had their lives destroyed. By 1974 the chairman could say there was no threat left. That was the purpose: so that he could sleep. The man in charge in Beijing today does the same work. His military commission had seven members, he purged five, and the sixth has not been seen. Politics is in command, as the party's own slogan says, and in April 2020 the slogans in Hong Kong said it plainly: politics first, science second.
