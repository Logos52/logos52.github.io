# East Asian Exams - The Arms Race

One national exam at the end of school decides which university a student in China, South Korea, Japan, Taiwan or Singapore enters, and the university name then follows the person into hiring and pay for life. Because the exam ranks students against each other, every family that pays for extra tutoring forces every other family to pay too. That contest explains the region's tutoring bills, its birth rates and its surplus of graduates, and it settles whether the fix is a ban on tutoring or a different way of picking people.

## Core takeaways

- The exam ranks students against each other, not against a fixed bar, so spending by one family forces spending by all the others just to hold rank.
- Every family would be better off if all of them stopped, and none can stop alone, which is why the tutoring bans in Korea in 1980 and China in 2021 failed.
- Raising one child to 18 in Korea costs about 7.8 times the income per person, against 4.1 times in the US, and about a third of that goes to cram schools.
- A child's schooling has to be funded in full or the child loses rank, so families stop at one child; Korea's fertility rate was 0.72 in 2023, 0.75 in 2024 and 0.80 in 2025.
- The exam sorts by family income more than by ability: 36% of Korea's richest students reach the top universities against 9% of the poorest.
- Degrees outrun the jobs that need them: China graduated 12.22 million students in 2025, its youth unemployment stood at 18.9% in August 2025, and at least 70,000 of its delivery drivers hold master's degrees.
- The owner's position is that the system cannot be repaired from inside and should be replaced by judging people on evidence of ability, which the internet now makes visible for almost anyone.

## How it works

- The exam
  - China's civil service exam dates from 605 AD. The current university entrance exam, the gaokao, came back in 1977 after an 11-year ban; 5.4% of the 5.7 million candidates passed. South Korea copied the model with its college entrance exam, the Suneung, and Japan with a national test.
  - Places at the top universities are fixed. A score matters only relative to the other scores.
- The arms race
  - Private tutoring, in Korea sold by cram schools called hagwons, raises a child's rank. Once some families pay, the rest must pay to hold rank.
  - Korea: 6% of students in hagwons in 1979, 75% in 2008, over 80% in 2024. Families spent a record 29.2 trillion won on tutoring in 2024.
  - The contest moved down the ages: entrance exams for hagwons at age 7 and now at 4, kindergarten tests from 20 months, a two-hour English exam for 5-year-olds. Nearly half of Korean children under 6 attend a hagwon.
  - The school-age population fell 14.5% in 3 years while spending per student rose 43.7%. Fewer children did not lower the spending.
- The cost and the birth rate
  - Raising a Korean child to 18 costs about $275,000, 7.8 times income per person; about a third is tutoring.
  - A second child halves what each child gets in the contest, so families stop at one. Korea's birth rate is low mainly because families do not go back for a second child.
  - Korea's fertility rate was 0.72 in 2023, 0.75 in 2024 and 0.80 in 2025. The 2025 rise comes from a larger birth cohort and weddings delayed by lockdowns and is expected to fade after about 2028. One estimate puts Korean fertility 28% higher without status competition and education spending. The population is projected to fall from about 52 million to 22 million by 2100.
  - Taiwan, with over 54,000 cram schools, sits at 0.695. Singapore, with about 85% of upper-primary pupils in private tuition, sits at 0.87.
- Sorting by family income
  - Top-income Korean households spend nearly seven times what the poorest spend on tutoring. The poorest fifth spend about as much on cram schools as on food.
  - The three top Korean universities, known as SKY, take a few percent of students and supply nearly half of senior government officials and a large share of top CEOs. Samsung sets an internal grade by university, so pay at 40 traces back to the exam at 18.
  - In China, urban students are five times as likely as rural students to enter a top university; the odds are about 1 in 2,000 in some provinces.
- Too many graduates
  - About 70% of Koreans go to university, the highest rate in the world, and the degree pay premium there is 20% against about 40% in most developed countries.
  - China graduated 12.22 million students in 2025 and about 12.7 million in 2026. Youth unemployment for ages 16 to 24 hit 21.3% in June 2023; publication paused and resumed under a rebuilt measure, at 18.9% in August 2025. Ages 25 to 29 sit near 7%, ages 30 to 60 near 4%.
  - Graduates who find no fitting job keep studying or wait: 77.5% of Chinese undergraduates planned postgraduate study in 2024, civil service applicants per post in China rose from 40 in 2021 to 98 in 2026, and 504,000 young Koreans were "resting" in February 2024.
  - Peter Turchin's term for a society that trains more people for elite positions than it has positions is elite overproduction; the people left out turn against the society.
  - Chinese youth coined a word for each step down: involution (neijuan, everyone working harder for the same fixed reward), lie flat (2021), let it rot (2022), and in 2023 the degree as Kong Yiji's gown, after a story about a failed scholar who will not do manual work and dies poor in his scholar's gown.

## Why bans fail

- Korea banned private tutoring in 1980; officials whose children were caught lost office; tutoring went underground; Korea's Constitutional Court struck the ban down in 2000. A 10 p.m. curfew on hagwons came in 2009. Killer questions, exam items outside the school curriculum that only tutoring prepared for, were removed in 2023.
- China banned for-profit tutoring in 2021; 700,000 centres shut and $77 billion of market value was wiped out; tutors came back disguised as housekeepers; 70% to 80% of high schoolers still take extra classes; poor families lost the cheap option.
- Parents defend the exam. Without it, they expect the best places to go to the best-connected. The exam has been the accepted idea of who deserves power in China for 1,400 years.
- A ban removes the tutoring but not the ranking. As long as places are fixed and scores are ranked, the contest returns in another form.

## What this desk holds

- Merit means personal ability. The exam may once have measured it; it now measures how much a family paid for exam practice.
- The system survives because it is how things have always been done, and it cannot be repaired from inside. Each fix, a ban, a curfew, the removal of killer questions, leaves the ranking in place, and the contest moves.
- The replacement is judging people on evidence of exceptional ability, work they have done that others can see. One company group already hires this way, on that evidence and a short conversation rather than on the school name. The internet now makes such evidence visible for almost anyone.
- Replacing the exam would take a change in the society and perhaps the end of the tradition. This desk holds that it is still the only repair, and that the replacement would probably be Western.
