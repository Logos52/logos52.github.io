# East Asian Exams - The Arms Race

In South Korea and China, one exam taken by teenagers decides, according to a video by The Invisible Hand, which university a student attends. The university then decides the job, the social standing and even the marriage that follow. Because the exam ranks students against each other and not against a fixed standard, any extra effort by one family forces every other family to match it just to keep its place. The video argues that arms race explains the cram schools, the cost of raising a child, the low birth rate and the growing pile of graduates with nowhere to go. Each family acts rationally, and the sum of those choices is ruinous for everyone.

## Where the exam came from

China's university entrance exam, the gaokao, returned in 1977 after Mao's death, when Deng Xiaoping needed to modernise the country after the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution. Mao had banned it for 11 years. The video puts the 1977 sitters at about 5.7 million and the pass rate at 5.4%. The 1977 cohort went on to lead Chinese politics, technology and science. The idea is far older. In 605 AD the Sui Dynasty created the keju, an exam that in theory let any man test his way into the imperial civil service whatever his birth. Confucian values added the belief that study is the main road to mobility, family honour and success. South Korea later copied the model with its CSAT, also called the Suneung, and Japan with its national entrance test. The logic was meritocratic, since everyone sat the same test and only the best rose.

## Why the arms race starts

University admission is positional. Each parent would be better off if all the others stopped paying for extra tuition, but a parent who stops alone sacrifices their own child. Economists call that pattern an arms race. In Korea it now reaches toddlers. A test at age 6 or 7 decides entry to an elite cram school, called a hagwon, and an even earlier exam now exists for 4-year-olds. The video says nearly half of Korean children under 6 attend them.

The cost changes how many children people have. The video cites the Yuwa Population Research Institute, which ranks Korea as the most expensive country in the world to raise a child. Raising one to age 18 costs roughly $275,000, which is about 7.8 times the country's GDP per person, against 4.1 times in the US. A child becomes a campaign that has to be fought all the way, so one child raised properly beats two raised cheaply. Korean couples still become parents, and almost none go back for a second child.

## Why bans fail

Korea has tried to stop the race for 45 years. In 1980 the government banned all private tutoring and expelled officials from public office if their children were caught with a tutor. Tutoring went underground and charged more for the legal risk, and in 2000 the Constitutional Court struck the ban down. Sam's POV says that between 1979 and 2008 the share of students in hagwons rose from 6% to 75%. A 10 p.m. curfew in Seoul in 2009 led hagwons to rebrand as study libraries or teach on chartered buses. In 2023 the government removed the "killer questions" that sit outside the school curriculum and favour hagwon students, and hagwons sold the uncertainty of the new format back to parents at higher prices. Sam's POV also says the school-age population fell 14.5% over three years and spending per student rose 43.7%.

## Who wins and what is left over

The system now protects the already privileged more than it lifts anyone up. One study cited in the video found that 36% of Korea's richest students reach the top universities, against 9% of the poorest. The three SKY universities admit about 2% of students and supply nearly half of top CEOs and senior officials. The video adds that in China, urban students are five times more likely than rural ones to reach a top university.

A second loop follows. Peter Turchin calls it elite overproduction, meaning a society trains more qualified people than it has elite posts. The video says China graduated a record 12.7 million students in 2026, and that more than a fifth of delivery drivers on its two largest platforms hold a degree, and youth slang has moved from involution to "lying flat" to "let it rot." The same supply-and-demand problem is building in the West, where degrees have multiplied faster than graduate jobs.
