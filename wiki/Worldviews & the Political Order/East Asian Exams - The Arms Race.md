# East Asian Exams - The Arms Race

In China, South Korea, Japan, Taiwan and Singapore, one national exam at the end of school decides which university a student enters, and the university's name then follows the person into hiring and pay for life. The exam ranks students against each other, so every family that pays for extra tutoring forces every other family to pay too. That contest explains the region's tutoring bills, its birth rates and its surplus of graduates, and it settles whether the fix is a ban on tutoring or a different way of picking people.

## Takeaways

- The exam ranks students against each other, with no fixed bar.
- One family's tutoring forces every other family to pay to hold rank.
- All would gain if all stopped, and none can stop alone.
- Tutoring bans in Korea and China failed.
- Families stop at one child, and Korea's fertility rate is under 1.
- The exam sorts by family income more than ability.
- There are more graduates than jobs that need a degree.

## How it works

China's civil service exam dates from 605 AD, and the current university entrance exam, the gaokao, came back in 1977 after an 11-year ban. South Korea copied the model with its Suneung, and Japan with a national test. Places at the top universities are fixed, so a score matters only relative to the other scores. Private tutoring raises a child's rank, and once some families pay for it, the rest must pay to stay where they were.

- In 1977, 5.4% of 5.7 million gaokao candidates passed.
- Korean cram schools are called hagwons.
- Korea: 6% of students in hagwons in 1979, over 80% in 2024.
- Korean families spent a record 29.2 trillion won on tutoring in 2024.
- Hagwons now hold entrance exams for children aged 4.
- Kindergarten tests start at 20 months.
- Nearly half of Korean children under 6 attend a hagwon.
- The school-age population fell 14.5% in three years.
- Spending per student rose 43.7% over the same three years.

```
fixed places, scores ranked against each other
        |
one family pays for tutoring --> rivals pay too
        |                           |
cost per child rises          rank unchanged
        |
one child, not two  -->  fertility 0.7 to 0.8
        |
degree for nearly everyone  -->  more graduates
                                 than graduate jobs
```

## The cost and the birth rate

Raising a Korean child to 18 costs about $275,000, which is 7.8 times income per person, against 4.1 times in the US, and about a third of it goes to tutoring. A child's schooling has to be funded in full or the child loses rank, and a second child halves what each child gets. So families stop at one, and Korea's birth rate is low mainly because families do not go back for a second child. One estimate puts Korean fertility 28% higher without the status competition and the education spending.

- Korea's fertility rate: 0.72 in 2023, 0.75 in 2024, 0.80 in 2025.
- The 2025 rise comes from a larger cohort and delayed weddings.
- It is expected to fade after about 2028.
- Korea's population is projected at 22 million by 2100, from 52 million.
- Taiwan: over 54,000 cram schools, fertility 0.695.
- Singapore: about 85% of upper-primary pupils tutored, fertility 0.87.

## Sorting by family income

The richest Korean households spend nearly seven times what the poorest spend on tutoring, and the poorest fifth spend about as much on cram schools as on food. The result is that 36% of Korea's richest students reach the top universities, against 9% of the poorest. Korea's three top universities, known together as SKY, take a few percent of students and supply nearly half of senior government officials and a large share of top CEOs. Samsung sets an internal grade by university, so pay at 40 traces back to the exam at 18.

- Urban Chinese students reach top universities five times as often as rural.
- In some Chinese provinces the odds are about 1 in 2,000.

## Too many graduates

About 70% of Koreans go to university, the highest rate in the world, and the degree pays 20% more, against about 40% in most developed countries. China graduated 12.22 million students in 2025 and about 12.7 million in 2026, into an economy without jobs for them. Peter Turchin calls a society that trains more people for elite positions than it has positions a case of elite overproduction. The people left out turn against the society.

- China's youth unemployment, ages 16 to 24: 21.3% in June 2023.
- China paused the figure, then resumed with a new measure.
- The new measure gave 18.9% in August 2025.
- At least 70,000 Chinese delivery drivers hold master's degrees.
- 77.5% of Chinese undergraduates planned postgraduate study in 2024.
- Chinese civil service applicants per post: 40 in 2021, 98 in 2026.
- 504,000 young Koreans were "resting" in February 2024.
- Involution means everyone working harder for the same fixed reward.
- Chinese youth named their response: involution, lying flat, letting it rot.
- Lying flat was named in 2021, letting it rot in 2022.
- In 2023 the degree became Kong Yiji's gown, a failed scholar's robe.

## Why bans fail

A ban removes the tutoring and leaves the ranking. As long as places are fixed and scores are ranked, the contest returns in another form. Parents defend the exam, because without it they expect the best places to go to the best-connected. In China the exam has been the accepted idea of who deserves power for 1,400 years.

- Korea banned private tutoring in 1980, and tutoring went underground.
- Korea's Constitutional Court struck the ban down in 2000.
- A 10 p.m. hagwon curfew came in 2009.
- Exam questions only tutoring prepared for were removed in 2023.
- China banned for-profit tutoring in 2021, and 700,000 centres shut.
- Tutors came back disguised as housekeepers.
- 70% to 80% of Chinese high schoolers still take extra classes.
- Poor families lost their cheapest way to keep up.

## What this desk holds

Merit means personal ability. The exam may once have measured it, and now it measures how much a family paid for exam practice. The system survives because it is how things have always been done, and it cannot be repaired from inside, since each fix leaves the ranking in place. The replacement is judging people on evidence of exceptional ability, meaning work others can see, which the internet now makes visible for almost anyone.

- Tesla and SpaceX already hire on that evidence and a short conversation.
- Replacing the exam would take a change in the society.
- It may take the end of the tradition.
- The replacement would probably be Western.
