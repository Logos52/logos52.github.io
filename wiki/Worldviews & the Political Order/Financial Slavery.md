# Financial Slavery

Some modern lending works like legal slavery, because the lender profits when the borrower fails and relies on the state to stop the borrower from getting out. The argument is usually put as satire, meant to help people spot and avoid predatory loans. Honest finance runs the other way, since the lender shares the borrower's risk and wins only when the borrower succeeds. Once a lender removes that risk, the goal shifts from repayment to keeping the borrower paying compound interest for life.

Usury breaks the link between lender and borrower, because a lender who expects the borrower to fail profits from the failure through interest on the interest. The arrangement holds only when the state stops the borrower from walking away, as with student debt that cannot be cleared through bankruptcy. A whole country can fall into the same trap when it borrows every year to cover its gap and its interest, and a central bank deepens the trap by printing the money it lends. In the United States the national debt stood at $39 trillion in April 2026, and interest alone now costs more than the military.

## Honest and dishonest lending

An honest investor puts money into a company because he wants it to succeed, and if it fails he loses too. An honest loan follows the same rule, because the lender hopes the client prospers enough to pay him back with interest. Usury breaks that link. A lender who expects the borrower to fail profits from the failure, since the borrower ends up paying far more than he borrowed through interest on the interest and never escapes.

The arrangement only holds if the borrower cannot walk away, which is where the state comes in. Dishonest finance needs the power to coerce, and the loan contract gives that power legal cover. Student loans show how it works. The state ruled that because an education cannot be handed back, student debt cannot be cleared through bankruptcy, so a borrower who falls behind stays bound to it. The state also floods universities with easy loans to 17-year-olds, so colleges have no reason to cut prices. When student lending was private, banks lent only to students likely to repay, which kept colleges affordable.

Private firms profit from failure without the state's help as well. Buy now, pay later companies let people finance even a fast-food order without interest until they miss a payment, at which point late fees begin. Their business depends on getting people to spend more than they have, so they profit from the bad decisions they make easy.

## The same trap for a nation

A whole country can fall into a debt trap when its politicians spend more than they collect in taxes and borrow every year to cover the gap and the growing interest. A central bank deepens the trap. It creates money, buys the government's bonds with it and is repaid out of taxes, so citizens pay interest on money that was printed and that shrank the value of their own savings. Because the government cannot repay, it borrows more printed money to pay off the old loans, and the debt keeps growing. The people who gain are the state's bureaucracy, the central bank's staff and the rich, who hold assets such as housing that keep their value as money loses it. Everyone else takes on larger debts to afford the same things.

The American numbers show the scale. The national debt stood at $39 trillion in April 2026 and grows by roughly 7.5 billion dollars a day. Interest alone now costs more than $1 trillion a year, which is more than the country spends on its military. Each household carries a share of about 289,000 dollars of that debt, which it never agreed to borrow. Young adults carry their own load as well, since Americans aged 18 to 29 together owe $1.12 trillion. That combined burden invites an uncomfortable comparison, because slaves in ancient Egypt paid a 20% tax on their harvest.

## Open questions

- How does a lender who profits from failure think about a borrower differently from one who shares the risk?
- Why does an easy loan look like a sensible choice to a 17-year-old?
- How does a flood of easy student loans feed rising tuition?
- How does borrowing printed money to repay old debt feed itself over time?
