# What is the virtue of a proportionate response?

# What is the virtue of a proportionate response?

A proportionate response answers an attack with a strike of similar size, such as a transmitter for a downed plane. The question in the title is a line from The West Wing in 1999, and in June 2026 Donald Trump reposted a clip of that scene after his own military called its strikes on Iran proportional. Proportion has no moral virtue of its own, and its real case rests on what the alternative costs.

## Takeaways

- The law of war never requires answering blow for blow.
- A predictable response deters nothing, because the enemy plans around it.
- A disproportionate strike costs allies.
- It spends the biggest threat a state holds in reserve.
- It sets a precedent that returns against the state's own soldiers.
- Enemies adapt to attempts at dominance, often by hiding among civilians.

## The scene

President Bartlet asks what the virtue of a proportional response is. His top general, the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, answers: "It isn't virtuous, Mr. President. It's all there is, sir." Bartlet is then shown the disproportionate option, the bombing of Damascus airport with thousands of civilians dead, and he orders the proportional strikes instead. The price persuades him, and no moral argument is made.

- The planned targets were emptied for four days before the strikes.
- The enemy had "factored in" the expected response.
- The price of the big option: no allies, no coalition, no Congress.
- "A five thousand dollar punishment for a fifty buck crime."

## What the law says

The legal rule and the popular idea differ. Under the 1977 Additional Protocol to the Geneva Conventions, an attack is banned when the expected harm to civilians is excessive against the concrete and direct military advantage. The rule weighs civilian harm against military gain, and nothing in it matches a transmitter with a transmitter. A hospital or school used for military purposes loses its protection, and the side that put its forces there carries the blame.

- Arguments against "eye for an eye" symmetry leave the legal rule untouched.
- The legal test gets slower where the enemy hides among civilians.
- Perfidy is narrow: faking protected status to kill or capture.

## Why deterrence fails

A response the enemy can work out in advance is a price, and the enemy budgets for it. Where the rules of engagement are known and stable, an opponent reorganises around them. At that point the rule stops deterring and starts handing the enemy an advantage. This is the strongest ground for disproportion.

- The opponent drops uniforms.
- It puts weapons under protected sites.
- It times its attacks to the rule.

## What still holds

Three reasons for proportion survive, and each is a cost: lost allies, a spent threat and harm to captured soldiers. A state that needs allies loses them with a massacre. A maximum response uses up the maximum threat, and afterwards the state can only repeat itself or stop. A state that announces "a head for an eye" also tells the enemy how to treat its captured soldiers.

- RAND's 2008 study found attempts at dominance provoke asymmetric responses.
- Human shielding is one of the predicted responses.
- The coalition cost binds most where a state is weakest.
- The reciprocity cost binds most against states, less against irregular forces.

## Ratios as policy

Fixed ratios of retaliation have been official policy before. A German order of 16 September 1941 set 50 to 100 executions for each German soldier killed, and its signer, Wilhelm Keitel, was convicted at Nuremberg. In 2008 Gadi Eisenkot, head of Israel's Northern Command, promised disproportionate force on any village that fired on Israel. In July 2026 an Iranian member of parliament, Hamid Rasaei, put the ratio plainly: "an eye for an eye" is wrong because it does not deter, and "a head for an eye" is right because it does.

| Response | What follows |
| --- | --- |
| Proportional | Priced in by the enemy, allies kept, threat kept |
| Disproportional | Deters once, allies lost, threat spent |
| Fixed ratio | The 1941 order, prosecuted at Nuremberg |
