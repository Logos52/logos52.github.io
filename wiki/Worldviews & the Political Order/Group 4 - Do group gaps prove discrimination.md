# Do group gaps prove discrimination

Four arguments examine whether an outcome gap between groups proves discrimination, with the opening argument defining the inference that the remaining three examine. Even within one channel, explanations diverge: one account points to genetic distance between long-isolated groups, while another blames racial fatigue on societal fatherlessness rather than race.

## Racial Egalitarianism - The Disparity Inference

In this debate, the disparity inference jumps from an outcome gap between groups straight to discrimination as its cause. This argument holds that the step works only if all racial groups are identical underneath (racial egalitarianism), and ranks six common defenses of that premise. Because the argument concerns group averages, exceptions at the individual level leave the group-level argument untouched.

## Two answers rejected

Mainstream leftism treats all groups and cultures as equal, attributing any income or crime gap solely to discrimination. Mainstream conservatism replies that culture explains the gaps, implying any group can adopt a working culture and assimilate anywhere. Both answers are rejected: discrimination acts alongside other causes, and a culture reflects the people who create and keep it. Avoiding group differences turns egalitarianism into an unexamined dogma.

## The six defenses

1. Denying any genetic link to intelligence: twin and adoption studies are cited for heritability, and evolutionary selection implies groups can diverge in cognitive traits, illustrated by comparing Border Collies and Chihuahuas within a single species.
2. Citing 99.9% genetic similarity: raw overlap ignores functional weight, since humans share roughly half their genes with bananas and 90% of DNA sequence with cats, while dogs and gray wolves share 99.9% after diverging 20,000 to 30,000 years ago yet differ sharply.
3. Emphasizing within-group variation: total variance hides single high-impact variants concentrated by population, such as malaria-protective Duffy-negative alleles across roughly 95 to 100% of West and Central Africa, Tibetan EPAS1 altitude alleles, and dry-earwax ABCC11 alleles in 80 to 95% of East Asia.
4. Calling race a social construct: Naming a pattern does not make it imaginary, any more than building named concepts around the sun or molecules makes them invented.
5. "IQ tests are unreliable." Despite measurement flaws, IQ remains the strongest available predictor of life outcomes, stronger than family wealth, and no society exists where higher IQ stops being an advantage.
6. "Culture explains all disparities." This is treated as an escape hatch for critics who reject the discrimination story yet fear the racist label: genes came before culture, both reinforce each other, and a culture built by one people need not work for every other.

## Genetics and Unequal Societies - The Distance Claim

Another argument claims genetic differences across human populations produce distinct social orders, meaning no single political system suits all peoples. To discuss group-level biology without naming real populations, it uses fictional stand-ins (circles, squares, and triangles).

The causal sequence moves from geographic isolation through genetic distance and nervous system variation to the political order a group sustains.

## Geography and distance

In the hypothetical setup, three groups live apart, with a large desert isolating the squares for nearly 40,000 years, longer than the 15,000 to 30,000 years estimated for domestic dogs to diverge as a distinct species. Populations that stay isolated with minimal mixing keep adapting to local conditions.

Genetic distance is framed via the fixation index (a measure of matching allele frequencies between populations). Below 0.15 marks moderate or low difference (like closely related North American gray wolf subspecies), whereas above 0.15 marks strongly differentiated organisms, with domestic dogs and gray wolves placed around 0.17. The hypothetical groups are assigned 0.15 (circles and squares), 0.19 (triangles and squares), and 0.11 (circles and triangles), placing triangles and squares further apart than dogs and wolves. No empirical source is supplied for those figures.

## From genes to societies

Rejecting the claim that group differences are only skin deep, this account cites an unnamed 2011 study reporting small differentiation in skin-related genes and high differentiation in nervous-system genes. From there it infers that shared nervous systems shape shared intellect, personality, and temperament, which in turn shape cohesive societies. Individualistic, decentralized orders are mapped to circles, collectivist centralized orders to triangles, and little comparable civilization to squares, leading to the view that one political template cannot fit all peoples.

## The Civil Rights Act - Freedom of Association

Under freedom of association, citizens choose companions and business counterparts without state coercion. On this view, the Civil Rights Act of 1964 rightly ended Jim Crow laws that forced private owners to segregate, yet wrongly replaced them with laws forcing owners to integrate.

On that standard, forcing integration repeats the same breach of liberty in reverse, earning a one out of 10 score.

## Title II and business owners

Economists have long argued that competition would erode segregation because turning away customers for no reason forfeits revenue to rivals. Defenders of the Act reply that racist exclusions persisted across large regions even without Jim Crow. Both observations are reconciled by questioning whether owners were acting irrationally: if a group were statistically more likely to skip bills, steal, or harass staff, avoiding that risk and security cost would affect honest customers from the same group as well. To the objection that owners merely bowed to racist patrons, the same question is pushed back one step to why those patrons felt that way.

## Title VII and jobs

Title VII banned hiring discrimination, which this critique considers worse than Title II because an employer's hiring motive is hard to prove when any candidate can fall short on merit. Later enforcement shifted to disparate outcomes, comparing protected groups and treating statistical gaps as proof of discrimination, effectively mandating blank-slate assumptions by law.

## Racial Fatigue

"Black fatigue" is described here as exhaustion with a culture seen as hostile and hypocritical rather than anger at any one crime. Filed under racial fatigue, the reply answers a critic who had called an earlier talk racist yet wound up conceding its underlying claims.

Ultimately, the cause is placed outside race itself: the fatigue stems from societal fatherlessness rather than skin color.

## The three claims

The reply restates three claims from the earlier talk:

1. Homicide rates among black Americans are very high.
2. Fatherlessness contributes directly to that homicide rate.
3. Welfare rules that paid out only when no father lived in the home encouraged fatherlessness.

Because the critic conceded the second and third points, the reply asks what was actually refuted.

## How the figures are argued

The comparison tracks changes over time rather than across racial groups, comparing modern black America with black America before the civil rights era, when legal discrimination was far worse. Black male homicide victimization (used as a proxy for offending) stood near 42 per 100,000 around 1960, nearly doubled by 1970, and sits near 50 per 100,000 today. Before civil rights, roughly 78% of black children grew up with two parents, versus under half today, and 70 to 78% are born outside marriage.

The shift is blamed on the War on Poverty, as post-civil-rights federal welfare programs courted black voters while tied to a father's absence from the home, effectively penalizing two-parent households.
