# Women's Suffrage - The Substitute Husband

Extending the franchise changed what the electorate wants, and the change has a direction. The argument here is not that the new voters vote badly in some general sense but that they vote for a specific substitution — the state assuming the functions a household provider once performed — and that the substitution is structurally available in one direction only.

Two mechanisms are proposed for the pattern, and one of them is considerably better supported than the other.

## The prediction

The clearest evidence that the pattern is structural rather than incidental is that it was described before it happened. A Greek comedy from the fourth century BC stages women taking control of the assembly and enacting a programme: communal provision, redistribution to eliminate need, hostility to private property, and the dissolution of exclusive pairings in favour of collective arrangement.

It was written as farce and it names, in order, the policy cluster that modern voting data shows. Contemporary surveys place women eight to fifteen points ahead of men in support for expanding transfer programmes already large by any historical standard.

A description that old cannot be an artefact of the data it matches — whatever produced it was not reading current polling — which is what makes it worth more here than another contemporary survey.

## The substitution

The state can perform the functions of a provider and a protector. It can supply income, housing, medical care, and physical security, imperfectly and at scale.

That makes it a viable substitute for one half of a household arrangement, and a voter for whom it is a viable substitute is a voter with a rational interest in expanding it. The interest is strongest where the alternative is least available — among the unpartnered — and weakest among women whose own household already supplies what the state would.

**The asymmetry is what makes this specific rather than general.** The state cannot substitute for what a wife provides a husband. There is no programme that delivers companionship, a household, or children, and no expansion of provision creates one. So the incentive runs one way: expanding the state substitutes for a male role and does not substitute for a female one, which means the political coalition it creates has no mirror image.

From that follows the sharpest claim on the page. A platform that draws disproportionate support from unpartnered women has an incentive to keep them unpartnered, because partnership reduces the demand for the substitute. Nobody need intend this for it to operate; incentives of that kind work through what a coalition finds itself rewarded for, not through anyone's plan.

**What this does not establish.** That the incentive exists follows from the asymmetry above. That it has produced the observed decline in family formation is a causal claim about a period with many other things happening in it, and the material does not isolate it. The mechanism is available; its share of the outcome is not measured here. A sister page traces a schooling chain to the same household result, and neither isolate is claimed.

## The agreeableness account

The second mechanism is the better-evidenced one and it is not about welfare at all.

Women score higher on agreeableness on average, and one consequence is greater sensitivity to perceived consensus. A voter with that disposition tracks the dominant institutional position rather than reasoning independently toward a position that would put them at odds with it.

The account makes a prediction that can be checked against the record, and it holds: when the dominant institutions were conservative, women voted more conservatively than men. The gap ran the other way then and turned over when the institutions did, which is not what a fixed political preference would do.

That is a considerably stronger finding than a claim about which direction women vote, because it explains both directions with one mechanism. What it identifies is not a political preference but an amplifier — a bloc that increases the weight of whatever the prevailing institutional view happens to be.

The consequence is about scrutiny rather than about policy. A large body of voters tracking consensus makes the consensus harder to dislodge, reduces the electoral cost of institutional capture, and rewards dogmatism over argument. None of that depends on which direction the amplification runs.

## The verdict

On the agreeableness account, the extension is largely redundant — an amplifier attached to institutions, adding weight to positions that already held it. Where households are intact the redundancy holds for a second reason: aligned spouses vote the same way, and the additional vote changes no outcome.

On the substitution account, it is worse than redundant, because it creates a coalition whose interest runs against the household formation that produces the next electorate.

The two accounts are compatible, and the amplification account does not stand on its own: an amplifier is only dangerous in the direction it amplifies, and the direction is set by institutions that have their own reasons for the settlement.

What is not in dispute is the joint outcome. Family formation is at a recorded low, transfer spending at a recorded high, reported loneliness rising in both sexes, and the range of institutionally acceptable opinion narrower than it was. Whether the franchise caused that or merely coincided with it is the question the material answers and does not settle.

## Open questions

If the amplification account is correct, the effect reverses whenever institutions do. What would that predict about a period in which institutional consensus moved right?

Where an incentive exists but its share of an outcome is unmeasured, what evidence would separate it from the other changes of the same period?
