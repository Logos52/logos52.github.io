# Britain - Poorer Than Mississippi

In April 2026 a London think tank, the Institute of Economic Affairs, asked 3,000 British voters where Britain would rank on income per person if it were an American state; the average guess was seventh, and the answer was last, below Mississippi. The survey matters because it shows how a country's economy can keep growing in total while each person in it gets poorer, and it names the policies that did it: refused building permission, high tax on the next pound earned, expensive energy, welfare that penalises taking a job, and immigration with no matching housing.

## Core takeaways

- Britain's total output is still the fifth largest in the world; output per person is the number that has fallen, and it is the one that sets what a worker takes home.
- Output per hour worked grew 0.6% a year from 2008 to 2024, against 1.9% in America, because firms invest less of the economy's output than any other large wealthy country.
- Housing is short because building permission is refused: only 8.7% of Britain's land is built on, and since 1947 every new building has needed its own approval from the local council.
- Both tax and welfare take most of the next pound earned at the points where people decide whether to work more, so many decide not to.
- People with the means to leave are leaving: 16,500 millionaires in 2025 and about 250,000 citizens in 2024, three quarters of them under 35.
- Government borrows the difference between what it spends and what it taxes: debt is £102,000 per household and interest costs £111 billion a year.
- In the same survey 77% wanted cheaper energy, 72% lower taxes on workers, and 59% easier planning.

## How it works

- Investment stopped.
  - Firms put 18.9% of output into new plant and equipment in 2024, the lowest in the G7, the group of seven large wealthy economies.
  - What Britain invents is sold abroad: the chip designer Arm went to Japan in 2016 and listed in New York in 2023; DeepMind went to Google in 2014; a £3.8 billion battery plant sold for £8.6 million in 2023.
  - Industrial electricity costs about four times the American price, so factories close: Britain's last blast furnace in 2024, Scotland's last oil refinery in 2025.
- Building stopped.
  - Every new building can be blocked by its neighbours through the council, so an average house costs 7.6 times an average salary, and 25 times in London.
  - Home ownership among people aged 25 to 34 fell from 55% in 1990 to 31% in 2023.
  - A city in Texas, Dallas-Fort Worth, permitted about as many homes in 2024 as the whole of Britain built.
- Extra work stopped paying.
  - Between £100,000 and £125,140 a worker's tax-free allowance, £12,570, is withdrawn, so the rate on each extra pound is 62%, higher than the rate on a £400,000 income.
  - A single parent in council housing who moves from a £20,000 job to a £30,000 job loses most of the extra £10,000 to withdrawn benefits and tax; the rate can pass 80%.
  - A council flat let at £120 a week where a private landlord would charge £500 is a £20,000-a-year subsidy tied to one address, so a job in another city means giving it up. Only 6% of the 4.3 million social homes change hands in a year.
  - 2.8 million people are on long-term sickness benefits, and working-age welfare now costs more than income tax raises.
- People arrived faster than homes.
  - Net immigration, arrivals minus departures, was 944,000 in the year to March 2023 and 2.55 million from 2021 to 2024, 3.8% of the population.
  - Each arrival needs a home, a doctor and a school place; total output rose because there were more people, and output per person went sideways or down.
- The state borrows what tax does not cover.
  - Debt is 93% of output in early 2026, the highest since the early 1960s; among 36 advanced economies Britain has the fifth highest deficit and the third highest borrowing costs.
  - Interest is forecast at £137 billion a year by 2030, and the Office for Budget Responsibility, the government's fiscal watchdog, projects debt at 275% of output by the 2070s if nothing changes.

## The timeline

- 1945: second largest economy, debt at 250% of output. Coal, rail, steel, power, gas and the Bank of England nationalised; the National Health Service founded in 1948; 126,000 council homes a year built to 1980.
- 1950s and 1960s: West Germany grew 8% a year and Japan 10%; Britain grew 2.5% and was 14th on income per person by 1970.
- 1974 to 1979: a three-day week during a coal strike, an IMF loan and 25% inflation in 1976, a winter of strikes.
- 1979 to 1990: the nationalised industries sold, the top tax rate cut from 83% to 40%, the City deregulated in 1986. The coal, steel and shipbuilding towns never recovered.
- 1997 to 2008: house prices quadrupled, borders opened to new EU members in 2004, spending rose from 35% to 42% of output. In 2008 the state took over two large banks at a cost of £137 billion.
- 2008 to 2024: output per hour grew 0.6% a year and fell 0.5% over the five years to 2024.
- 2024 to 2026: two budgets added about £51 billion of tax; the highest emigration since 1964; a growth forecast of 0.8% for 2026, the sharpest cut in the G7.

## What the number hides

The comparison with Mississippi is a per-person figure, and a forecast to 2040 gives both a total and a per-person figure. Total output is expected to grow from about $4 trillion to $6.8 trillion, which keeps Britain fifth in the world, while the per-person rank slides from 19th into the low 20s. A country can stay large in total while each household in it earns less in real terms than its parents did at the same age. Economists call such a country a poor big country.
