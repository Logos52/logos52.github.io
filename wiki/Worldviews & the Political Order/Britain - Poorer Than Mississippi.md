# Britain - Poorer Than Mississippi

In April 2026 the Institute of Economic Affairs, a London think tank, asked 3,000 British voters where Britain would rank on income per person if it were an American state. The average guess was seventh, and the answer was last, below Mississippi. Britain's economy keeps growing in total while each person in it gets poorer, and the causes are policies: refused building permission, high tax on the next pound earned, expensive energy, welfare that penalises taking a job, and immigration with no matching housing.

- Britain's total output is fifth in the world.
- Output per person has fallen, and it sets take-home pay.
- Firms invest less than in any other large wealthy country.
- Only 8.7% of Britain's land is built on.
- Tax and welfare take most of the next pound earned.
- The young and the rich are leaving.
- Debt is £102,000 per household, with £111 billion a year in interest.

## How it works

Output per hour worked grew 0.6% a year from 2008 to 2024, against 1.9% in America, and wages are paid out of output per hour. Firms put 18.9% of output into new plant and equipment in 2024, the lowest share in the G7, the group of seven large wealthy economies. Industrial electricity costs about four times the American price, so factories close, and what Britain invents gets sold abroad. The companies that stay face a tax system that takes most of any extra pound a worker earns.

- Arm, the chip designer, went to Japan in 2016.
- DeepMind went to Google in 2014.
- A £3.8 billion battery plant sold for £8.6 million in 2023.
- Britain's last blast furnace closed in 2024.
- Scotland's last oil refinery closed in 2025.

In the same April 2026 survey, 77% of voters wanted cheaper energy and 59% wanted easier planning.

## Housing

Since 1947 every new building in Britain has needed its own approval from the local council, and neighbours can block it there. An average house now costs 7.6 times an average salary, and 25 times in London. Home ownership among people aged 25 to 34 fell from 55% in 1990 to 31% in 2023. Dallas-Fort Worth, one city region in Texas, permitted about as many homes in 2024 as the whole of Britain built.

- Homes are scarce because councils refuse permission.
- Scarce homes push up rents and prices.

## Work that does not pay

Between £100,000 and £125,140 a worker loses the £12,570 tax-free allowance, so each extra pound in that band is taxed at 62%, a higher rate than a £400,000 income pays. At the bottom, a single parent in council housing who moves from a £20,000 job to a £30,000 job loses most of the extra £10,000 to tax and withdrawn benefits, and the rate can pass 80%. A council flat let at £120 a week, where a private landlord would charge £500, is a £20,000 a year subsidy tied to one address. Taking a job in another city means giving it up.

- Only 6% of 4.3 million social homes change hands in a year.
- 2.8 million people are on long-term sickness benefits.
- Working-age welfare costs more than income tax raises.

## People and debt

Net immigration, arrivals minus departures, was 944,000 in the year to March 2023, and 2.55 million from 2021 to 2024, which is 3.8% of the population. Each arrival needs a home, a doctor and a school place. Total output rose because there were more people, while output per person went sideways or down. At the same time the people with the means to leave are leaving, and the state borrows what tax does not cover.

- 16,500 millionaires left in 2025.
- About 250,000 citizens left in 2024, three quarters of them under 35.
- Debt is 93% of output in early 2026.
- That is the highest share since the early 1960s.
- Britain has the fifth highest deficit of 36 advanced economies.
- It has the third highest borrowing costs of the same 36.
- Interest is forecast at £137 billion a year by 2030.

The Office for Budget Responsibility, the official forecaster, projects debt at 275% of output by the 2070s.

## The timeline

Britain came out of the Second World War as the second largest economy in the world, with debt at 250% of output, and it nationalised its main industries. It fell behind its rivals in the 1950s and 1960s. It sold the industries in the 1980s and has grown slowly since 2008. The towns that lived on coal, steel and shipbuilding never recovered from the sales.

- 1945: coal, rail, steel, power, gas and the Bank of England nationalised.
- 1948: the National Health Service founded.
- To 1980: 126,000 council homes built a year.
- 1950s to 1960s: yearly growth 8% West Germany, 10% Japan, 2.5% Britain.
- 1970: Britain 14th on income per person.
- 1974 to 1979: a three-day week and 25% inflation.
- 1974 to 1979: a loan from the International Monetary Fund.
- 1974 to 1979: a winter of strikes.
- 1979 to 1990: industries sold, top tax rate 83% down to 40%.
- 1997 to 2008: house prices quadrupled.
- 1997 to 2008: state spending rose from 35% to 42% of output.
- 2004: borders opened to new EU members.
- 2008: the state took over two large banks for £137 billion.
- 2024 to 2026: two budgets added about £51 billion of tax.
- 2026: a 0.8% growth forecast, the sharpest cut in the G7.

## What the total hides

A forecast to 2040 has Britain's total output growing from about $4 trillion to $6.8 trillion, which keeps it fifth in the world. Over the same years its rank on output per person slides from 19th into the low 20s. Economists call such a country a poor big country. It is large in total, and its households earn less in real terms than their parents did at the same age.
