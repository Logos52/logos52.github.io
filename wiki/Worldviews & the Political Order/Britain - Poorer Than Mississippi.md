# Britain - Poorer Than Mississippi

A [Front Page video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ITFz2aDjKxY) argues that Britain is a large economy with a falling place on income per person, and the IEA poll it builds on suggests Britons, on average, overestimate where Britain stands. A poll for the Institute of Economic Affairs, a London think tank, asked UK voters where Britain would rank among US states on GDP per person. On average they put it 7th, and the [IEA reports](https://iea.org.uk/media/brits-want-growth-and-back-less-red-tape-cheaper-energy-and-lower-taxes-to-get-it-finds-landmark-iea-research/) using IMF data, that it actually comes last, below every one of the 50 states. The video argues that the gap comes from slow, compounding choices, with each refused planning permission, tax rise, closed factory and departing millionaire or young doctor adding to it.

## The poll

The IEA's poll was run by Freshwater Strategy with 3,001 UK voters on 13 to 18 January 2026 and published on 13 April 2026. It found that 87% think the UK should focus more on growth and 9% think the country is already wealthy enough. The policies people most often said would grow the economy were cutting energy costs at 77%, cutting taxes on workers at 72%, cutting taxes on business at 66% and easing planning at 59%. Understanding was weak, though. Asked what GDP growth means, 32% did not know or were unsure, and more people expected the government to benefit from growth than themselves. When told the true ranking, 27% said they felt shocked.

## How the video tells the decline

Front Page starts in 1945, when, on its account, Britain left the war as the second largest economy and also bankrupt. It says the post-war state nationalised whole industries and founded the National Health Service, and that living standards rose for about 20 years while West Germany and Japan grew faster. By the video's figures, Britain had slipped to 14th place worldwide in income per person by 1970. It describes the strikes of the 1970s and an IMF loan in 1976. The Thatcher reforms that followed, it says, revived growth, while the coal, steel and shipbuilding towns never recovered. It argues that finance in London took the place of making things, and that the 2008 crash exposed this. Since then, it says, productivity per hour has barely grown.

## The choices the video blames

The video names four areas, and in each it gives a reason the policy holds Britain back.

1. On building, the video says the shortage is permission to build, with land not the problem, because under the 1947 planning system every new building needs a council's approval and neighbours can block it. It contrasts this with Austin, Texas, which it says added 120,000 homes in nine years and saw median rents fall.
2. On tax, it says a frozen personal allowance pulls more workers into higher rates. It also describes a band between £100,000 and £125,140 a year where losing the allowance creates an effective marginal rate of 62%. Its objection is that the rate falls on the mid-career professionals a country most wants to keep. It reports 16,500 millionaires leaving in 2025, and young skilled emigrants such as doctors, who it says earn far more in Australia. It also gives 252,000 British citizens emigrating in a year. The [ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/populationandmigration/internationalmigration/bulletins/longterminternationalmigrationprovisional/yearendingjune2025) records the same figure for the year to June 2025, though the [ONS also says](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/populationandmigration/internationalmigration/articles/improvinglongterminternationalmigrationstatisticsupdatingourmethodsandestimates/november2025) its new method revised British emigration sharply upward, from 82,000 under the old method to 283,000 under the new one in its comparison.
3. On energy, it says British industrial electricity costs roughly four times the American price, which it calls the difference between a factory surviving and shutting. It cites closures and cancelled investments, including the end of primary steelmaking at Port Talbot.
4. On welfare, the video argues the system has stopped rewarding work. Its example is a single parent whose raise from £20,000 to £30,000 loses most of the extra money to withdrawn benefits and tax. It says lifetime council tenancies tie people to one flat, so some turn down jobs elsewhere. The video says the state borrows to cover its spending and quotes the Office for Budget Responsibility, which [wrote](https://obr.uk/box/the-uks-fiscal-position-in-international-context/) that "the UK's fiscal position is increasingly vulnerable, by both historical and international standards."

The video also treats immigration as the most politically explosive number. It argues that total GDP grew because there were more people, while GDP per person went sideways or down. Its reason is that housing, health care and schools could not keep up with the arrivals.

## What both sources agree on

The video says nearly everyone running Britain agrees on the fix, which is more houses, cheaper energy, lower marginal tax on productive workers and welfare reform. It points to the IEA poll as proof that the public asks for the same supply-side changes, and argues that neither main party's leader is popular enough to carry them out. The IEA's own conclusion is that voters will back far-reaching reform once they see how it improves their lives and once they are shown that the state, not the market, caused the stagnation.

## Open questions

Where do the video's other UK figures come from? It names bodies such as Henley & Partners, the International Energy Agency and the Centre for Policy Studies in passing. Only the IEA poll, the ONS emigration figure and the OBR quote have been checked against official releases.

The video gives British emigration as 257,000 in one place and 252,000 in another. Its claims that this was the highest since 1964 and that three quarters were under 35 come from a Centre for Policy Studies comment, not from the ONS. It also says about 3,000 voters took the poll where the IEA says 3,001.
