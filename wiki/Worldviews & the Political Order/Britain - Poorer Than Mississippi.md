# Britain - Poorer Than Mississippi

## Reasoning

### Abstract

Mississippi is the state Americans have mocked for 150 years as the poorest corner of their country, and the United Kingdom now ranks below it on income per person. In April 2026 a London think tank asked 3,000 British voters where Britain would rank if it were an American state. The average guess was seventh, above Texas and Florida. The answer was fifty-first, behind every state including Alabama, West Virginia, Arkansas, and Mississippi. A century ago the country ruled a quarter of the planet. It invented the steam engine and modern banking and finished the Second World War as the second largest economy on earth. It is now poorer per person than the state whose median household income ranks last in America. Almost nobody in Britain knows it happened.

### Core takeaways

- Britain is still a big economy and a poor one at the same time. Total output keeps it fifth in the world while output per person has fallen below every American state. Most Britons do not know it.
- The country stopped investing, building, and making after 2008, so the output of an hour's work has barely grown in fifteen years while America's grew three times as fast.
- Taxes that bite hardest on the most productive workers, and energy at four times the American price, are driving out the skilled, the rich, and the companies, and the leavers are mostly young.
- Housing is the clearest choice. Britain has the land and refuses the permission, so every new building can be blocked by its neighbours, while Texas builds its way to lower rents.
- The welfare state has stopped rewarding work. A council flat cannot be taken to a new job, a pay rise can lose more than it gains, and the state borrows the difference.
- Everyone who runs the country agrees on the cure, and the public asks for it, but neither party has a leader popular enough to deliver the painful part.

### Essay

The finding surprises Britons because the country is still big. Total output keeps Britain the fifth largest economy in the world, and that is the number people read in the news. Income per person is the number that decides how much an ordinary worker takes home. On that number the country now sits below every American state, including the ones Americans have joked about for a century and a half. The gap between the country Britons think they live in and the one they do live in is the whole story.

The video traces how it happened as a chain of choices rather than one mistake. After the war the state took over industry and built a welfare state. For twenty years living standards rose while the rebuilt rivals grew three and four times faster. The 1970s broke the arrangement, with power cuts, a bailout, and rubbish in the streets. The reforms of the 1980s revived growth by turning the country into a financial centre, and left the industrial regions behind. The boom that followed was borrowed from lenders and from house prices. When that ended in 2008 the country simply stopped investing, building, and making. The output of an hour's work has barely moved since. Whatever a British university invents now gets sold abroad within a few years.

The tax system then charges people for staying. The rates that bite hardest fall on the mid-career doctors, engineers, and small-business owners a country most wants to keep. Factory electricity costs about four times what an American factory pays. So the rich leave for Dubai and Milan, the young doctors leave for Australia where the pay doubles and the registration takes weeks, and the companies follow, cancelling plants at home and building them in America. The country that invented modern steel-making can no longer make steel from ore.

Housing is the clearest choice of all, because the land exists. Only a small fraction of the country is built on. What is missing is permission. A planning law from 1947 makes every new building need individual approval from the local council, which means every new building can be blocked by its neighbours. A green belt and layers of environmental rules sit on top. A railway spends a fortune on a tunnel for bats, flats are refused for being five storeys tall, and a theme park dies over a spider. Texas simply builds, and its rents fall.

The welfare state has stopped rewarding work. A council flat is a large subsidy that exists only as long as the tenant never moves. So a job in another city is turned down. The flat passes down the family while the next family waits outside for decades. A parent who takes a better job can lose most of the raise to withdrawn benefits and higher tax. The rolls of people signed off sick grow every month. The state now pays more in working-age benefits than it collects in income tax, cannot cut them without riots or raise taxes without driving out the last high earners, and borrows the difference at some of the highest interest costs in the rich world.

Immigration on a scale never seen in peacetime was laid on top. Total output rose because there were more people spending, and output per person fell. Every arrival needs a home, a doctor, and a school place. A frozen planning system and a stalled economy cannot supply them. Most of the growth in the housing shortfall over the decade is the arrivals. The result at the ballot box is an insurgent party leading every poll and two main-party leaders too unpopular to carry a mandate for anything hard.

### Conclusion

The strange part is that the diagnosis is not contested. Everyone who runs modern Britain agrees on what the country needs. It needs far more houses, much cheaper energy, and lower rates on its most productive workers. It needs a planning system that says yes and a welfare state that stops paying people not to work. The public asks for exactly that in the polls. What is missing is a government popular enough to do the painful part. Without it the forecasts run one way, with debt climbing for decades and the rank on income per person sliding through the twenties, and the phrase economists already use for where the country is heading is a poor big country, enormous on paper and stagnant on the ground.

## The numbers

The numbers behind the ranking are not close. Public debt sits at 93% of national output, the highest since the early 1960s, about £2.9 trillion or £102,000 for every household, with interest alone costing £111 billion a year. Industrial electricity cost 26.63 pence a kilowatt-hour in 2026, the highest of the 25 wealthy countries tracked and about four times what American factories pay. In 2025, 16,500 millionaires left the country for good and took $91.8 billion with them, the largest outflow of rich people ever recorded anywhere, and 252,000 ordinary British citizens followed, three-quarters of them under 35. The IMF cut Britain's 2026 growth forecast to 0.8%, the sharpest cut in the G7. A British family doctor earns about half what the same doctor earns in Sydney, a British software engineer about a third of a San Francisco one, and a family home near London costs 25 times the local salary. Output per worker shrank 0.5% over five years.

In 1945 Britain was the second largest economy on the planet and bankrupt at the same time. The war had cost a quarter of the national wealth, run public debt to 250% of output, and killed 450,000 people. Bread and meat stayed rationed for years. Voters threw out the war leader and elected a quiet socialist. He nationalised coal, the railways, steel, electricity, gas, aviation, and eventually the central bank. In 1948 he founded a fully tax-funded health service that today employs 1.37 million people, the fifth largest employer on earth. For about twenty years the arrangement delivered. Slums were cleared, councils built 126,000 homes a year between 1946 and 1980, and wages rose.

Underneath, the rest of the world was catching up. West Germany grew 8% a year through the 1950s and Japan 10% a year through the 1960s, both rebuilt from rubble under occupation. Britain grew 2.5%. By 1970 the richest large economy in Europe in 1945 had fallen to fourteenth in the world on income per person, and the phrase for it was the sick man of Europe. The 1970s made the sick man sicker. A coal strike in 1974 put the country on a three-day working week, with offices shut at lunchtime, television dark at half past ten, and hospitals on candles. In 1976 the government ran out of money and took the largest emergency loan the IMF had ever made, $3.9 billion, as inflation hit 25%. In the winter of 1978 to 1979 the rubbish piled ten feet high in a London square, and the gravediggers struck and the bodies stacked up. A prime minister flying home from a Caribbean summit said he did not think there was mounting chaos. The next day's front page turned that into three words, crisis, what crisis. He lost four months later.

### The Thatcher turn and the two Britains

- The top income-tax rate fell from 83% to 60% and then to 40%.
- The City of London was deregulated in 1986 and became the largest financial centre in the world.
- The coal, steel, and shipbuilding towns never recovered, and by 1997 the country had split into a rich south-east and a north on the dole.

In May 1979 a grocer's daughter became the first woman prime minister and spent eleven years at war with the welfare state. She sold telecoms, gas, the airline, steel, and oil, then the electricity grid, the water companies, and the railways. She broke the coal miners in a year-long strike and cut the top income-tax rate from 83% to 60% and then 40%. In 1986 she deregulated the City of London, and it became the largest financial centre in the world. Through most of the 1980s it worked. Growth rose, unemployment fell, London boomed, and the sick man label faded.

The bill came due in the places the boom forgot. The coal towns of Yorkshire and South Wales, the steel towns of Sheffield and Scunthorpe, and the shipyards of Newcastle and Glasgow never recovered. The country that built the machinery of the Industrial Revolution traded its factories for a financial industry headquartered in one square mile. By 1997 the economy had split in two. London and the South East were rich, educated, global, and service-based. The industrial north, the mining valleys, the coastal towns, and the Midlands lived on what economists called structural unemployment and everyone else called the dole. The two Britains spoke with different accents, voted for different parties, and earned wildly different incomes, and the gap kept widening.

### Borrowed growth, then no growth

- Public spending rose from 35% of output in 1997 to 42% by 2008, and two of the four largest banks were nationalised in 2008 at a cost of £137 billion.
- Productivity grew 0.6% a year from 2008 to 2024 and fell 0.5% between 2019 and 2024. American productivity grew 1.9% a year.
- A £3.8 billion battery factory went bankrupt in 2023 and sold for £8.6 million.

The governments of 1997 to 2008 rode the financial boom for everything it was worth. Banks lent to everyone, and house prices doubled, tripled, and quadrupled. The borders opened to the new eastern members of the European Union in 2004, and public spending rose from 35% of output in 1997 to 42% by 2008. Every point of growth was borrowed, from foreign lenders, from Chinese savers, or from homeowners mortgaging houses about to fall in value. When an American investment bank went bankrupt in September 2008, the forty-year experiment in finance as a substitute for manufacturing unravelled in an afternoon. Two of the four largest banks were nationalised at a cost of £137 billion in cash and guarantees, and output fell 4.3% in 2009, the steepest fall since the 1930s.

The most important number then simply stopped moving. Productivity, the output of an average worker in an hour, grew 0.6% a year between 2008 and 2024, the worst in the G7 apart from Italy, and between 2019 and 2024 it fell 0.5%, the second worst five-year stretch since the series began. American productivity grew 1.9% a year over the same years, on technology and cheap shale energy. The reason, when it was finally pieced together, was that the country had stopped investing, building, and making. British firms spent 18.9% of output on investment in 2024, the lowest in the G7, and the trains still ran on Victorian track. Whenever a British university invented something valuable it was sold to an American buyer within five years. A Cambridge chip designer whose designs sit in nearly every smartphone was sold to a Japanese group for £24.3 billion in 2016 and listed its shares in New York in 2023. A British artificial-intelligence lab was sold to an American search company in 2014. A £3.8 billion battery factory the government had promised as the anchor of a green industrial future went bankrupt in January 2023 and sold for £8.6 million, about 0.2% of its planned cost.

The government has meanwhile made Britain more expensive to live, build, work, and be wealthy in than at almost any time in its modern history. Total tax will reach 37.7% of output by 2027, the highest since the war. The amount a worker can earn before paying any income tax was frozen at £12,570 in 2021 and the freeze now runs to 2031, so that as wages rise the freeze alone raises £55.5 billion a year by 2030, the largest single tax rise in sixty years, without a vote on it. By 2030, 24% of taxpayers will pay the higher 40% rate, up from 15% in 2021. Between £100,000 and £125,140 a year a quirk in the code withdraws the tax-free allowance and produces an effective marginal rate of 62%, on exactly the doctors, engineers, and small-business owners a country most wants to keep, so that a worker on £110,000 pays a higher marginal rate than one on £400,000. In October 2024 a new government added a £25 billion rise in employer payroll tax and raised capital-gains tax from 20% to 24%. It extended inheritance tax to pensions from 2027 and to farms, which brought tractors to Downing Street. The November 2025 budget added another £26 billion, and the fiscal watchdog called it the largest tax-raising parliament on record.

The people with the money and the mobility to respond have left. The 16,500 millionaires who gave up British residence in 2025 included a steel billionaire who had funded the governing party. The companies are leaving too, and energy is the reason most often given. Britain has the most expensive housing in the developed world relative to wages. The average house cost 7.6 times the average salary in 2025, nearly double the 3.9 that held through most of the last century. In one London borough it was 25.2 times. A first-time buyer with a standard deposit spends 36% of take-home pay on the mortgage alone. Home ownership peaked at 71% in 2003 and fell to 63% by 2024, and among 25 to 34 year olds, the family-forming generation, it fell from 55% in 1990 to 31% in 2023.

The cause is not land, since only 8.7% of Britain is built on. It is permission. A planning law from 1947 requires individual approval from the local council for every new building, so every new building can be blocked by its neighbours. 12.6% of England is green belt that cannot be built on at all. Successive governments have layered nitrate rules and other protections on top. In 2024 a London council rejected 449 flats because one building would stand five storeys tall. A new high-speed railway, already the most expensive in Europe, built a kilometre-long steel-mesh tunnel to protect a colony of bats at a cost of £100 million and 8,276 separate approvals. A nuclear plant in Wales waited years over seabirds, and a theme park east of London died in 2024 over rare jumping spiders. Austin, Texas added 120,000 homes in nine years, grew its housing stock 30%, and pushed rents down 16% to 22% from their peak. Texas permitted 133,549 homes in 2024, and Dallas and Fort Worth alone permitted 71,788, about what all of Britain builds in many years.

Behind the housing market sits social housing, 4.3 million council homes let at rents far below the market and home to one household in six. They were designed as a stepping stone and became a permanent address. About 45% of social tenants have stayed in the same property for over ten years, against 15% of private renters, only about 6% of the stock came free in a year and 2.7% in London, and 78% of new tenancies are for life. A council flat in inner London at £120 a week against a market rent of £500 is a tax-free subsidy of about £20,000 a year. The subsidy exists only if the tenant stays in that flat, so a tenant offered a job in Manchester turns it down. Meanwhile 1.34 million households sit on waiting lists, 165,000 children live in temporary accommodation, 32 councils report waits over 18 years, and three London boroughs have waits over a hundred years at current rates. One generation stays because the incentive is overwhelming. The next waits outside, because the only ways out are a funeral or a prison.

The deeper problem is that the system has stopped rewarding work. A single parent with two children in council housing who moves from a £20,000 job to a £30,000 job loses most of the extra £10,000. It goes to withdrawn housing benefit, withdrawn credits, withdrawn childcare help, and higher tax, an effective rate above 80% in the worst cases. So the arithmetic of taking the job is worse than the arithmetic of refusing it. The share of working-age adults neither working nor looking has risen sharply since the pandemic, with about 2.8 million on long-term sickness benefits and the number growing every month despite repeated tightening. The state now spends more on working-age welfare each year than it collects in income tax, no government can cut it without riots or tax more without driving out the last high earners, and so it borrows the difference. Across 36 advanced economies Britain carries the fifth highest deficit, the third highest borrowing costs, and the sixth highest debt at once. Its own fiscal watchdog calls the position increasingly vulnerable by historical and international standards.

The health service is the closest thing Britain has to a religion, and in 2025 its waiting list in England held 7.25 million people, about one adult in nine, down from a 2023 peak of 7.77 million. The median wait for planned treatment is 13.6 weeks against 7.8 in 2019. Stitched through it all is immigration. In the year to March 2023 net immigration was 944,000, about three times the pre-Brexit average and the population of Glasgow added in a year. Between 2021 and 2024 it totalled 2.55 million people, 3.8% of the population, the largest peacetime demographic shift in British history. Total output grew because there were more people spending, and output per person went sideways or down. Each arrival needed a home, a doctor, and a school place. A frozen planning system and stagnant productivity could not supply them. Immigration accounts for about 89% of the 1.34 million rise in England's housing shortfall over the decade, and 67% of London's private renting households are headed by someone born abroad. Under pressure from both left and right, net migration fell to 204,000 in the year to June 2025, care-worker visas fell 81% after dependants were banned, and for the first time in decades more British citizens leave than return. Immigration is now the top issue for 51% of Britons, and 45% say the country should take no more migrants and should require many recent arrivals to leave. The insurgent party that campaigned for thirty years on the border polled 14.3% in 2024 and won five seats. By April 2026 it led every poll at 24%, with the Conservatives at 19% and the Liberal Democrats at 13%. The chancellor's net approval stood at minus 51 and the prime minister's at minus 48, after a gifts scandal, a farmers' revolt, and a reversed cut to pensioners' fuel payments. Over all of it hangs September 2022, when a prime minister's £45 billion of unfunded tax cuts set off a bond-market panic, forced the central bank into a £19.3 billion emergency intervention, cut £425 billion from pension funds in 72 hours, and ended her premiership in 49 days, outlasted by a lettuce a tabloid had filmed beside her photograph.

The painful part is that the diagnosis is not contested. Everyone who runs modern Britain agrees on what the country needs. It needs far more houses, much cheaper energy, and lower marginal rates on its most productive workers. Both main parties now have leaders with approval too deep in the negative to carry a mandate for the painful part.

The forecasts offer no comfort. The IMF's 0.8% is the sharpest downgrade in the G7. The fiscal watchdog projects debt at about 96.5% of output by 2028 before easing, interest rising from £111 billion to £137 billion by 2030, and, on unchanged policy, debt at 275% of output by the 2070s and 325% under stress. Total output is forecast to grow from about $4 trillion to $6.8 trillion by 2040, which keeps Britain the fifth largest economy in the world. Its rank on income per person slides from nineteenth to the low twenties. The phrase economists now use is a poor big country, enormous on paper, stagnant on the ground, whose citizens read that they live in the fifth largest economy while earning less in real terms than their parents did at their age. Nine percent of Britons think the country is wealthy enough and 70% expect it to get worse.
