# White Guilt

People of European descent are said to owe a debt for what was done in the past in their name. The debt is usually treated as already proven, so the arguments that are supposed to prove it rarely get stated out loud. There are six of them in circulation, and the question for each one is whether a debt owed today actually follows from it.

Every one of the six needs the same thing to be true, which is that what Europe did, or what Europe is now, has no match anywhere else. The whole case depends on that requirement, because something every society has done cannot make one society owe for it. There is a second objection as well, and it holds on its own, which is that guilt does not pass from parents to children. Even a crime with no match anywhere would not put a debt on the people who happen to share an ancestry with the people who committed it. All six arguments claim that something about Europe has no match elsewhere, so that claim is what each of them is tested on.

## Slavery

Every society practised slavery, and the institution is older than any of the societies now being accused. The details make the uniqueness claim weaker rather than stronger, because the Atlantic trade was not the largest in history and not the largest of its own era, and most of the people sold into it were bought from sellers who had already enslaved them rather than seized directly.

The one thing that was unusual counts in the accused civilization's favour rather than against it, because abolition started inside that civilization, was imposed at home at a real cost, and was then forced on other societies by naval power for decades. If being unique is the standard the argument sets, the unique part is the ending of slavery.

## Colonialism

Colonialism was also universal, and here the uniqueness claim has some support, because the scale had no precedent and no other civilization managed anything like it. Wanting to colonize was common, and succeeding at it was not.

The claim fails on what came after, because the territories were handed back, mostly within a generation and mostly without anyone forcing it by war, and that handing back is the part with no match elsewhere. The argument has also moved away from its history, because it now mostly serves as an explanation governments give for conditions that arose fifty years after the withdrawal, in places where the ability to run things declined after the handover and not before it.

## Extermination of native peoples

This argument is partly true, and the true part should be accepted.

As a general account it does not hold, because where the native population had built a settled civilization it merged with the arriving one rather than disappearing, and the people descended from both are the largest group in Latin America today. The deaths were catastrophic, and most of them came from disease rather than from anyone's decision.

But particular episodes were deliberate, and nothing in the general account answers for them, so the argument fails as an account of what happened in general and still points at real events inside it. That is a different result from the one the slavery and colonialism arguments got, and it is counted as a different result.

All three of the historical arguments come out the same way, because in each case the part that stood out was not the crime but the ending of it, the giving back, and the part of the record that was kept. The other three arguments do not compare history at all, because they make claims about the present, and a claim about the present can be checked against the present.

## Built-in advantage

The claim is that these societies are constructed, by design, to move wealth and standing from one population to another.

The claim can be tested, and it fails the test, because when it is asked which law or which mechanism produces the advantage today, the argument does not name one. People's choices also go against it, because the movement of people is overwhelmingly toward these societies, and it comes from the populations the argument says these societies are built to hold down, which does not fit the claim as stated.

## Built-in racism

This argument presents racism as a disposition rather than a policy, which means it can be measured. Where preference for one's own group is measured across populations, the group the claim is about comes out lowest rather than highest, so the measurement that would prove the claim proves the opposite.

## Guilt for one atrocity

This argument names one historical crime and hands responsibility for it to everyone who shares an ancestry with the people who did it. Two things defeat this argument, and the first is that most of the people killed in the episode usually named shared that ancestry themselves, so the claim that the crime was one race's act against another does not match the casualty figures. The second is that atrocity is not rare, because every civilization large enough to leave a record has one in that record.

What was unusual again counts in the accused civilization's favour, because writing the crime down, prosecuting it, and building permanent memorials rather than denying it has no consistent match elsewhere, and a civilization that builds the means to stop it happening again is being prosecuted on evidence it collected and kept itself.

## What the six add up to

Five of the six fail, and they fail in two ways. Slavery, colonialism, and guilt for one atrocity all depend on comparing Europe with other societies, and in each case the comparison comes out in Europe's favour, because the thing that stands out is the correction and not the crime. Built-in advantage and built-in racism make no comparison with history at all, and because each is stated in a form that can be checked against the present, the check can be made, and it returns the opposite of the claim. One argument partly holds, by pointing at real episodes inside a general account that is false.

None of the six establishes a debt owed today, because a debt that needs uniqueness fails against a record that every society shares, and a debt that needs descent fails against the second objection, which is that guilt does not pass from parents to children.
