# White Guilt

A debt is asserted. European populations are held to owe something on account of what was done historically in their name, and the obligation is treated as settled enough that the arguments establishing it are rarely stated. Six of them circulate. The question on this page is whether a present debt follows — from the historical charges, and from the present-tense claims that travel with them. The charges as a prosecution sit on the adjoining page.

Each of the six rests on a claim about uniqueness — that what happened here, or what is the case here, has no counterpart elsewhere — and that is the load-bearing part, because nothing common to every society can ground a debt owed by one of them. Responsibility does not transmit by descent either, which is an independent stop: even a unique crime would not bill the people who share an ancestry with its perpetrators. The sections below take the uniqueness claims, because those are the ones the six actually argue.

## Slavery

The practice was universal and the institution predates any of the societies under indictment. On the specifics the uniqueness claim gets worse rather than better: the transatlantic trade was not the largest in history and not the largest of its own era, and the captives were overwhelmingly purchased from sellers who had already enslaved them rather than seized directly.

What is genuinely distinctive runs the other way. Abolition originated in the accused civilisation, was imposed internally at cost, and was then enforced on others by naval power for decades. If uniqueness is the standard the argument sets, the unique element is the ending.

## Colonialism

Also universal, and here the uniqueness claim has something real behind it: the scale was without precedent, and no other civilisation achieved anything comparable. Ambition was general and success was not.

The claim does not survive what followed. The territories were relinquished, mostly within a generation and mostly without being militarily compelled, which is the part with no parallel elsewhere. And the argument's present function has drifted from its historical content — it now serves largely as an account offered by governments for conditions that postdate the withdrawal by half a century, in places where administrative capacity declined after the transfer rather than before it.

## Extermination of indigenous peoples

This one is conceded in part, and the concession should stand.

The general claim does not hold: where the indigenous population had built a settled civilisation, it merged with the arriving one rather than disappearing, and the mixed descendant population is the largest demographic in Latin America today. Mortality was catastrophic and most of it was epidemiological rather than directed.

But specific episodes were deliberate, inexcusable, and are not answered by any of the above. The argument fails as a general account of what happened and identifies real events inside it. That is a different result from the previous two and is worth marking as one.

## Systemic advantage

The claim is that the societies are constructed to transfer from one population to another by design.

It has a testable form and fails on it. Asked which current statute or mechanism produces the advantage, the argument does not name one. The revealed preference runs against it as well: movement is overwhelmingly toward these societies and from the populations the argument says they are built to disadvantage, which is difficult to reconcile with the claim as stated.

## Inherent racism

Presented as a disposition rather than a policy, and measurable as such. Where in-group preference is measured across populations, the group the claim is about registers the lowest, not the highest. The instrument that would establish the claim establishes its opposite.

## Collective guilt from a particular atrocity

The move is to name a specific historical crime and assign responsibility for it to everyone sharing an ancestry with its perpetrators. Two things defeat it. The majority of those killed in the episode usually cited shared that ancestry, so the racial framing does not survive contact with the casualty figures. And atrocity is not distinctive: mass violence appears in the record of every civilisation large enough to leave one.

What is distinctive again runs the other way. The institutional response — documentation, prosecution, and permanent memorial rather than denial — has no consistent parallel, and a civilisation that builds machinery to prevent recurrence is being indicted on evidence it collected and preserved itself.

## What the sequence shows

Five of the six arguments for the debt fail, in two ways. Slavery, colonialism, and collective guilt for an atrocity all turn on a comparison with other societies, and in each the comparison runs the wrong way: the element that stands out is the correction rather than the crime — abolition, relinquishment, institutional accountability. Systemic advantage and inherent racism make no historical comparison at all. Each is stated in a form that can be tested against the present, and the test returns the opposite of what is asserted. One partly holds, identifying real episodes inside a false general account.

None of the six establishes a present obligation. A debt that needs uniqueness does not survive a general record, and a debt that needs descent does not survive the stop already stated: responsibility does not travel down a line.
