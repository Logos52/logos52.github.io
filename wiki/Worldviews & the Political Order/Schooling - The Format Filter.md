# Schooling - The Format Filter

Modern schooling asks young children to sit still and listen to an adult for hours, and girls handle that format better than boys. Schools treated the boys who failed it as the problem, and then taught both sexes a story about men that set them against each other. Gen Z, the generation born around 1997 to 2012, carries the result into dating, work and politics.

- The seated classroom filters children by sex.
- Boys who fail it are punished or medicated.
- Boys catch up on standard tests by 16 to 18.
- The grades their teachers give them stay lower.
- Girls learn that boys are stupid and troublesome.
- Boys learn that women and institutions are a danger.
- Pay-gap policies created a real gap favouring young women.

## The filter

A primary classroom asks a child to sit still and pay attention to an adult talking for hours, which would be hard even for an adult. At that age boys lean toward spatial and mechanical activity and have stronger drives for movement and adventure. An imaginative boy escapes by daydreaming, and a less imaginative one finds something to do with his hands. Both fall behind, and both get into trouble for the way they fell behind.

- Schools could have changed the format, and they blamed the boys instead.
- The response was discipline and, more and more often, ADHD medication.
- Sex decides who passes the filter.
- Gender words came later and called the failure a fault in men.

## The test scores

Educators defend the system by pointing to the girls' better results, which only works if boys and girls are the same in every way that matters. That belief, often called the blank slate, is assumed and never argued. Two measures show where the problem lies. By 16 to 18 boys catch up on standardized tests such as the SAT and pull ahead in mathematics and at the top of science and engineering, while the grades their mostly female teachers assign stay lower.

## What the girls learned

Girls spent a decade watching boys fail, get punished and fall behind. They were praised as smarter, when what was being rewarded was being quieter and more agreeable. Both sexes absorbed the lesson that boys are less capable and less responsible, and a girl taught this is ready for anything said later about men.

- Boys: stupid or troublemakers.
- Girls: smart and responsible.
- What was measured was compliance with the format.

## The words and the suspicion

New words turned normal male behaviour into faults. Manspreading named how a man sits, and mansplaining named a man explaining something to a woman. Men bond by explaining how things work, and women more often by talking about people, so the taboo closed a main way men connect with women. In the MeToo years teachers treated every male student as a possible harasser, and the slogan believe all women made female students the judges of male students' character.

- A clumsy approach made a boy a creep, sometimes filmed and posted online.
- An accusation cost the accuser nothing, so false ones spread.
- Gossip and ruined reputations became common.
- Male teachers refused to be alone with female students.
- Boys stopped asking girls out, and some stopped talking to them.

## The pay gap

Classrooms taught the gender pay gap as fact, through posters, projects and assemblies, in the form that compares all men with all women. That form does not survive once field, hours and years of work are taken into account. The policies built on it gave girls preferences, in the form of women-only scholarships, warm recommendations and recruiting quotas, while boys had only mixed scholarships and fell in the rankings.

- The same preferences carried into universities and hiring.
- Young women are now often hired and paid more for the same job.
- That new gap comes from the preferences, which discriminate against young men.

## Where it ends

Girls came out taught that men are stupid, entitled and hold power at women's expense, and that institutions are on their side. Boys came out taught that women are dangerous and unaccountable, and that institutions will tear them down for someone else's benefit. People trained since childhood to see the other sex this way do not form households easily.

- Fewer relationships and fewer families.
- Open hostility between young men and young women.
- Gen Z is among the most politically divided generations on record.
