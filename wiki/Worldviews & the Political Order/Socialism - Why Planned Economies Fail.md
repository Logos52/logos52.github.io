# Socialism - Why Planned Economies Fail

The calculation problem is the argument that a state which owns every factory and farm cannot plan well. Nobody buys or sells those things, so no prices form to tell planners what anything is worth. As a result, planned economies run short of some goods while piling up others.

Socialism has a second problem, separate from prices, which is that it puts more effort into dividing wealth than into making it. A society with too many takers and not enough makers falls apart. Venezuela shows how far that can go, since it became so busy taking, dividing and reallocating that people starved in the streets. Attention focused on taking is attention not focused on building. Put plainly, "if you don't make stuff, there is no stuff."

Without prices, planners set quotas, so factories meet the quota and not the need. The economy then ends up with too much of one thing and too little of another. During the Great Leap Forward, between 36 million and more than 45 million people died between 1958 and 1962. Each step toward a toll like that looks harmless on its own. Over the last century socialism killed between 65 and 100 million people. That is a higher death toll than the Black Death. The common replies are that real socialism has never been tried and that the Nordic countries show it working, yet Denmark calls itself a market economy. Underneath both replies sits how each system handles mistakes, because a planner's error hits everyone at once.

## What prices do

Prices carry knowledge, since a price tells everyone at once how much people want something and how hard it is to supply. "Economic calculation" means using those prices to decide what to make and how much. A builder, for example, can judge whether houses are worth building only if he knows what the wood costs and what the houses sell for. Once one institution owns all the resources, there is nobody left to trade with, so no prices form and that knowledge is lost. Planning then becomes "a system of groping about in the dark."

Most useful knowledge is also spread across millions of people and cannot be written down, so only prices can gather it. That is also why faster computers would not rescue planning, because demand is subjective and prices are the only real signal of it.

## What goes wrong without them

Without prices to steer by, planners set quotas instead, which means factories meet the quota and not the need. The Soviet Union shows how large that task is. By 1985 its price bureau reviewed roughly 200,000 prices a year, against the roughly 20 million a working economy needs. Soviet nail factories show that gap at work. Judged by weight, they made a few huge nails nobody could hammer, but judged by count, they made thousands too small to use.

The result is a mismatch economy, with too much of one thing and too little of another. A warehouse can hold a mountain of shoes in one size and too few in another.

China under Mao took the same Soviet-style quotas and set one for every village, county and province, and the Great Leap Forward of 1958 shows where that leads. Villages competed to beat their grain and steel quotas, so peasants melted pots and door handles in backyard furnaces. The grain figures rose on paper even as people starved beside mountains of uncollected grain. Between 36 million and more than 45 million people died between 1958 and 1962.

A toll that large is easy to overlook, because socialism looks harmless, even kind. Each step feels like standing up for people who have less, and each small decision seems rational on its own, so the cost only shows once those decisions pile up. Judged by its results rather than its intentions, it has cost more lives than almost anything we think of as plainly evil, such as mass murders, mass shootings and plane hijackings. In the last century it killed between 65 and 100 million people. That is a higher death toll than the Black Death.

The quota method behind that toll did not end with Mao, because China still runs on quotas: a GDP growth target is met by building whatever counts as output, whether or not anyone buys it.

## The common replies

Defenders of planning answer this record in two main ways. The first is that real socialism has never been tried. That reply is the no true Scotsman fallacy, because the same failure follows "when 20 million prices get replaced by one committee." Socialism was also tried by choice in the Israeli kibbutz, which people joined freely and which later dropped equal pay once members left for better-paid jobs.

The second reply is that the Nordic countries show socialism working. Denmark's own prime minister rejected that label in 2015, telling a Harvard audience, "Denmark is a market economy." Those countries are also unusually homogeneous, and any economic model can work when incentives line up, as they do inside a family.

Underneath both replies sits the question of how each system handles mistakes. A bad restaurant in a market loses customers, so the damage stays small. A planner's error hits everyone at once, and it is hard to undo because admitting it would weaken the planners' authority.

## Open questions

- How does a whole society learn to chase the number instead of the need?
- How do a planner's errors travel through the whole system over time?
- How do small, sensible-looking decisions add up to a society that takes more than it makes?
