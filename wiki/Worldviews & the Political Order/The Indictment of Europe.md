# The Indictment of Europe

Three charges are brought against European history, and they are brought against Europeans collectively: slavery, colonialism, and mass killing. Whether a present debt follows from the same record is a different question, and answering these three charges does not settle it.

Two answers stop the charges, and they work independently. The first is that a charge singling out one society for punishment has to show the conduct was unique to that society, and a practice universal to human societies cannot do that. The second is that responsibility does not transmit by descent: the people now living are not parties to the acts, and sharing an ancestry with someone who was does not make them parties. Either answer is enough on its own. The usual reply that the sins of the father are not the sins of the son is the second answer. It is not a concession of the first.

Each of the three charges argues uniqueness, so each one is tested here on uniqueness. The descent answer stays in force alongside that test. Anyone who withdraws the collective framing withdraws both sides of it.

## Core takeaways

- The three charges brought against European history as a group are slavery, colonialism, and mass killing.
- A charge that asks for the punishment of one society has to show the conduct was unique to it. A practice found in every society large enough to have a history cannot show that.
- Responsibility does not pass down by descent. People alive now were not parties to the acts, and a shared ancestry does not make them parties.
- Each charge leaves out a related fact that runs against it: abolition came from the accused civilisation, colonial rule depended on local collaboration, and the mass killings were followed by prosecution and by institutions built to prevent more.
- The charges are brought where they will be conceded rather than where they would be resisted, and the evidence used to bring them exists because the accused compiled it.
- If the accusation is kept collective, the same standard runs against the accusers, and then no one bringing these charges obviously holds the standing to bring them.

## The charge of slavery

The charge requires that the Atlantic trade was uniquely wrong, because a practice universal to human societies cannot single out one of them for punishment.

The Atlantic trade was neither the largest nor the most brutal. The trade running east and north across the Sahara and the Indian Ocean exceeded it in scale and, by most accounts, in cruelty. It was not unusual either. Chattel slavery appears in the recorded history of essentially every society large enough to have one.

Capture was not a European operation. The supply was assembled by African polities that raided, enslaved, and sold, and the European role was purchase at the coast. The charge falls on the buying and leaves the selling uncharged.

What is unusual is the termination. Abolition was formulated in the accused civilisation, imposed at home against its own commercial interest, and then enforced on other societies by naval blockade for the better part of a century. That enforcement ran at cost, against resistance, and with no precedent anywhere.

The conduct was common to many societies and the abolition came from one of them. The uniqueness the charge needs is not there.

## The charge of colonialism

Empire was also universal in ambition, and here the uniqueness claim has something behind it. The scale achieved was unmatched, because everyone tried and only one succeeded.

Two facts complicate the account of how that scale was reached. The first is that colonial administration ran on local collaboration at every level, without which the manpower arithmetic does not work. Five hundred Spaniards took the largest empire in central America alongside a hundred and fifty thousand native allies, and a thousand Belgians held a territory the size of western Europe through millions of locally recruited enforcers. The second is that refusal was not futile everywhere. The survival of Ethiopia caps the claim that the conquered had no alternative to submission.

The harder claim is that collaboration was frequently rational. Populations facing extractive local powers of their own often judged the incoming administration the lesser evil. Technology, codified law, security, and medicine arrived alongside the extraction, and those had real value. Dissatisfaction became general late rather than early.

The uniqueness claim does not hold once the comparison is made. An empire sustained by the participation of the governed is also not an act performed on them from outside.

## The charge of mass killing

The atrocities are real and none of this denies them. Nearly every civilisation with the capacity has done this, several at greater scale, and the twentieth century's worst episodes were not distinctively European in either method or motive.

The distinction is in what happened afterward. The accused civilisation documented its crimes, prosecuted them, memorialised them, and constructed institutions specifically to prevent recurrence. Others deny theirs, minimise them, or continue to honour those responsible.

That produces the arrangement the charge depends on. The evidence available to prosecute exists because the accused compiled it. A system that assigns guilt in proportion to the admitted record rewards concealment and penalises confession, which is the opposite of what a system aimed at justice would do.

The charge is brought against the societies that will concede it, not against the ones that would resist it. That is a fact about the accusation rather than a unique crime.

## What the three charges come to

None of the three charges establishes the uniqueness a collective punishment would require. In each case the conduct was general, and in each case a related fact runs opposite to the charge: abolition, collaboration, and the institutions built afterward. Those are the parts the accusation leaves out.

Descent would have stopped the billing even if uniqueness had held, and it was not needed. A case built on uniqueness collapses when the comparison is actually made, and the comparison is not usually made, because the conclusion is treated as already entered.

If the accusation is kept collective, the same standard runs against the accusers, and on that accounting the standing to bring these charges is not obviously held by anyone bringing them. That result comes from the frame itself and is not a second indictment. Withdraw the frame and both sides of it go.

## How to practice this

1. Take a charge brought against a whole group. Ask whether the conduct was unique to that group. Notice whether the charge still holds once the conduct turns out to be general.
2. Check whether the charge assigns responsibility by descent. Notice that the people now living were not parties to the acts.
3. List what the accusation leaves out of the record it cites. Notice whether any of it runs opposite to the charge.
4. Ask where the evidence for the charge came from. Notice whether the accused compiled it themselves.
5. Ask whether the same charge is brought against societies that would resist it. Notice whether it is brought only where it will be conceded.
6. Apply the collective standard to whoever brings the charge. Notice whether they still hold standing under it.

## Open questions

If collective judgement is rejected in principle but the accusation is collective, is answering on its own terms a defense or a concession of the framing?

Where a civilisation's record is fuller because it compiled the record itself, what standard would compare it fairly against one that did not?

The charges are answered here as claims about uniqueness. If the case rests instead on effects that persist and beneficiaries who can be named, does the generality of the conduct touch it at all?
