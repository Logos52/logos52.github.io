# Socialism - The Calculation Problem

## Reasoning

### Abstract

The calculation problem is the reason socialism fails every time it is tried in full. A modern economy is millions of people making billions of decisions a day about what to build, grow, ship, save, and buy. Nobody in a ministry can know the answers. In a market nobody has to, because prices do the work. A shortage of copper pushes the price of copper up, every copper user on earth gets that signal at once, and each adjusts. When the state owns the factories, the farms, and the mines, there are no private buyers and sellers of those things. So there are no prices for them, and the planners have nothing to calculate with. The record of every country that ran the experiment says the same, and a new fact sits beside it. In April 2026, 62% of Americans aged 18 to 25 held a favourable view of socialism, and young women were the ones driving it.

### Core takeaways

- A planned economy has no prices for the things the state owns, so the planners cannot know what anything is worth, and every full attempt ended in shortage, famine, or flight.
- The Nordic countries that supporters point to are market economies with a welfare state on top, paid for by a 25% sales tax on everyone, and their own leaders say so.
- Every failure gets the same reply, that it was not real socialism, and that reply is the pattern rather than the exception.
- Young Western women lean toward socialism far more than young men who face the same rents and debts, and the difference comes down to five extra years in institutions that teach it and to feeds that repeat it.
- The polls show a protest vote in search of a label. The same young people distrust every party and, asked differently, call themselves pro-market.
- The sectors where young Americans are squeezed, housing, healthcare, college, and childcare, are the ones where government already dominates, and the cure is more competition there.

### Essay

The calculation problem is simple to say. A price is a message. When copper runs short its price rises, and every user of copper on earth gets the message at once and adjusts, with nobody in charge. The price carries knowledge that no office could collect, because most of it is in people's heads and never written down. A farmer's sense of his soil is that kind of knowledge. So is a foreman's sense of which machine is about to fail. When the state owns the factories, the farms, and the mines, there are no private buyers and sellers of those things. So there are no prices for them, and the planners have to guess. A planner's guess is made once for everyone, and admitting it was wrong would undermine the planner, so the mistake stays. A market's mistakes are made by millions of people one at a time, and each one is small and gets corrected when the customers leave.

Most of the fighting about socialism is fighting about a word. Supporters picture Denmark and critics picture Venezuela, and each is right about its own picture. Denmark is a market economy with a generous welfare state built on top of it, paid for by a heavy sales tax on everyone and by income taxes that start at middle-class incomes, and its own prime minister said so at Harvard. The welfare state is the second floor and the competitive private market is the foundation. Actual socialism, the kind tried for a century, takes the foundation away. Every country that tried it in full ended in shortage, famine, or flight. Each failure gets the same reply, that it was not real socialism, and the reply runs in a cycle. A regime is praised as the future, it fails, its admirers go quiet, and the next generation finds a new one.

The new fact set against that record is that young Western women now favour socialism far more than young men. The young men face the same rents, the same debts, and the same childcare bills. The causes, in order of weight, are ideology, campus, feeds, and money. Women spend about five more years than men inside institutions whose faculties sit far to the left of the country, learning a framework that reads personal economic strain as structural oppression and names capitalism as the villain in advance. Their platforms then repeat it several hours a day, while young men's platforms pull the other way. Two findings cut against the story the young women are told. Support for the most radical positions is strongest among the wealthiest, which suggests a belief worn as a badge rather than a need. And the pay gap they learn at sixteen nearly disappears for young workers without children in the same jobs, so the real problem is the cost of motherhood, which the Nordic countries address with leave and childcare and without abolishing markets.

The polls show a protest vote in search of a label. The same young people who favour socialism distrust every party and every institution, and, asked in other words, call themselves pro-market. They use the word because it points at problems they feel are real and because it annoys the people they blame. The problems are real. The sectors where young Americans are squeezed are housing, healthcare, college, and childcare. Those are the ones where government already dominates or where permitting and subsidy have bent the market. The cure is more competition in exactly those places.

The arithmetic then settles it. The state that would have to pay for a much larger welfare state already spends more on interest than on its military. Its pension and hospital funds run dry by law within a decade. Seizing every billionaire's entire fortune once would fund about a year and a half of the current deficit. The only honest ways to pay are a broad sales tax on everyone, the Danish way, or cuts to the same programmes the voters want expanded, and no party will propose either. So the system borrows until something gives. The question that survives when the labels are stripped away is who decides. Either millions of people decide through prices, or a small group decides through a plan.

### Conclusion

Socialism fails because it destroys the price that carries what people know, and every full attempt confirmed it. The Nordic countries that its supporters point to are what social democrats settle for after they win, a market with a welfare floor, and not what socialists promise. The young women voting for the word have a point about an economy that fails them and are wrong about the cause and the cure. The cause is a specifically American arrangement in which government has captured housing, healthcare, higher education, and childcare. The cure is more market in those places. The most likely future is neither victory. It is a long contested hybrid, and the generation that inherits the debt will do the naming.

## The numbers

Start with the debt those young voters will inherit. The United States owed $39 trillion in April 2026, a figure that grows by about $7.5 billion a day, with net interest above $1 trillion a year, more than the whole military budget. The same month 34% of 18 to 25 year olds held a favourable view of communism, and 40% of women in that age group called themselves liberal against 25% of men, the widest gap in the 24 years of the poll. The question here is not whether socialism works, since the record settles that. The question is why the most educated and connected generation in history has chosen to forget the record, and where the collision between the two facts goes.

Almost nobody who fights about socialism agrees on what the word means. Asked to define socialism in their own words in 2018, only 17% of Americans gave the textbook answer, government ownership or control of production. 23% said equality, and the rest said free healthcare, free college, government benefits, or the Nordic countries. In a 2014 poll 42% of millennials said they liked socialism, 16% could define it, and 64% of the same group said they wanted a free-market economy. Supporters picture Denmark and critics picture Venezuela, and each side is right about its own picture.

Denmark is not socialist. Its prime minister said so at Harvard in October 2015, while an American senator was calling it the model. His words were that Denmark is far from a socialist planned economy and is a market economy. Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Finland, and Iceland all rank in the top 15 of a widely used index of economic freedom. Denmark ranks above the United States. Sweden has run a nationwide school voucher programme since 1992 and abolished its wealth tax in 2007. Norway abolished its estate tax in 2014. The Nordic welfare states are funded by value-added taxes of about 25% paid by everyone at the checkout. Their income taxes have top brackets that start at incomes that would count as middle class in America. The welfare state is the second floor and the competitive, privately owned market is the foundation. Actual socialism, the kind tried for over a hundred years, nationalises the factories, farms, banks, and resources. It replaces market prices with central planning and restricts or abolishes private capital. The Soviet Union ran that full programme, China followed, and Cuba, North Korea, Cambodia, and Venezuela ran their own versions.

### The calculation problem

- By 1985 the Soviet price bureau set about 200,000 prices a year. A working economy needs about 20 million, updated all the time.
- Nails priced by weight came out huge and unusable. Nails priced by count came out tiny and unusable.
- East Germany's car had no fuel pump because none could be sourced, and buyers waited up to 16 years for one.

The calculation problem was set out in 1920 by an Austrian economist, and it was mathematical rather than moral. Planners cannot know how much copper a factory needs in a given week or how many tomatoes a village can spare, and under state ownership there is no market in capital goods to tell them. A colleague extended it in 1945 with a second point. Knowledge is not just large, it is dispersed and mostly unwritten. A farmer knows his soil. A foreman knows which machine is about to break, and a shopkeeper knows which customers pay on time. No planner can collect that, let alone process it. Markets process it automatically through the price, and socialism destroys the price and throws the knowledge away.

The Soviet Union ran the test for seventy years. By 1985 its price bureau was reviewing about 200,000 prices a year, and a working modern economy needs about 20 million updated continuously. The gap was filled by shortages, black markets, and waiting lists. A spokesman for the last Soviet leader joked that the Soviets would one day conquer every country except New Zealand, which they would leave alone so that somebody could still tell them the market price of goods. A glass factory was ordered in 1933 to switch to dairy and fish, with no training, equipment, or cows. It complied because refusal meant prison. Nails priced by weight produced a few enormous nails nobody could hammer, and nails priced by count the next year produced thousands too small to use. East Germany's car was built from recycled plastic resin because steel was always short. Buyers waited up to 16 years for one. It had no fuel pump because none could be sourced, so fuel fell into the engine by gravity from a tank above it. By 1991 the Soviet budget deficit was 34% of national output, trade among the republics had fallen 83% in 18 months, and the union dissolved on 26 December 1991.

### The record

- The Soviet famine of 1932 and 1933 killed three to five million Ukrainians while their grain was exported for machinery.
- China's famine of 1958 to 1962 killed 36 to 45 million people after villages melted their tools for steel and killed the sparrows.
- Venezuela, on the largest oil reserves on earth, saw output per head fall 87% and a quarter of its people leave.

The Soviet regime nationalised industry, banned private trade, and shot the peasants who resisted. Collectivisation from 1929 drove small farmers onto state farms. The famine that followed killed three to five million Ukrainians in 1932 and 1933. Grain was seized from the starving villages and exported to pay for machinery. The camp system held about 1.8 million people at its peak and 1.5 to 1.8 million died inside it, and millions more were executed in the purges of 1937 and 1938. A 1997 academic collection put the Soviet death toll at about 20 million and other scholars put it nearer 15 million.

China's story is worse. In 1958 its leader ordered every village to build a backyard steel furnace and melt down its pots, tools, and door hinges. That produced useless pig iron while nobody farmed. Local officials afraid of purges reported grain totals upward. The centre believed them and collected more, and grain that did not exist was taken anyway. A campaign to kill sparrows, which ate grain, removed the birds that ate locusts, and the locusts ate what was left. The famine of 1958 to 1962 killed at least 45 million by one archival count and 36 million by another. About 2.5 million of the deaths were from beatings, torture, and execution for resisting seizures. China kept exporting grain through the worst year. Sixteen years after the famine the leader died. Within two years his successor let peasants farm their own plots, legalised private business, and opened zones for foreign capital, under the name of socialism with Chinese characteristics. In 1978 about 88% of Chinese lived in extreme poverty. By 2020 it was below 3%, about 800 million people lifted out in four decades, by the thing the regime had retreated from.

Venezuela is the modern case. In the 1970s it was the fourth wealthiest country on earth per head, sitting on 303 billion barrels of oil, the largest reserves anywhere. In 1998 it elected a former officer on a promise to share the oil wealth. He nationalised the oil company and in 2003 purged thousands of its engineers for loyalists. He imposed price controls on food and medicine and nationalised farms, factories, mills, and banks. He funded a vast programme of social spending from oil. While oil stayed above $100 a barrel it looked like it was working. Poverty fell, and output per head rose from about $4,000 in 1999 to $14,200 by 2013. Then the leader died, oil fell, and fifteen years of damage surfaced at once. Oil production collapsed because the people who knew how to run refineries had been fired a decade earlier. Nationalised farms stopped producing. Price controls made every product a loss, so private firms stopped making them, shelves emptied, hospitals ran out of medicine, and in 2013 the government put a toilet-paper factory under military occupation for sabotaging the revolution. Output per head fell to about $1,800 by 2019, an 87% drop. Inflation reached 130,000% in 2018 by the central bank's count and nearly a million percent by the IMF's. Venezuelans lost an average of 24 pounds in a year, zoo animals were stolen and eaten, and by 2024 about 7.4 million people, a quarter of the country, had left, the largest refugee flow in the history of the Western Hemisphere.

### The reversal, and the not-real-socialism move

- In 1978 about 88% of Chinese lived in extreme poverty. By 2020 it was under 3%, after private farming and business were allowed.
- The freest quarter of economies average about $120,000 of output per head. The least free quarter average about $10,600.
- Asked what real socialism would look like, supporters name Denmark, Sweden, or Norway, which are not socialist.

The same decades show the mirror image. In 1920 about 84% to 90% of humanity lived in extreme poverty, by 1990 it was 38%, and by 2024 it was about 8% to 12%, some 1.5 billion people lifted out in a generation. The driver was market capitalism spreading across Asia. China accounts for about 800 million of the gains. Vietnam removed almost all its poverty between 1993 and 2020 through farm reform, private enterprise, foreign investment, and exports that grew about 20% a year. India in July 1991 had reserves for three weeks of imports and flew 67 tons of gold to London as loan collateral. It then dismantled a forty-year licensing system, cut tariffs from over 150% to 30%, and shrank the list of industries needing a state licence from nearly all to six. About 270 million Indians left poverty in the three decades after. The freest quarter of economies on the economic-freedom index average about $120,000 of output per head and the least free quarter about $10,600, a ratio above ten to one. Since 1800 life expectancy has gone from about 38 years to 73.2, and child mortality from 43% to about 3.7%. Literacy has gone from 12% to 87%, and output per head on earth has risen about thirtyfold. Almost all of that happened in economies built on private property, voluntary exchange, and market prices.

Each failure is met with the same reply, that it was not real socialism. Philosophers call that move the no-true-Scotsman fallacy, in which every counterexample is redefined out of the category. The pattern is nearly mechanical. A regime rises, Western intellectuals praise it as the future, it produces famine or purge or flight, the intellectuals go quiet, and the next generation discovers a new regime and calls it the real thing. An American senator honeymooned in the Soviet Union in 1988 and praised its subway fares and housing costs, and a year later said he had never seen a hungry child in Cuba. Asked what the real version would look like, the answer is almost always Denmark, Sweden, or Norway, which are not socialist by any economic definition. Pressed on why every real attempt produced the same result, the answer shifts to corruption, sanctions, imperialism, or bad luck. Those explanations are structural. Hand a small group unchecked control of an economy and the control concentrates, the incentives rot, the information disappears, and the repression begins. It went that way in Moscow, Beijing, Havana, Pyongyang, Cambodia, and Caracas alike. The system is the problem, and the leader of any given attempt is a symptom.

### Where American capitalism is bent

- In constrained coastal cities the gap between what a house costs and what it would cost to build is above 50% of its value.
- About half of American healthcare runs through government programmes and the other half through tax-subsidised insurance.
- The official inquiry into the 2008 crisis found regulatory capture, government-backed mortgages, and cheap central-bank money behind the bubble.

The market record is not clean. American income inequality rose sharply after 1980, the Gini coefficient going from about 0.397 in 1967 to about 0.49 in 2023, and the top 1% went from 8% of national income in the late 1970s to over 20% by the 2010s. Housing became far less affordable. Healthcare spending reached 18% of output without the longevity of poorer countries. A prescription painkiller marketed by a private firm helped kill more than 800,000 Americans between 1999 and 2022.

None of those is a failure of markets as a system. Each traces to a place where the American version has been bent. Housing in the big cities is expensive because zoning, permitting, and local political capture block building. One Harvard economist puts the gap between what a house costs and what it would cost to build above 50% of the home's value in the constrained coastal cities. Healthcare is expensive because about half of it runs through government programmes and the other half through tax-subsidised employer insurance, so the market barely functions. The official inquiry into the 2008 crisis found regulatory capture, government-backed mortgage securitisation, and central-bank rate policy behind the bubble. The name for the system that produces those results is crony capitalism. The cure is more competition and less government picking of winners. Almost nobody is teaching that distinction to the generation now rising.

The ideology comes from the universities. Women now earn about 60% of American bachelor's degrees, up from about 40% in 1970, concentrated in the humanities and social sciences where faculty politics sit furthest from the country's. At Harvard 82% of the arts and sciences faculty called themselves liberal or very liberal and 1% conservative. The public is about 37% conservative, 37% moderate, and 26% liberal. The curriculum followed the faculty. Women's and gender studies grew out of 1970s feminism and absorbed critical theory, post-structuralism, and Marxist feminism. The idea that race, sex, and class overlap spread from there into the rest of the humanities. A student trained in it learns to read her own economic frustrations as structural oppression. The pay gap, student debt, housing, climate, and abortion arrive in one package. The package names the villain in advance, and the villain is capitalism. About 25 states no longer require a high-school economics course, down from 28 in 2014, and most students graduate having read the theorists and none of the economists.

The difference is that women spend five more years on average inside institutions that teach a Marxist-adjacent reading of that strain and then several hours a day on platforms that repeat it. The strain is the fuel and the campus and the algorithm are the spark.

Two findings cut against the headline story. One is the idea of luxury beliefs, that as cars and handbags became cheap the upper middle class shifted to signalling status through beliefs that cost the believer little and others a lot. Support for defunding the police in 2020 was strongest among the wealthiest Americans and weakest among the poor, who are three to seven times more likely to be crime victims. Young women who described themselves as struggling were slightly less pro-socialist than young women who said they were doing well. The other is the pay gap itself. The headline figure is 83 cents on the dollar, a raw average of all women's full-time earnings against all men's. A Harvard economist who won the 2023 Nobel prize for the work controlled for hours, occupation, industry, experience, and family status. She found 94 to 99 cents for young childless workers. Most of the remaining gap opens at the birth of the first child. It reflects the premium that law, finance, and consulting pay for long inflexible hours that mothers cannot accept. The 83-cent figure is taught at 16 and the 99-cent figure is not, so women build their politics on the first. The real problem the data shows is the motherhood penalty. Paid leave, childcare subsidy, and workplace flexibility would address it, and Denmark, Sweden, and Germany provide those without abolishing markets.

The young men are in a different crisis in the same house. Real male wages are down about 14% since 1979, millions of prime-age men are out of the labour force, and men without a degree face what one sociologist in 1987 called an unmarriageable trajectory. The median first marriage has moved to 30 for men and 28 for women from 25 and 23 in 1980, and about one in three young adults is projected never to marry. Women outnumber men on campus six to four. So millions of college-educated young women cannot find a college-educated male peer who is single, employed, and interested. Over half of young liberal women say they would not date a man who voted for the Republican president. 56% of men aged 18 to 29 voted for him in 2024. In functional terms most of the male peers those women would consider have been eliminated by politics.

The polls contradict themselves, and the contradiction is the finding. The same young women who favour socialism in record numbers also distrust government, the media, and the universities in record numbers. They distrust both parties too. A 2025 Harvard youth poll found only 13% of young Americans thought the country was headed the right way, 58% used a negative word for the Democrats, 56% for the Republicans, and 40% for both at once. The same poll found support for socialism among young Americans down to 21%, support for democratic socialism at 29%, and identification as a capitalist down to 19%. Gallup's 2025 polling had Americans as a whole favouring capitalism over socialism 54% to 39%. What the numbers show is a generation that has rejected every existing label. They call themselves socialist because the word points at problems they feel are real. The word also annoys the people they blame. In other polls, worded differently, they call themselves pro-capitalist. The belief is a protest vote in search of a label, a vessel for economic frustration rather than a description of a system they want. The 2014 poll where 42% liked socialism and 16% could define it set the template. Votes are still votes, though, and policy demands are still policy demands.

The demands cannot be paid for. The national debt stood at about $39 trillion in April 2026, about 120% of output, and the Congressional Budget Office projects 156% by 2055 under current law without one new programme. Net interest passed $1 trillion a year in 2025, more than the military budget and the third largest item after Social Security and Medicare. It is projected to double to about $2.1 trillion a year by 2036. The driver is demographic. By 2050 about 22% of Americans will be over 65 and two and a half times as many over 85 as today. Social Security's trust fund is projected to run out in 2033, when the law automatically cuts benefits by 23%, and Medicare's hospital fund hits the same wall the same year with an 11% cut.

Into that arithmetic young voters ask for universal healthcare, costed at $30 to $40 trillion over a decade, free college, cancellation of $1.7 trillion of student debt, a green programme whose own advocates cost at about $90 trillion over ten years, universal childcare, housing subsidies, and expanded paid leave. Taxing every American billionaire at 100% and seizing everything they own would raise about $5 trillion once, enough for about 18 months of the current deficit. The only honest ways to fund a much larger welfare state are broad taxes on the middle class, like the 25% sales tax that funds Denmark, or deep cuts to the programmes the same voters want expanded. Neither is available to either party. So the system borrows, the interest eats more of the budget, and eventually something gives. The IMF put global public debt just under 94% of world output in 2025 and on track for 100% by 2029, and the United States is further along than almost any peer and less able to agree on a correction.

The optimistic path is what some call supply-side progressivism or abundance politics. Liberalise zoning so housing can catch up, expand energy production, reform how healthcare is delivered, invest in infrastructure, and pair it with a modest safety-net expansion funded by consumption taxes rather than wealth taxes. The yes-in-my-backyard movement has already won in Minneapolis, Austin, and parts of California. The pessimistic path is a fiscal crisis within about fifteen years, in which the bond market loses confidence in America's ability to pay its interest. At that point benefits are cut, taxes rise, the dollar loses reserve status, and the post-war order unwinds. Britain had a shadow of that in September 2022, when an unfunded tax cut triggered a bond panic and ended a government in 49 days, and an American version would be systemic. Over both paths sits artificial intelligence. One study found each extra industrial robot per thousand workers cut the employment rate by 0.2 points and wages by 0.42%, concentrated on blue-collar men without degrees, and one bank estimated 300 million full-time jobs exposed to generative AI. If the gains go to capital while workers are displaced, demand for redistribution will rise whatever the young think of socialism. A universal basic income is already in the mainstream conversation.

- Under a plan one bad policy kills the sparrows and another empties the supermarkets, and admitting it would undermine the planners.
- Every socialist regime built a secret police, because a plan needs enforcement.

Strip away the polling, the feeds, the curricula, and the celebrities, and one question is left, who decides. Under market capitalism millions of people decide, on their own knowledge, preferences, and budgets, coordinated by prices. The system makes mistakes constantly because every individual does, and the mistakes are small, local, and correctable. A restaurant that serves bad food loses customers, and a factory that makes the wrong thing goes bankrupt. A bad idea dies quietly, and a good one spreads because others copy it. Under socialism a small group decides one policy for everyone at once. Its mistakes are large, systemic, and nearly impossible to correct, because admitting them would undermine the planners. One bad policy kills the sparrows, another empties the supermarkets, another marches two million people out of a capital in 72 hours. A 1944 book argued that central planning ends in coercion because planning needs enforcement. Every socialist regime has confirmed it with a secret police. The Nordic countries that young voters point to are the other thing, market capitalism with a generous welfare state on top, paid for by consumption taxes on working people and defended by democratic institutions that no planning committee ever had. The Nordic model is what social democrats settle for after they win, not what socialists promise.

The young Western women voting for the system that killed 65 to 100 million people last century have a point about the American economy failing them. They are wrong about the cause and the cure. The cause is a specifically American crony capitalism that has captured housing, healthcare, higher education, and childcare. Those are exactly the sectors where government rather than the market dominates. The cure is more market capitalism aimed at the sectors where the distortion is worst. The most useful sentence a Western politician could say is the one the Danish prime minister said at Harvard, that Denmark is a market economy whose welfare state is funded by that market's productivity. Every country that tried the shortcut is on a list of former countries or a list of famines. The most likely future is neither victory. It is a long contested hybrid with more industrial policy, more family benefits, more housing intervention, and more selective public ownership. Against it stands a counter-movement on the right offering pro-family tax relief, housing supply, and anti-monopoly law in the language of market renewal. The trust-fund wall of 2033 is seven years away. Whatever comes next will be named by the generation now entering adulthood, trained in intersectional theory, carrying the debt, and priced out of the property their parents owned at their age.
