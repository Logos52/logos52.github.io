# Validity and Truth

An argument can be valid without being sound. It is valid when its form makes it impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false. It is sound only when it is valid and every premise is actually true. Checking an argument therefore takes two separate questions, whether the conclusion follows and whether the starting points hold.

Because validity is about form, a valid argument with a false premise is valid but unsound. Truth is still worth checking, since the closer someone's picture is to the truth, the better that person does. Many arguments hide a premise called the warrant, which links the evidence to the point being made, and a question can hide a premise in the same way. Testing an argument fairly means restating the other side so well that its holder would welcome the wording, then listing points of agreement, and only then offering a rebuttal. Being offended is no part of that test.

## Valid versus sound

Validity is about form, so it says nothing yet about whether the premises are true. Take a simple case. Elizabeth owns either a Honda or a Saturn, she does not own a Honda, so she owns a Saturn. If the first two lines are right, the last one has to be right, whatever car she really owns. The premises do not have to be true for that form to be valid. A valid argument with a false premise is therefore valid but unsound.

The second question is worth the effort because truth has practical value. The closer someone's picture of a business, a channel or a relationship is to the truth, the better that person does at it. Arguing about what is true in politics or society comes from the same place.

## Finding the hidden link

Many arguments hide one of their premises, so the first job is to find it. A useful model breaks an argument into six parts, which are the claim, grounds, warrant, qualifier, rebuttal and backing. The first three do most of the work. The claim is what the writer wants to prove, and the grounds are the evidence offered for it. The warrant is the assumption, stated or not, that links the grounds to the claim.

Hearing barking and howling, for example, supports the claim that a dog is nearby only because we know dogs bark and howl. That known fact is the warrant.

A question can hide a premise in the same way. Asking how someone's views on Harry Potter changed "given JK Rowling's bigoted opinions" already assumes the opinions are bigoted. In one classroom case, a student who asked that question walked through the presupposition with his teacher and changed his mind once he saw he did not have the evidence for it.

## Stating the other side first

Testing an argument fairly also means getting the other side's version right before attacking it. A fair critic first restates the other person's position so clearly and fairly that they say, "Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way." After that the critic lists any points of agreement and mentions anything learned from them, and only then offers a rebuttal. The order works because it turns an opponent into a more receptive audience for the criticism. The medieval article form did something similar. Each article of the *Summa Theologiae* opens with the objections to its own answer, and the article asking whether God exists states the problem of evil first.

Being offended is no part of that test, even though debates now often treat it as proof that the offended person is in the right and the critic is in the wrong.

## Open questions

- Why can an argument feel airtight to the person making it while resting on a warrant he never noticed?
- How does someone come to treat offence as proof of being right?
- Why does hearing his own view restated fairly change how a person listens to criticism?
- How does the habit of chasing the truth in business and relationships carry over into the way someone argues about politics?
