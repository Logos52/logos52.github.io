# The Woke Mind Virus

The woke mind virus is a phrase, popularized by Elon Musk, for "woke" ideas treated as an existential threat to American society. The phrase often travels with a claim that the left and right differ in mental health. A large study of American teenagers found that depressive symptoms rose for everyone after 2010 but fastest among liberal girls.

The phrase belongs to a wider attack on "woke" ideas from the alt-right, the religious right, moderate liberals and libertarians, and Musk called it "the mortal threat to civilization." A study of 86,138 American 12th-grade students from 2005 to 2018 found that the shares identifying as liberal and conservative barely moved. ## Where the phrase comes from

The phrase belongs to a wider attack on "woke" ideas from the alt-right, the religious right, moderate liberals and libertarians. Musk used it repeatedly in his own posts, calling it "the mortal threat to civilization" and writing that "the woke mind virus is either defeated or nothing else matters." In one reply he wrote that it "is penetrating the firewalls of some of the world's smartest meat computers at a prodigious rate!"

By 2025, conservative commentators such as Rod Dreher and James A. Lindsay had begun using "woke right" for far-right beliefs that mirror the far left.

## What one study found

The teenage study tested that claim on a national scale. It used yearly samples of American 12th-grade students from 2005 to 2018, 86,138 students in all, and tracked four scales of internalizing symptoms, meaning problems turned inward such as depressive mood.

Across those years about 19.8% of students identified as liberal and 18.1% as conservative, and those shares barely moved.

Depressive symptoms rose for every group after 2010, but the rise was steepest for liberal girls. Liberal girls whose parents had less education scored highest of all. Their average depressive-affect score rose from 2.02 points in 2010 to 2.75 points in 2018. The same pattern held across the other symptom scales.


## How far the study goes

The findings point to a growing mental-health gap between teenagers by political belief, and one hypothesis tries to explain it. Rising exposure to politicized events may have contributed to the increase, and the lens through which a teenager sees the political climate may then shape how much it hurts. That hypothesis is possible and not proven.
