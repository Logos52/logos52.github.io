# DEI as Anti-Discrimination

Diversity, equity, and inclusion programs are defended on two separate grounds, and one of them is testable. Six of the ten common defenses say the programs make organisations perform better, which is a claim about outcomes that can be measured and has been. The remaining four say the programs correct a wrong, which is a claim about justice and is argued differently. Sorting them apart is most of the work, because the two halves have very different records.

Underneath the justice case sits one premise: that unequal outcomes between groups are produced by systems acting on them. The defenses that inherit it fail where it does; the rest are answered without ever reaching it.

## The performance case

**Higher profits.** The strongest version of this rests on a widely circulated consultancy finding of a performance advantage in the region of a quarter. If it held, it would be the best argument available, because it would make the programs pay for themselves and end the debate on the defenders' terms. It has not replicated. An independent 2024 attempt found no relationship, firms that discontinued their programs have reported no financial harm, and meta-analyses of board gender diversity cluster around zero and below.

**More creative teams.** The mechanism offered is that different backgrounds supply different ways of seeing a problem. The evidence separates two things the argument keeps together: diversity of skill and professional background does show gains in innovation, while diversity defined demographically shows mixed to negative effects. What the finding supports is hiring people who think differently, which is not what the programs select for.

**Reduced bias.** The observation is sound — people do favour their own — and the remedy does not follow from it. A general preference operating unevenly across individuals is answered here with a preference applied uniformly by policy, which is the same phenomenon with an administrator. The programs are also built to close outcome gaps rather than to detect bias in decisions, so success is measured by the distribution rather than by whether anyone was treated unfairly.

**Improved morale.** The claim is that people work better where they are visibly included, and that the programs supply the signal. The programs' own results run the other way, including elevated turnover among the groups they set out to protect.

**Serving diverse markets.** If employees from a group genuinely build a better product for that group, they are the better hire on ordinary grounds and no separate policy is needed to find them. If they do not, their identity is not doing the work the argument assigns it. The defense is self-cancelling in either direction, and it also assumes a customer preference that price and quality routinely override.

**A broader talent pool.** Widening a search does raise the quality of what you find. That is an argument for recruiting more widely, and the programs are not that: making identity a factor in the decision necessarily lowers the weight carried by everything else, which is the opposite of the claim.

The pattern across the six is that each one, taken seriously, argues for something the programs are not. Recruit widely, hire for cognitive difference, watch for bias in decisions — all defensible, and all distinct from selecting on demography.

## The justice case

**Equal opportunity.** Uncontroversial as stated, and the programs are not that. Equalising the chance to compete and equalising the result of the competition are different operations, and the second one is what the policies measure.

**Representation.** Representation in public life is good, and wanting it is reasonable. Where this goes wrong is in retrofitting — reassigning established characters or historical figures rather than creating new ones — which carries an implication the advocates do not intend: that the group being represented could not generate its own.

**Merit as a myth.** The claim is that standards are so shot through with prejudice that merit cannot be separated from it. But whether an aircraft is landed safely, or whether someone can carry an adult out of a burning building, is settled by the outcome rather than by anyone's opinion of the standard. Where the test is objective, merit is objective, and the argument's strength is confined to cases where it is not.

**Correcting historical injustice.** This is where the underlying premise becomes visible. The claim requires that present disparities were produced by identifiable systems, and asking which injustices, laws, or systems produce a specific gap generally does not return an answer — because the framework treats a non-systemic explanation as itself evidence of prejudice. A claim that cannot be answered wrongly is not doing the work of an argument.

## What the ten come to

Five of the defenses contain something real: bias exists, wide recruitment helps, cognitive diversity helps, representation matters, and equalising opportunity is the right aim. In each case what survives argues for a policy other than the one on the table. One more survives in part — merit is contestable wherever no objective test settles the matter, and no wider than that.

Profits, morale, and market tailoring never needed the premise; each failed on its own ground. What holds up the remainder is a single premise — that a gap between groups is evidence of a system acting on them — and the defenses resting on it stand or fall with it. ## Open questions

If cognitive and professional diversity produce the gains that demographic diversity was credited with, what would a hiring policy built on the former actually select for?

Where a standard genuinely cannot be made objective, what replaces merit as the criterion without reintroducing the discretion the standard was there to remove?
