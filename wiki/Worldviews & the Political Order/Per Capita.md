# Per Capita

Per capita means per head. It is a count divided by the population the count came from.

A raw total tells you how much of something there is. A rate tells you how common it is for a person inside that population. The two answer different questions, and a total is often used where a rate is meant.

The same substitution runs through everything measured across groups of different sizes. A country with larger total output is not thereby a country whose citizens are better off. That question is answered by output per person, and the two rankings come apart.

## Core takeaways

- Per capita is a count divided by its population. A total measures how much there is. A rate measures how common it is.
- A group four times the size of another will produce more of nearly everything, including things nobody wants. A larger count on its own establishes only that the group is larger.
- Dividing by population often reverses the answer the raw counts suggested.
- There are three explanations for refusing to divide: the arithmetic is a step more abstract than reading a total, the total supports the point being made, or the rate leaves other variables uncontrolled. Only the third is legitimate.
- A person who cites per capita where it helps them and calls it misleading where it does not is not confused. Genuine confusion produces errors in both directions.
- A rate says how common something is and says nothing about why. It is where a causal argument starts.

## Two bags of apples

Two bags of apples sit on a table. The first holds a thousand apples, twenty of them poisoned. The second holds ten apples, two of them poisoned. One apple has to be eaten, and the eater picks the bag it comes from.

Most people pick the second bag, because two is a smaller number than twenty. The second bag poisons one eater in five. The first poisons one in fifty. Picking the second bag is ten times worse, and it feels like the safer choice throughout.

The twenty poisoned apples in the first bag are real. They are not the number that governs the decision. The number that governs it is the share of poisoned apples in each bag.

## Where the mistake happens

The characteristic error is comparing raw counts drawn from populations of very different sizes and treating the larger count as the larger problem. A group four times the size of another will generate more of nearly everything, including things nobody wants, and saying so establishes only that it is larger. The comparison people actually intend is whether something is more common here than there. That comparison requires the division, and the division frequently reverses the answer the raw counts suggested.

When somebody declines to do the division, there are three explanations. Only one of them is respectable.

## Explanation one: the arithmetic is a step more abstract

Per capita requires holding two numbers at once, dividing one by the other, and interpreting a result that has no direct referent in the world. That is one layer of abstraction more than reading a total, and one layer is enough to lose people. This is not a moral failing. Rule it out first, because the two explanations that follow are only visible once it has been eliminated.

## Explanation two: the total supports the preferred conclusion

Someone who can perform the division declines to, because the divided figure undercuts the point being made. There are two versions of this. In the deliberate version the person knows the rate reverses the claim and presents the total anyway. In the motivated version the raw number confirms something the person already believes, so no impulse to check arises. The motivated version is a real cognitive failure and a milder one, since the machinery was available and simply never engaged.

The test that separates this from the first explanation is whether the same person uses rates when rates favour them. Someone who cites per capita where it helps and calls it misleading where it does not is not confused. A person who is genuinely confused gets it wrong in both directions, and this person does not.

## Explanation three: the rate leaves other variables uncontrolled

This is the only legitimate objection, and it is legitimate because it is true. A rate tells you how common something is and says nothing about why. Comparing crime rates without accounting for poverty genuinely does leave the interesting question unanswered, and someone raising that is raising something real.

It has a counterfeit, and the two are easy to tell apart. The honest version says a further variable is needed to understand the pattern. The counterfeit says the rate is worthless because it does not explain everything. No statistic in any field has ever satisfied that demand, and making it removes the number from consideration.

The discriminating test is whether the person actually performs the control they are asking for. Someone who names a confound, applies it, presents what came out, and explains why that variable is the relevant one is doing analysis. Someone who names a confound and stops has produced a reason to disregard a figure. That is a different activity using the same vocabulary.

## What the rate is for

The rate is the beginning of an argument rather than the end of one. It establishes that something is more or less common in one population than another. Any causal claim has to start from that fact, and the rate is not itself a causal claim.

What it protects against is narrower and more useful: being moved by a number that was never about the question. The twenty poisoned apples were real, verifiable, and irrelevant to the only decision on the table.

## How to practice this

1. When two counts come from populations of different sizes, divide each count by its population before comparing them. Notice whether the division reverses which figure is larger.
2. Before judging someone who quoted a total, check whether they can do the division at all. If they cannot, this is the first explanation and nothing further follows.
3. Watch whether the same person cites rates when rates favour them. Selective use rules out confusion, because confusion produces errors in both directions.
4. When someone names a missing variable, check whether they applied it and showed the result. Notice whether you were given an analysis or only a reason to disregard a figure.
5. Once you have a rate, stop before stating a cause. Notice that the rate tells you how common something is and not why.
