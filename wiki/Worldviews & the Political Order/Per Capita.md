# Per Capita

Per capita means "per person". It is the plain division that turns a raw count into a rate, so a reader can see how common something is for the people involved. A large group will usually have a large count of almost anything, which is why a raw total says little until it is divided by the size of the group. Per capita still has one real limit, because it shows how common something is without explaining why it happens.

Bag A holds more poisoned apples than bag B but is the safer bag to eat from. The same division shows that a higher total GDP does not mean richer citizens and that Americans die on foot about 2.7 times as often as Europeans. People skip the rate for three reasons, which are that the division is hard for them, that bias makes the raw count feel like enough, or that other variables need controlling for. Only the last is a fair objection, and a serious version of it actually controls for the variables it names.

## Totals against rates

The simplest case is a choice between two bags of apples. Bag A holds 1,000 apples with 20 poisoned apples among them, while bag B holds 10 apples with 2 poisoned apples. Bag A has more poisoned apples in total, yet it is the safer bag to eat from, because a far smaller share of its apples is poisoned.

Country wealth works the same way. A country with a higher total GDP, meaning the value of everything it produces in a year, does not necessarily have richer citizens than a country with a lower one. Adding more people raises total GDP, so a measure of how well people live has to look at GDP per person instead.

Rates also make comparisons between places of different sizes fair. The European Union has a population 34% larger than the United States, yet it recorded about 3,714 pedestrian deaths in 2022 against 7,522 pedestrian deaths in the United States. Per person, Americans die on foot about 2.7 times as often as Europeans.

The same division works when two groups each make up a known share of a population. American men are about half the population but account for roughly 75% of suicide deaths. As rates, that is 22.7 suicides per 100,000 men against 5.9 suicides per 100,000 women, nearly four to one.

## Why people skip it

Commentators who quote raw totals often leave the rate out. A common example holds that white Americans benefit most from SNAP food aid because they are the largest racial group on it. The same group is also the majority of the country, so the useful figure is how its share of recipients compares with its share of the population, and by that measure it is underrepresented.

Three explanations cover most cases like this. The first is that the division is too hard for someone, because it means holding two numbers, dividing them and reading the result. The second is bias, where a person can do the sum but skips it, sometimes knowingly to mislead an audience and sometimes without noticing, because the raw count seems to confirm what he already believes. There is a simple test for this, since someone who uses per capita when it helps him and calls it misleading when it does not is reasoning selectively.

The third explanation is the only fair objection, which is that other variables need controlling for, such as poverty when comparing crime rates. Wanting more variables to understand a gap is different from calling the rate useless unless it explains everything. A serious version of the objection actually controls for the variables it names, shows the results and explains why those variables are relevant.

## Open questions

- Why does a raw total feel more convincing than a rate to the person using it?
- How does someone come to use per capita when it helps and call it misleading when it does not?
- How does adding one more control variable change what a rate can explain?
