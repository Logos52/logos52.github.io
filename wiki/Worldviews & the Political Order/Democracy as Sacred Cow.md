# Democracy as Sacred Cow

Democracy is usually treated as a good that needs no defence and that every country should have. People defend it with seven arguments: consent, the wisdom of crowds, the lack of a better alternative, fairness, peaceful transfer of power, liberty and wealth. Only peaceful transfer of power survives a close reading, so a reader can stop leaning on the others.

## Takeaways

- All but one of the usual arguments break or shrink.
- Elections let the likely loser of a fight concede without fighting.
- That holds only while votes track who would actually fight.
- The fairness argument gets no real answer.
- A broken argument for democracy does not make democracy bad.
- Democracy needs a population able to carry it.

## The weak arguments

Each argument below is stated at its strongest and then answered. Consent that binds needs a real way to refuse, and a voter has none, since abstaining exempts nobody and the losing side is bound on the same terms as the winner. Every state coerces, and the vote only changes who authorises the coercion. The wisdom of crowds needs independent judgments, and most voters repeat a position they already heard, so the total measures what was broadcast.

- Learning politics costs effort, and one vote never decides an election.
- So staying uninformed is the sensible choice for a voter.
- Politicians know this and appeal to feeling.
- Churchill's "worst form except all the others" fit his own time.
- It does not hold across the centuries.
- Voters have voted themselves treasury money until the state went bankrupt.
- That pattern goes back to ancient Greece.
- The Civil Rights Act, passed against racial discrimination, ended freedom of association.
- An elected official values a citizen only until his term ends.
- A hereditary ruler's horizon includes his heirs.

## Wealth and fairness

Democracies are richer, and the correlation is real. The rich countries are the Western ones, so the correlation may track the region and not the form of government. Setting up a democracy and keeping it without outside help takes a competent, self-reliant population, and such a population could produce both the wealth and the democracy. Countries made democratic by outside intervention have ended up poorer.

- Fairness: one person, one vote needs nobody to weigh whose views count.
- Anyone who sets the weights holds the real power.
- The usual answer, that people differ so equal is unfair, misses this.
- The fairness argument stands unanswered.

## The one that holds

Votes measure support, and each side assumes the larger side would win a civil war, so the smaller side concedes instead of fighting and power changes hands without violence. This works while votes track who would actually fight. When many voters would never fight, the count misleads, and once that shows, violence becomes more likely. An example with two parties shows the limit.

- Party A: a well-armed, motivated 45 percent of voters.
- Party B: an apathetic 55 percent.
- B wins every election, and A would win any civil war.
- The question is how long A accepts the results.
- In a monarchy, a power struggle is a dispute among elites.
- In a mass democracy it is a civil war reaching every family.

```
   votes track force            votes stop tracking force
   -----------------            -------------------------
   loser sees it would lose     loser sees it would win
   a fight, so it concedes      a fight, so it stops conceding
   -> peaceful transfer         -> higher chance of violence
```

## Who can carry it

John Adams, the second American president, wrote that the US Constitution was made only for a moral and religious people. The case against democracy applies that line to modern societies, and says voting needs a population able to carry it. Aristocracy and monarchy are better aligned with a country's long-term survival.

## What this desk holds

The owner is unsure about democracy as a system. His own claim is narrower and concerns who makes up the electorate: mass immigrants should not hold decisive voting power over local policy. Deciding who holds the vote does not weight anyone's vote, so it avoids the question of who sets the weights.
