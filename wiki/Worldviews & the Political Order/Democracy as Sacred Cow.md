# Democracy as Sacred Cow

Democracy is usually treated as a good that needs no defense and should be set up in every country. People defend it with arguments about consent, the wisdom of crowds, the alternatives, fairness, peaceful transfer of power, liberty and wealth. Only the argument about peaceful transfer of power survives a close reading, so a reader can stop leaning on the other six.

## Core takeaways

- Six of the seven usual arguments for democracy either break or shrink to a narrower claim than the one people make.
- The one that holds: elections let the side that would lose a fight concede without the fight, which stops power struggles turning violent.
- That argument holds only while votes track who would actually fight, and it turns dangerous once they stop.
- The argument from fairness is not answered here; the answer given never reaches its strongest form.
- Showing that an argument for democracy breaks does not show that democracy is bad.
- The case against democracy here is scoped to modern societies, on the claim that voting needs a population able to carry it, and that claim is asserted without evidence.

## The argument

The seven arguments, each stated at its strongest, then the answer given.

- Consent of the governed. Voting is how the ruled agree to their rulers.
  - Answer: consent that binds needs a real way to refuse. Abstaining exempts nobody, and the losing side is bound on the same terms as the winner. Every state coerces; the vote only changes who authorizes the coercion.
- Wisdom of the crowds. Many independent guesses average out better than most single guesses.
  - Answer: the result needs independent judgments. Most voters repeat a position they already heard, so the total measures what was broadcast.
  - Second leg: learning politics costs effort and one vote never decides an election, so staying uninformed is the sensible choice for a voter. Politicians know this and appeal to feeling.
- The worst form except all the others. Every alternative did worse.
  - Answer: arguably true in Churchill's own time, not across the centuries. Voters have repeatedly voted themselves money from the treasury until the state went bankrupt, a pattern back to ancient Greece. This narrows the claim to a period; it does not destroy it.
- One person, one vote is fair. Equal weight is the only rule that needs no judge of whose views count more, since anyone who sets the weights holds the real power.
  - Answer given: equal is not the same as fair, because people differ.
  - That answer never addresses the point about who sets the weights, so the defense stands unanswered here.
- Elections replaced violent power struggles, the one argument that holds.
  - Votes measure support. Each side assumes the larger side would win a civil war, so the smaller side concedes instead of fighting.
  - Limit: this works while votes track who would fight. When people who would never fight vote, the count deceives, and once the deception shows, violence gets more likely.
  - Worked case: party A has a well-armed, motivated 45 percent of voters; party B has an apathetic 55 percent. B wins every election and A wins any civil war. The open question is how long A accepts the results. The case is made up to show the mechanism; no record of it happening is given.
  - In a monarchy or aristocracy a power struggle is a dispute among elites. In a democracy with mass mobilization it becomes a civil war that reaches ordinary families.
- Democracy protects liberties. Constitutions, courts, a free press and the need to win re-election keep rulers from abusing voters.
  - Answer: the Civil Rights Act was meant to end racial discrimination and in practice ended freedom of association, argued in full on its own page.
  - Second leg: an elected official has reason to value a citizen only for the length of his term, while a hereditary ruler's horizon includes his heirs. This leg is asserted with no instance or mechanism behind it.
- Democracies are richer. The correlation is real and granted.
  - Answer: the rich countries are the Western ones, so the correlation may track the region rather than the form of government. Setting up a democracy and keeping it without outside help takes a competent, self-reliant population, which could produce both the wealth and the democracy. Countries made democratic by outside intervention have ended up poorer.

```
   votes track force            votes stop tracking force
   -----------------            -------------------------
   loser sees it would lose     loser sees it would win
   a fight, so it concedes      a fight, so it stops conceding
   -> peaceful transfer         -> higher chance of violence
```

## Where the case is thin

- The alternative is never specified. Aristocracy and monarchy are named as better aligned to a country's long-term survival, but no way of choosing the rulers is given.
- The argument from uninformed voters counts against mass participation; it says nothing in favour of hereditary rule, which has its own selection problem.
- John Adams wrote that the US Constitution was made only for a moral and religious people. The line is about one constitution and is stretched here to modern societies in general.

## What this desk holds

- The owner is unsure about democracy as a system. His own claim is narrower and concerns who makes up the electorate: mass immigrants should not hold decisive voting power over local policy.
- Who holds the vote is a lever that can be pulled without weighting anyone's vote, so it does not run into the unanswered point about who sets the weights.
