# Democracy as Sacred Cow

Democracy is defended less often than it is assumed. The arguments for it exist and get repeated, but they are seldom examined, because examining them reads as an attack on something settled rather than a question about something contested. The question here is narrower than that reflex expects: not whether democracy can work, but whether the reasons usually given for it hold up. Seven are in common circulation. Two are still standing at the end, and not for the same reason.

The narrower question matters because the wide one is unanswerable. A system can be right for a population that can carry it and wrong for one that cannot, which makes the interesting claim conditional rather than universal — and none of the seven defenses is conditional. They are offered as reasons democracy suits anyone.

## Consent

The strongest version runs like this. A government is legitimate when the people it rules have agreed to be ruled, and voting is how that agreement gets given and renewed. Whoever votes participates in authorizing the government; whoever abstains was offered the authorization and declined it. Rule of that kind differs in kind from rule imposed by force.

The word does the work, and it does not survive being looked at. Consent of the sort that makes an agreement binding requires that refusal be possible and consequential. A vote offers no refusal: abstaining exempts nobody from the result, and the losing side is bound on exactly the terms of the winning one. The plainest test is that compliance still needs enforcing. An arrangement resting on agreement would not require police to collect what was agreed to.

What survives is smaller than the defense wanted. Voting settles who authorizes the coercion and leaves the coercion in place.

## The many and the few

Aggregate enough independent judgments and the average beats most of the individuals composing it. The effect is real. If the many are better than the few, the many should decide.

The effect has a condition, and the condition is independence. Mass electorates do not supply it: most voters hold a position they have already heard, so the aggregate measures how widely something was broadcast rather than how many people worked it out. The second problem is structural. Becoming informed costs real time, and one vote never decides an outcome, so the return on the effort rounds to nothing. Staying uninformed is the rational choice, and a system that makes ignorance rational cannot then claim the wisdom of the crowd it produced.

## The comparative claim

Every alternative has been tried and produced worse results, so democracy wins on comparison even with its defects admitted.

Read across a long enough span, the comparison stops holding. The claim was defensible in the middle of the last century and is doing different work now, when the pattern it has to survive is the one where an electorate discovers it can vote itself the contents of the treasury and does so until there is nothing left. That caps the claim to a period rather than destroying it, which is the honest result and less than the defense needs.

## Equal weight

One vote each is the only distribution that requires no judgment about whose opinion counts more. Any other scheme needs someone to set the weights, and whoever sets them holds the real power — so equal weighting is not one option among several but the only one that keeps an unaccountable authority out.

The usual answer to this is that equality is not fairness, since equal shares are fair only where the recipients are alike in whatever the shares are for. That answer is true and it does not reach the argument above. Nothing in it addresses the regress: if votes are to be weighted, somebody weights them, and that somebody rules. The regress is what a rebuttal would have to break, and the usual one does not touch it.

Composition is a different lever. Who is in the pool can change by admission and by remigration, and nobody has to assign a weight. The regress does not arise, because no weigher is introduced. The defense above is about how votes are counted among whoever already holds them. It is silent on who those people are.

## What actually holds

One defense survives intact.

Before elections, changing rulers meant fighting. Elections let relative support be measured without the fight: each side estimates who would win, and the side that would lose concedes rather than pay for losing. That is a real achievement and nothing else on the list comes close to it.

It also has a condition. The mechanism works while votes track actual force, and it degrades when they stop.

```text
Party A holds a motivated, well-armed 45% of the country.
Party B holds an apathetic 55%.

B wins every election. A wins any civil war.

The question the arrangement cannot answer is how long A keeps conceding
to a count that measures something other than what a fight would settle.
```

There is a second cost, and it belongs to democracies specifically. Where power is contested among a small ruling class, a succession fight stays among them. Mass mobilization carries the same fight into ordinary households and sorts families onto opposite sides.

## Liberties

Democracy protects liberties better than the alternatives, through constitutional protections, independent courts, a free press, and rulers who face re-election and therefore have reason not to abuse the people who will judge them.

The record complicates this. Legislation intended to end discrimination has ended freedom of association instead: one protection was extended, and a different one was quietly spent to pay for it. The incentive half of the defense is weaker than it sounds, since an elected official's horizon ends with his term and a citizen's usefulness beyond it is somebody else's problem — though the alternative on offer, a ruler whose horizon includes his heirs, is asserted rather than demonstrated.

## Wealth

Democracies are substantially richer than non-democracies, and the correlation is strong and persistent.

The correlation is not in dispute. What it licenses is. The wealth tracks the West more closely than it tracks the system, and the direction of the arrow is unsettled: establishing a democracy and holding it without outside help takes a competent and self-reliant population, which is the sort of population that produces wealth under most arrangements. Where the system has been installed from outside on populations that had not produced it, the results have generally been worse than what preceded them.

## What the count comes to

Five of the seven defenses break outright or narrow to a period. One holds, with a failure condition attached to it. One is left standing because the answer usually given to it does not reach it.

That is a thinner case than the reverence suggests, and it is a case rather than a verdict. A system can outlive every argument made for it. What the count establishes is that the arguments are load-bearing in far fewer places than the people repeating them assume, and that treating the question as closed has cost the defenders the ability to say why.

## Open questions

If votes work as a proxy for force, what happens to the mechanism in a society where the capacity for force has become concentrated in a professional class that does not vote as a bloc?

Any weighting of votes requires someone to set the weights. Is there a scheme where the weigher is constrained by something other than the votes being weighted?

If composition can change the electorate without anyone assigning weights, what stops that lever from doing the work the weighting debate is trying to do?
