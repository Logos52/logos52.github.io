# The Gen Z Gender War - The Split

The split is the growing gap between young women and young men in politics, schooling, dating and marriage. The Front Page video [The Gen Z Gender War](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MVPpfKOqXY0) cites a 2025 King's College London survey of 30 countries. It says young women there are about 30 percentage points more progressive than young men, up from a gap of 6 points in 2000. The video's view is that each side's grievances are real, and that the two sides now live in separate media worlds that feed those grievances back to them.

## Two movements growing apart

The video traces the women's side through four waves of feminism, from the Seneca Falls convention of 1848 to the online fourth wave that began around 2012. Its example of the fourth wave's reach is Alyssa Milano's 2017 "Me Too" tweet, which the video says drew 12 million Facebook engagements in 24 hours.

On the men's side it describes the manosphere, which it calls a loose mix of men's rights activists, pickup communities and involuntary celibates. It names the sociologist Warren Farrell as an intellectual anchor. His 1993 book *The Myth of Male Power* argued men face disadvantages in courts, schools and health, and the video says the manosphere took those arguments far beyond what he intended. Andrew Tate brought this to mass scale, according to the video, because algorithms rewarded him for ever more extreme statements.

## The feeds that push each side

The video's explanation for why the split hardens is algorithmic. It cites a University College London and University of Kent study that set up TikTok accounts behaving like lonely teenage boys, and says misogynistic content in their feeds rose from 13% to 56% in 7 days. The pipeline, it argues, starts with reasonable topics like loneliness, lifting or feeling overlooked, so each step seems fine until the end point is extreme. Women's feeds, the video says, served content framing heterosexual men as uniformly threatening or socially incompetent. The video's summary is that neither feed reflects real people, because both show whatever holds attention longest.

Its example of the two worlds talking past each other is the 2024 "man or bear" question on TikTok. The video says women who chose the bear meant the constant threat assessment they do around strange men, while many men heard a verdict that they were dangerous as a category.

## School and the dating pool

The video calls the college reversal the most statistically distinctive trend in the split. It says men were 58% of US college students in 1970 and women about 60% by 2015. It notes that when women lagged, the country passed Title IX and closed the gap in about 20 years, while the reverse gap has had no similar response. It cites Richard Reeves's argument that boys develop verbal and executive skills 12 to 18 months later than girls, so classrooms that reward sitting still fall harder on them. It adds that men who skip college are not always choosing badly, since a skilled electrician or plumber earns $50,000 to $80,000 a year without tuition debt.

The video connects this to dating through assortative mating, the tendency to partner with someone of similar education. As women gain more degrees, it says, educated women see fewer men they consider comparable and men without degrees fall below more women's stated preferences. It cites OkCupid data suggesting women engage positively with about 14% of male profiles and men with about 46% of female profiles.

## Opting out

The video describes exits on both sides. South Korea's 4B movement, which began around 2015, rejects dating, sex, marriage and childbirth with men. The video says US searches for it spiked after the 2024 election even though formal followers numbered in the thousands. On the men's side it describes MGTOW, men going their own way, and "passport bros" who seek partners abroad.

## Three possible futures

The video closes with three paths:

- The current trajectory continues, politics sorts by sex, and fertility heads toward South Korea's. The video treats South Korea as the leading indicator.
- Partial convergence comes through exhaustion and policy, such as Reeves's idea of starting boys in school a year later and the EU's Digital Services Act putting rules on algorithms.
- Fragmentation takes over, with each side retreating into separate lives, including AI companions such as Replika.

Its conclusion across all three is that both sides describe real problems. It lists women facing workplace barriers and physical risk in dating and men facing an education crisis and a suicide rate it puts at nearly four times women's.
