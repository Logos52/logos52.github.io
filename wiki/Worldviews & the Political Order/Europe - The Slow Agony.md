# Europe - The Slow Agony

The slow agony is a name for what happens to Europe if it keeps making the choices it has made for the past quarter of a century. A former head of the European Central Bank used the phrase in a 2024 report on European competitiveness. What it describes is a European economy that keeps growing more slowly than the American one, year after year.

In 2008 the European Union's economy was larger than America's, at 16.4 trillion dollars against 14.8 trillion. By 2025 the American economy was about 29 trillion dollars and the European economy was 19.5 trillion. The gap of 10 trillion dollars did not exist in 2008, and it now exceeds the economies of Japan, Germany, and the United Kingdom combined. The 2024 report said Europe needs 800 billion euros of new investment every year to stop falling behind. That is roughly double what it cost to rebuild Europe after the Second World War. A year after the report, 11.2% of its recommendations had been carried out.

## Core takeaways

- In 2008 the European Union's economy was larger than America's. By 2025 it was 10 trillion dollars smaller, and the gap is projected to reach 15 trillion by 2030.
- The euro was built without the three conditions its founding theory required. After the financial system stopped working in 2008, Europe cut spending and raised rates while America spent, and Southern Europe lost a decade and many of its young people.
- Europe closed its reactors, banned drilling, and bought Russian gas. When that gas stopped, European gas prices rose more than 1,000% and the factories that ran on it began to close.
- No European Union company worth more than 100 billion euros has been founded from scratch in 50 years. Venture money, pension rules, and 27 separate legal systems keep pushing founders out.
- America added 53 million people between 2000 and 2025 and took the skilled immigrants. The Union added 22 million, its birth rate fell to 1.34, and its working-age population is projected to fall by 64 million.
- Europeans work fewer hours than Americans and keep their savings in banks rather than in stocks. The 2024 report wrote down what to change, and each change needs 27 governments to agree.

## How big the gap is

In 2008 the European Union produced 16.4 trillion dollars of goods and services and the United States produced 14.8 trillion. European leaders spoke openly of the euro replacing the dollar as the reserve currency, and European newspapers wrote of the American model collapsing under its own excess. By 2025 the American economy had reached roughly 29 trillion dollars and the European economy sat at 19.5 trillion. The 10 trillion dollar gap exceeds the economies of Japan, Germany, and the United Kingdom combined. Germany's output per person is now roughly that of Oklahoma, and France has fallen below Arkansas.

The gap is projected to widen. The International Monetary Fund's long-term projections of late 2025 put the American economy at roughly 40 trillion dollars in 2030 and the European economy at roughly 25 trillion, so a gap of 10 trillion in 2024 becomes 15 trillion by 2030. The Eastern European countries that joined the Union in 2004 keep converging. The Western and Southern countries that produce most of the bloc's output keep stagnating. By 2050 China and India together are projected to be larger than the United States, Europe, and Japan combined.

## The four choices

Each time a decision came, Europe took leisure over output, precaution over speed, fragmentation over unity, and dependency over independence. America took the opposite each time, by luck and instinct more than by plan. Neither set of choices was wrong in the abstract. The European set stops working when the world changes, and the world changed. Europe was the birthplace of modern industry and modern science, and in less than a generation it was passed by the country that fought a revolution against it.

## The euro

In the 1990s a group of European finance ministers merged 11 national currencies into one, the largest bet in European economic history. The franc, the Deutsche mark, the lira, and the peseta were withdrawn. Greece had not met the entry requirements and was waved in 3 years later. The euro was born in 1999. The plan was that a continent-sized currency would make Europe a peer of the United States in finance, end the money lost to exchange, hold inflation down through a central bank in Frankfurt modeled on the German one, and serve as a symbol of unity.

The economist whose paper founded the theory of shared currencies won the Nobel Prize for it. He had warned decades earlier what a shared currency needs. The countries using it must be structurally similar. Workers must be able to move freely between regions. A central authority must be able to move tax revenue from booming regions to struggling ones. The eurozone at launch had almost none of that. German and Greek labor markets had almost nothing in common. A Portuguese welder could not move to Finland, because the welder did not speak Finnish. No European tax authority could take money from Germany and send it to Greece. The warning was that the euro, as designed, would amplify shocks rather than absorb them, and the warning was ignored.

For 10 years the currency seemed to work. Cheap German credit flooded the south. Greek households took mortgages at German rates. In 2006 Spain was starting more houses a year than Germany, France, and Italy combined. Irish banks grew to 10 times the size of the Irish economy.

## What each side did after 2008

In September 2008 the global financial system stopped working. The United States responded fast and at scale. Congress passed 475 billion dollars to recapitalize American banks within 3 weeks. The Federal Reserve began buying mortgage bonds in bulk. By early 2009 an 831 billion dollar stimulus was law, and the United States ran a budget deficit equal to 10% of its economy in a single year. Europe did the opposite. Germany, remembering the hyperinflation that destroyed the Weimar Republic, demanded austerity. Its chancellor insisted that European governments cut spending, raise taxes, and shrink deficits during a financial panic. The Federal Reserve bought trillions in bonds. The European Central Bank raised interest rates in 2011, in the middle of a debt crisis.

Greek government debt, understated for years by a series of Greek finance ministers, was revealed to be massive. Borrowing costs for Greece, Spain, Portugal, Ireland, and Italy spiked to levels that made their debts impossible to pay. None of the five could print its own currency. The only option left was internal devaluation: cutting wages, pensions, and public jobs until the domestic economy shrank enough for exports to compete again. Greece took three international bailouts. Between 2010 and 2014 its economy shrank by 26%, the longest peacetime recession of any developed economy on record. Youth unemployment reached 59.5% in Greece and 56.4% in Spain. Educated young Southern Europeans, many of them speaking three languages, left for London, Berlin, Amsterdam, Dubai, Singapore, and Miami. Italy's economy in 2026 has still not returned to its level before the crisis. Italian real wages are lower than in 1990, and Italy is the only G7 country where workers earn less than their parents did 35 years ago. The American economy finished its recovery by 2012 and then grew faster.

## Energy

In the early 2000s, in the shale basins of Pennsylvania, Texas, and North Dakota, small oil and gas companies learned a new method. They pumped water, sand, and chemicals into rock 2 miles underground, which cracked open shale that earlier drillers had written off. A Saudi oil minister said the Americans would never get it out at a profit. By 2015 the Americans had quadrupled natural gas production and tripled oil production. By 2018 the United States was the world's largest oil producer. By 2023 it was the world's largest exporter of liquefied natural gas, ahead of Qatar and Australia. ## Births and the size of the workforce

The European Union's fertility rate fell to 1.34 births per woman in 2024, the lowest ever recorded on the continent. Italy sits at 1.18, Spain at 1.0, Malta at 1.01, and every member state is below the replacement rate of 2.1. Italy is on track to lose 12 million people by 2070. Its median age is 49, and 156,000 Italians left in a single year. Germany's population grew by 0.1% in 2024, entirely from immigration, because more native Germans died than were born. America's rate, at roughly 1.68, is higher and also falling, and American immigration keeps growing the country. The United States added roughly 53 million people between 2000 and 2025 and the Union added roughly 22 million.

## Hours, pay, housing, and savings

The young are doing worse than their parents did at the same age. A software engineer at Google's Munich office, working on the same code as a counterpart in Mountain View, earns 40 to 50% less once stock is counted. A young doctor in Paris earns roughly a third of one in Boston. Spanish youth unemployment was still 25.3% in 2024. In France 33% of people aged 60 to 64 work, against 61% in Germany, so the French retire early and keep the young out to make room. A studio in central Paris costs more per square foot than most of Manhattan. Amsterdam, London, Dublin, Munich, and Stockholm are among the most expensive housing markets on Earth against local income. Zoning, land rules, and rent control stopped building while the capitals grew through the 2010s. Barcelona announced a total ban on short-term rental licenses by 2028, because its own citizens could no longer afford the city.

The treatment of business failure differs as well. A bankrupt French entrepreneur takes nine times longer to recover legally than an American one. The American president's companies filed for bankruptcy protection six times and he was elected twice, and a French entrepreneur with six filings would be barred from running any business. American startups give every engineer stock options, and European ones, legally and culturally, do not, under labor contracts so protective that the employer hesitates to hire. The average American worker logs 1,811 hours a year. The German logs 1,341, about 470 fewer, which is roughly 10 weeks a year, and the French, under the 35-hour week written into law in 2000, log about 1,490. Productivity per hour in Germany and France is often competitive with America. Europeans take the difference as vacation rather than output, and vote to keep it that way.

## What Europe is doing now

Europe remains one of the wealthiest regions on Earth, with a life expectancy of about 82 years against 79 in the United States. Norway's sovereign fund reached 2.1 trillion dollars in late 2025, about 340,000 dollars for every Norwegian. Poland is projected to pass Japan in output per person by 2030. Ireland's tax strategy made Dublin the European base for half of American big tech, and Switzerland remains among the most innovative economies anywhere. After the 2022 invasion Europe began to rearm, faster than at any time since the Cold War. By 2025 all 32 NATO members met the 2% of GDP target agreed in 2014, Poland spent more than 4%, and Germany amended its constitution in March 2025 to lift the debt brake for defense. Rheinmetall's shares rose 15-fold between February 2022 and late 2025.

The politics are not coordinated. France has had five prime ministers in under two years and its debt was cut to the lowest rating in its history. Germany's governing coalition collapsed in November 2024. Italy's prime minister is stable mostly because her opposition is more fractured. Spain's government depends on separatists whose leaders are in some cases fugitives. The European Parliament's latest election delivered historic gains to parties that want to dismantle the project. The Commission's president has proposed a competitiveness compass, a savings and investments union, and a single corporate legal regime for technology companies across all 27 states. Every one of those proposals needs the unanimous agreement of 27 governments.

Artificial intelligence is the next contest, and on the current figures it is already decided. In 2024 American private investment in artificial intelligence was 109 billion dollars and European Union investment was under 10 billion. The American Stargate project, announced in January 2025, committed 500 billion dollars over 4 years, and 400 billion of it was secured by October 2025. France announced 109 billion euros in February 2025. 70% of the world's foundation models since 2017 were built in the United States, and three American cloud companies control more than 65% of Europe's cloud market. The AI Act was restrictive enough that by late 2025 the Commission was drafting a digital simplification package to roll parts of it back.

The 2024 report wrote the prescription down: common European borrowing at continental scale, a capital markets union, simpler rules, unified defense procurement, electricity at American prices, selective high-skilled immigration, and an admission that the welfare states were built for a population pyramid that no longer exists. Asked a year later whether the recommendations would be adopted, its author said he was very pessimistic.

## What the model bought and what it costs

The European model delivered shorter working weeks, longer holidays, and stronger safety nets, and Europeans voted for it every time. What those comforts cost was slower growth, lower wages, and a smaller share of the economic future. The cost matters now because the conditions those choices assumed have ended. Under those conditions America paid for Europe's defense, Russia sold cheap gas, China bought the exports, and artificial intelligence was a problem for later. Whether the continent adapts or drifts will be decided in Berlin, Paris, Rome, Warsaw, and Brussels in the next few years. The signs so far are mixed.

## How to practice this

1. Check a claim about an economy's size against its output per person. Germany's output per person is now roughly that of Oklahoma. Notice whether a large total is hiding a low figure per person.
2. Before joining a shared arrangement, list the conditions it needs to work. The euro needed similar economies, workers who can move, and a shared treasury. Notice which conditions are missing on the day it starts.
3. Count how many separate approvals your plan needs before it can reach anyone. A company founded in Berlin has to cross 27 legal systems. Notice whether the count, rather than the idea, is what slows you down.
4. Check what happens to your work if one supplier stops. Germany took 55% of its gas from Russia, and the supply ended in a single week. Notice which parts of the work stop with it.
5. Compare hours worked against output per hour before judging a gap in results. A German works about 470 fewer hours a year than an American. Notice whether the gap comes from hours rather than from productivity.
6. Check where your savings sit. 58% of American households own stocks, against about 20% of German households. Notice the difference between what a deposit paid and what an index returned.

## Open questions

- The account calls every European choice a choice and every American one an accident or an instinct. Which American choices were choices, and which European ones were forced?
