# Europe - The Slow Agony

Europe has fallen steadily behind the United States, and the cause lies in its own choices more than in any outside shock. It traded output for leisure, bet its industry on Russian gas, kept 27 national markets instead of building one and regulated faster than it built. Those choices were affordable while trade grew, Russian energy flowed and America paid for Europe's security, but that world has ended.

The gap shows in incomes, output and stock returns, and around 70% of the gap in output per person comes from lower productivity. Behind it sit costly energy after the loss of Russian gas, internal barriers between 27 national markets, rules that only the largest firms can carry and fewer hours worked. The danger is now urgent because the workforce is shrinking, so growth must come from productivity. Most of the known remedies, though, need all 27 governments to agree.

## How far behind

The gap shows first in household incomes. Real disposable income per person, meaning what people have left to spend after taxes and inflation, has grown almost twice as much in the United States as in the European Union since 2000. Measured at constant prices, the GDP gap between the two widened from slightly more than 15% in 2002 to 30% in 2023. Around 70% of the gap in output per person comes from lower productivity, and most of that productivity gap lies in the tech sector.

Investors have seen the same divergence. American stocks returned roughly 500% including dividends over the 25 years that the leading eurozone index returned about nothing in price terms. Only 20% of German households own stocks, against 58% of American households, so most European savings sit in bank deposits and government bonds that gain little.

## The choices behind it

Energy shows how a single choice spread through industry. Germany shut its nuclear plants, banned most fracking and came to rely on Russia for 55% of its natural gas. When Russia invaded Ukraine, Europe lost its pipeline gas from Russia. European firms still pay electricity prices 2 to 3 times American levels and gas prices 4 to 5 times higher. The gap is driven mainly by Europe's lack of natural resources and flaws in its common energy market. The chemical company BASF began closing plants in its home town and placed its next $10 billion complex in China instead.

Fragmentation does similar damage to new companies. A European software firm must deal with 27 legal systems to reach as many customers as an American firm reaches under one. The single market's internal barriers act like a 45% tariff on goods and a 110% tariff on services. Rules written to restrain American tech, such as the data protection law GDPR, raised compliance costs that only the largest firms could carry. No EU company worth more than 100 billion euros has been founded from scratch in 50 years.

Work and capital follow the same pattern. The average American works 1,811 hours a year and the average German 1,341 hours, so competitive output per hour turns into much less output per worker. American pension funds put almost 2% of their assets into venture capital, European ones 0.01%, and between 2015 and 2024, 57% of European startup founders who moved went to the United States.

## Why it is now urgent

The EU's workforce is projected to shrink by close to 2 million workers a year by 2040, so growth must come from productivity. Productivity growth at its pace since 2015 would only keep the EU's output flat until 2050, while closing the gap needs 750 to 800 billion euros of extra investment every year. High taxes, costly housing and a pension system that makes today's workers pay for a growing number of retirees also leave young Europeans unable to afford homes or children, which shrinks the next generation further.

The remedies are known, including common borrowing, a single capital market, simpler rules and cheaper energy. Most of them need all 27 governments to agree, which leaves a choice between paralysis and deeper integration. European voters have so far kept choosing shorter hours, long holidays and safe pensions over higher output.

## Open questions

- How do voters' choices of shorter hours, long holidays and safe pensions add up over time?
- How does the need for every government to agree shape which remedies ever get tried?
- How do the people who write rules like GDPR see their purpose?
- How do founders weigh rules like GDPR when choosing where to build?
