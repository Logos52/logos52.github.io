# Europe - The Slow Agony

## Reasoning

### Abstract

The slow agony is the name a former head of the European Central Bank gave, in a 2024 report on European competitiveness, to what happens if Europe keeps choosing the way it has chosen for a quarter century. In 2008 the European Union's economy was larger than America's, 16.4 trillion dollars against 14.8 trillion. By 2025 the American economy stood at about 29 trillion and the European one at 19.5 trillion. The gap of 10 trillion dollars did not exist in 2008 and now exceeds the economies of Japan, Germany, and the United Kingdom combined. The report said Europe needed 800 billion euros of new investment every year, roughly double the cost of rebuilding Europe after the Second World War, to stop falling behind. A year later 11.2% of its recommendations had been carried out. The story behind the gap is a continent that, choice after choice, took leisure over output, precaution over speed, fragmentation over unity, and dependency over independence, against a country that almost by accident chose the opposite.

### Core takeaways

- In 2008 the European Union's economy was larger than America's. By 2025 it was 10 trillion dollars smaller, and the gap is projected to reach 15 trillion by 2030.
- The euro was built against the theory that justified it. When the crisis came Europe tightened while America spent, and the south lost a decade and its young.
- Europe bet its industry on Russian gas and against nuclear power and shale. When the gas stopped, the factories that ran on it began to close.
- No European company worth 100 billion euros has been founded from scratch in 50 years. Capital, rules, and 27 separate markets kept the founders leaving.
- America took skilled immigrants and grew by 53 million people. Europe took a humanitarian caseload, its births fell to the lowest ever recorded, and its workforce is set to lose 64 million.
- Europeans work fewer hours, keep their savings in banks, and vote to keep both. The way out is written down, and the politics cannot pass it.

### Essay

The gap has a shape, and the shape is a set of choices. A continent that was the birthplace of modern industry and modern science was passed, in less than a generation, by the country that fought a revolution against it. Each time a decision came, Europe took leisure over output, precaution over speed, fragmentation over unity, and dependency over independence. America, by luck and instinct more than by plan, took the opposite each time. Nothing about either path was wrong in the abstract. The European path stops working when the world changes, and the world changed.

The first choice was the currency. Eleven countries merged their money on a theory whose own author had said it needs three things. Economies that move together. Workers who can move between them. A treasury that can move money from the booming region to the struggling one. The eurozone had none of the three. For a decade cheap northern credit hid that, and the south built houses and borrowed at northern rates. When the world's banks seized up, the two continents answered in opposite directions. America recapitalized its banks in weeks, printed, and ran a deficit of a tenth of its economy. Europe, led by a country that remembers hyperinflation, cut, taxed, and raised rates in the middle of the panic. Countries that could not print their own money could only shrink their wages until their exports sold again. Their educated young left for cities that would pay them.

The second choice was energy. American drillers cracked open rock nobody thought would pay, and within a decade the country was the largest producer of oil and the largest exporter of gas, with the cheapest gas in its history. Europe closed its reactors, banned the drilling, and ran a pipeline straight to Russia, until more than half of Germany's gas came from one supplier. The invasion of Ukraine ended that supply in a single week. Gas prices multiplied, and factory electricity tripled against America's. The chemical, aluminum, fertilizer, and car plants had run on cheap gas for seventy years. They began to shut, one by one, or to rebuild in China.

The third choice was what to do with ideas. In 2000 Europe made most of the world's phones, ran the world's internet calls, and had the third largest software company on earth. Twenty-five years later each of those companies is gone, sold, or listed in New York. No European company of the first rank has been founded from scratch in half a century. The reasons stack. A founder in Berlin faces 27 legal systems where a founder in Austin faces one. The venture money is a fifth of America's and the pension funds keep almost none of it in startups. The rules written to tame the American giants raised compliance costs the giants could pay and the startups could not, so the rules entrenched the giants. More than half the founders who left, left for America.

The fourth choice was people. American universities hold endowments and research budgets an order of magnitude beyond Europe's, and they pull in over a million foreign students a year, more than half of whose unicorns have an immigrant founder. Europe took in millions too, but as asylum seekers who mostly could not enter its regulated labor market. The backlash reshaped its politics from Britain to Austria. Underneath both, births fell below replacement everywhere in the Union. The workforce is projected to lose a quarter of itself this century, while the pensions built on a young workforce stay in place. Europeans also chose their hours. They work ten fewer weeks a year than Americans, at a productivity that is often as good. They take the difference as vacation. They keep their savings in banks. Americans put every paycheck into stocks that have returned six times as much since 2000.

The prescription has been written down by the man who ran Europe's central bank. Borrow together. Unify the capital markets. Simplify the rules. Buy defense together. Get electricity to American prices. Pick skilled immigrants. Admit that the welfare state was built for a population pyramid that no longer exists. Europe has begun to respond, rearming faster than at any time since the Cold War. But every proposal needs 27 governments to agree, the governments are falling, and a year after the prescription was written, one recommendation in nine had been carried out. Its author says he is very pessimistic.

### Conclusion

So the slow agony is not a verdict on Europe's model. The model delivered shorter weeks, longer holidays, and stronger safety nets, and Europeans voted for it every time. The bill is that the comforts were paid for with slower growth, lower wages, and a smaller share of the economic future. It comes due now because the world those choices assumed has ended. In that world America paid for Europe's defense, Russia sold cheap gas, China bought the exports, and artificial intelligence was tomorrow's problem. Whether the continent adapts or drifts will be decided in Berlin, Paris, Rome, Warsaw, and Brussels in the next few years. The signs so far are mixed. No position on Europe is held on this page, because none is on record here yet.

In the 1990s a group of European finance ministers made the largest bet in European economic history: merging 11 national currencies into one. The franc, the Deutsche mark, the lira, and the peseta went. Greece had not met the entry requirements and was waved in 3 years later. The euro was born in 1999. The theory was that a continent-sized currency would make Europe a peer of the United States in finance, end the money lost to exchange, tame inflation through a central bank in Frankfurt modeled on the German one, and stand as a monument to unity.

The economist whose paper founded the theory of shared currencies won the Nobel Prize for it. He had warned decades earlier what a shared currency needs. The countries using it must be structurally similar. Workers must be able to move freely between regions. A central authority must be able to move tax revenue from booming regions to struggling ones. The eurozone at launch had almost none of that. German and Greek labor markets had almost nothing in common. A Portuguese welder could not move to Finland, because the welder did not speak Finnish. No European tax authority could take money from Germany and send it to Greece. The warning was that the euro, as designed, would amplify shocks rather than absorb them, and the warning was ignored. For 10 years it seemed to work. Cheap German credit flooded the south. Greek households took mortgages at German rates. In 2006 Spain was starting more houses a year than Germany, France, and Italy combined. Irish banks grew to 10 times the size of the Irish economy.

### The crisis

- America: 475 billion dollars for banks in 3 weeks, an 831 billion dollar stimulus, a deficit of 10% of the economy.
- Europe: austerity, and a central bank that raised rates in 2011.
- Greece shrank 26% from 2010 to 2014. Youth unemployment hit 59.5% there and 56.4% in Spain.

In September 2008 the global financial system seized up. The United States responded fast and big. Congress passed 475 billion dollars to recapitalize American banks within 3 weeks. The Federal Reserve began buying mortgage bonds by the truckload. By early 2009 an 831 billion dollar stimulus was law, and the United States ran a budget deficit equal to 10% of its economy in a single year. Europe did the opposite. Germany, remembering the hyperinflation that destroyed the Weimar Republic, demanded austerity. Its chancellor insisted that European governments cut spending, raise taxes, and shrink deficits during a financial panic. While the Federal Reserve bought trillions in bonds, the European Central Bank raised interest rates in 2011, in the middle of a debt crisis.

Greek government debt, understated for years by a series of Greek finance ministers, was revealed to be massive. Borrowing costs for Greece, Spain, Portugal, Ireland, and Italy spiked to levels that made their debts impossible to pay. None of the five could print its own currency. The only option was internal devaluation: wages, pensions, and public jobs cut until the domestic economy shrank enough for exports to compete again. Greece took three international bailouts. Between 2010 and 2014 its economy shrank by 26%, the longest peacetime recession of any developed economy on record. Youth unemployment in Greece hit 59.5%, and in Spain 56.4%. A generation of young Southern Europeans, educated and often speaking three languages, left for London, Berlin, Amsterdam, Dubai, Singapore, and Miami. Italy's economy in 2026 has still not returned to its level before the crisis. Italian real wages are lower than in 1990, and Italy is the only G7 country where workers earn less than their parents did 35 years ago. The American economy finished its recovery by 2012 and then accelerated.

A Saudi oil minister said the Americans would never get it out at a profit. By 2015 the Americans had quadrupled natural gas production and tripled oil production. Europe went the other way. In 2011, after the Fukushima accident, Germany's chancellor announced the phase-out of every German nuclear plant, and Germany banned hydraulic fracturing across most of its territory. A former German chancellor had left office in 2005 and joined the board of Russia's state gas company within weeks. His successor approved the Nord Stream pipeline, a direct underwater gas link from Russia to Germany that bypassed Poland and Eastern Europe. By 2021 Germany depended on Russia for 55% of its gas, 35% of its oil, and 50% of its hard coal. 10 European Union countries depended on Russia for more than half of their imported gas. On 24 February 2022 Russian tanks crossed into Ukraine. 3 days later Germany's chancellor pledged 100 billion euros to rebuild the German military and committed the country to weaning itself off Russian energy. European gas prices rose more than 1,000% at their peak. Industrial electricity in Germany and France surged to three times American prices. In September 2022 the Nord Stream pipelines were sabotaged by an underwater explosion, and no country has claimed responsibility. In April 2023 Germany's last three reactors, Isar 2, Neckarwestheim 2, and Emsland, went quiet on the chancellor's orders.

The bill came due in the factories. BASF, the largest chemical company on Earth, runs the largest integrated chemical complex on Earth at Ludwigshafen, with 39,000 employees, and for 70 years its logic was cheap Russian gas turned into ammonia and fertilizer. In 2023 it began shutting its plants one by one, closed 11 plants, and announced its next 10 billion dollar complex would be built in China. In the 2024 visa year 1,177,000 international students studied at American universities and contributed 55 billion dollars. A 2022 study found 55% of American unicorns had at least one immigrant founder. The heads of Nvidia and Microsoft were born abroad.

Europe takes in millions of migrants too, of a different composition. In 2015, at the peak of the Syrian war, more than 1.3 million people applied for asylum in the Union in a year. Germany took roughly 890,000, mostly from Syria, Afghanistan, and Iraq. Its chancellor said "we can do this." 78% of unemployed immigrants in Germany lacked any vocational qualification, against 38% of unemployed native Germans, and the German apprenticeship system that turns teenagers into electricians and welders is almost closed to anyone without near-native German. Immigrant children underperform on European tests at rates that have not improved in a decade. The politics followed. In 2016 the United Kingdom voted to leave, on migration above all. Alternative for Germany, founded as a fringe anti-euro party in 2013, became the second largest party in the Bundestag in 2025 and won Thuringia outright in September 2024, the first such win since the Second World War. Italy elected its first far-right prime minister since Mussolini. The Sweden Democrats became Sweden's second largest party. The Freedom Party, founded by a former SS officer, won Austria with almost 29%. America took the skilled immigrants and Europe absorbed the humanitarian caseload. A hundred thousand Indian engineers on American work visas, each earning 200,000 dollars, built some of the most valuable companies on Earth. The asylum seekers who arrived in Europe after 2015, most unable to speak a local language or enter the regulated labor market, have been net fiscal costs in most serious peer-reviewed studies.

The European Union's fertility rate fell to 1.34 births per woman in 2024, the lowest ever recorded on the continent. Italy sits at 1.18, Spain at 1.0, Malta at 1.01, and every member state is below the replacement rate of 2.1. Italy is on track to lose 12 million people by 2070. Its median age is 49, and 156,000 Italians left in a single year. Germany's population grew by 0.1% in 2024, entirely from immigration, because more native Germans died than were born. America's rate, at roughly 1.68, is higher and also falling, and American immigration keeps growing the country: the United States added roughly 53 million people between 2000 and 2025 and the Union added roughly 22 million.

The deadline nobody can negotiate is the workforce. Eurostat projects the Union's working-age population, people from 20 to 64, falling from 262 million in 2025 to 198 million by 2100, a loss of 64 million workers. In Italy the retiree-to-worker ratio could reach 90%, nine retired Italians for every 10 working ones. Every European welfare state, pension system, and health system was built on a young workforce supporting an old one. That assumption is ending. American Social Security faces a shortfall within a decade too, but America holds 335 million people under one government, one currency, one capital market, one language, one tax system, and one military command.

The young are doing worse than their parents did at the same age. A software engineer at Google's Munich office, on the same code as a counterpart in Mountain View, earns 40 to 50% less once stock is counted. A young doctor in Paris earns roughly a third of one in Boston. Spanish youth unemployment was still 25.3% in 2024. In France 33% of people aged 60 to 64 work, against 61% in Germany, so the French retire early and keep the young out to make room. A studio in central Paris costs more per square foot than most of Manhattan. Amsterdam, London, Dublin, Munich, and Stockholm are among the most expensive housing markets on Earth against local income. Zoning, land rules, and rent control stopped building while the capitals grew through the 2010s. Barcelona announced a total ban on short-term rental licenses by 2028 because its own citizens could no longer afford the city.

The culture runs the same way. A bankrupt French entrepreneur takes nine times longer to recover legally than an American one. The American president's companies filed for bankruptcy protection six times and he was elected twice, where a French entrepreneur with six filings would be barred from running any business. American startups give every engineer stock options, and European ones, legally and culturally, do not, under labor contracts so protective that the employer hesitates to hire. The average American worker logs 1,811 hours a year. The German logs 1,341, about 470 fewer, roughly 10 weeks a year, and the French, under the 35-hour week written into law in 2000, about 1,490. Productivity per hour in Germany and France is often competitive with America. Europeans take the difference as vacation rather than output, and vote to keep it that way.

The markets delivered their verdict. Since 2000 the S&P 500 has returned roughly 500% with dividends. The Euro Stoxx 50 has returned roughly 0% in price terms over the same 25 years, and the Italian index is still below its 2000 peak. 58% of American households own stocks, through retirement accounts that pour part of every paycheck into equities. In Germany about 20% do and in Italy closer to 15, and European savings sit in bank deposits and government bonds. Europe's own fund industry estimates that European households forfeited 1 to 2 trillion euros over 11 years by not investing in equities. American public markets are now about five times the size of European ones, 65% of global market value against 12%, and Europe runs 27 national exchanges and 35 listing venues with no unified capital market, every attempt since 2015 having failed.

Europe remains one of the wealthiest regions on Earth, with a life expectancy of about 82 years against 79 in the United States. Norway's sovereign fund reached 2.1 trillion dollars in late 2025, about 340,000 dollars for every Norwegian. Poland is projected to pass Japan in output per person by 2030. Ireland's tax strategy made Dublin the European base for half of American big tech, and Switzerland remains among the most innovative economies anywhere. After the 2022 invasion Europe began to rearm. By 2025 all 32 NATO members met the 2% of GDP target agreed in 2014, Poland spent more than 4%, and Germany amended its constitution in March 2025 to lift the debt brake for defense. Rheinmetall's shares rose 15-fold between February 2022 and late 2025.

The politics are not coordinated. France has had five prime ministers in under two years and its debt was cut to the lowest rating in its history. Germany's governing coalition collapsed in November 2024. Italy's prime minister is stable mostly because her opposition is more fractured. Spain's government depends on separatists whose leaders are in some cases fugitives. The European Parliament's latest election delivered historic gains to parties that want to dismantle the project. The Commission's president has proposed a competitiveness compass, a savings and investments union, and a single corporate legal regime for technology companies across all 27 states. Every one of those proposals needs the unanimous agreement of 27 governments.

Artificial intelligence is the next contest, and the account says it is already over. In 2024 American private investment in AI was 109 billion dollars and European Union investment was under 10 billion. The American Stargate project, announced in January 2025, committed 500 billion dollars over 4 years, and 400 billion of it was secured by October 2025. France announced 109 billion euros in February 2025. 70% of the world's foundation models since 2017 were built in the United States, and three American cloud companies control more than 65% of Europe's cloud market. The AI Act was restrictive enough that by late 2025 the Commission was drafting a digital simplification package to roll parts of it back. The 2024 report wrote the prescription down: common European borrowing at continental scale, a capital markets union, simpler rules, unified defense procurement, electricity at American prices, selective high-skilled immigration, and an admission that the welfare states were built for a population pyramid that no longer exists. Asked a year later whether the recommendations would be adopted, its author said he was very pessimistic.
