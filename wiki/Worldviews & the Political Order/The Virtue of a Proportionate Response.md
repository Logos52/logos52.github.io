# The Virtue of a Proportionate Response

A proportionate response matches an attack with one of roughly the same size, and its weakness is that the enemy can see it coming. "They hit an airplane, we hit a transmitter." The law of war asks something different, because its test weighs harm to civilians against the military advantage expected and says nothing about matching the enemy's last strike. A harder answer avoids the first problem, but it carries the risk of escalation.

A matched response gets priced in, as when the planned targets had stood empty for three days, and the gradual bombing of North Vietnam gave the enemy time to build up its anti-aircraft defences. The legal test in Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions bans any attack whose civilian harm is excessive against the military advantage expected. One strategy, the Dahiya doctrine, rejects proportion outright by destroying civilian infrastructure so that the population turns against the militants. The case for restraint rests on escalation, which leaders need to understand and dampen.

## The trouble with matching

A matched response is predictable, and a predictable response gets priced in. In the scene that poses the question, the planned targets are two ammo dumps, an abandoned railroad bridge and an intelligence agency, and satellites show they have been empty for three days. The enemy has already factored in the cost. When an adviser answers that a proportional response "isn't virtuous. It's all there is," the reply is that there is another option, the disproportional response.

The Vietnam War shows the same flaw in practice. The plan for bombing North Vietnam was to start at a low level and build up gradually, both to signal intent and to limit risk. Against that plan, the Joint Chiefs of Staff wanted to hit hard at once. The gradual approach failed because it gave North Vietnam time to build up its anti-aircraft defences. Deterrence works the same way, since a power that keeps a large military but never uses it invites others to call its bluff.

## What the law measures

The legal test asks a different question. Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions bans any attack expected to cause civilian deaths, injuries or damage "excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated." Proportion in law therefore means proportion between civilian harm and military gain. The same article bans reprisals against civilians and forbids using civilians to shield military targets. Planners must also do everything feasible to confirm that targets are military and must choose methods that keep civilian harm as low as possible.


## Going openly disproportionate

One military strategy rejects proportion outright. The Dahiya doctrine calls for the large-scale destruction of civilian infrastructure to pressure hostile governments, so that the civilian population turns against the militants and the enemy sues for peace. An Israeli colonel wrote that Israel "should target economic interests and the centers of civilian power that support the organization." Its name comes from the Dahieh neighbourhood of Beirut, where Hezbollah had its headquarters and which was heavily damaged.

## Why escalation is the risk

The case for restraint rests on escalation. Escalation is a natural tendency in any competition, and in war the pressure becomes intense because losing a contest of deadly force costs so much. Leaders therefore need to understand and dampen escalation of every kind, whether deliberate, accidental or inadvertent.

## Open questions

- Why does matching an enemy blow for blow feel like the fair choice to leaders?
- How does escalation feed on itself once a war starts?
- How do planners who choose an openly disproportionate doctrine expect it to change the enemy's thinking?
- How do those planners weigh the doctrine against the harm to civilians?
