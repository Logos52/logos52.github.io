# The Civil Rights Act - Freedom of Association

Freedom of association is the right of a person to choose whom he keeps company and does business with, free from force. Jim Crow laws broke that right by forcing businesses to refuse customers by race, and the Civil Rights Act of 1964 broke it the other way, by forcing businesses to serve, hire and school by group. Sixty years later America is still accused of institutional racism, racial tension is rising, and the lost freedom has not returned.

## Takeaways

- Jim Crow and the Act's service rule both tell owners whom to serve.
- Refusing a customer group can be a rational business choice.
- The Act's hiring rule made matching group shares the only safe defense.
- That burden of proof produced quotas, affirmative action and later DEI.
- School integration was enforced with troops, fines and custody threats.
- Forced association means the other side had declined it.

## Title II and the market

Title II, the section covering shops, restaurants and hotels, forced owners to serve every group. Economists long argued that businesses did not need such a law to desegregate, because an owner who turns away paying customers out of plain prejudice loses revenue to a rival who serves them. Defenders of the Act answer that segregated businesses lasted for generations anyway, and that is also true. Both facts fit together only if the refusal paid.

- Taste-based discrimination costs the owner money, so competition removes it.
- Statistical discrimination uses a group average when individual facts are missing.
- Suppose a group, on average, stole more, paid less, and harassed staff.
- Serving it would then cost more than the honest members brought in.
- Refusal would survive competition, because it paid.
- An owner following his customers' wishes raises the same question about them.

## Title VII and the burden of proof

Title VII banned discrimination in hiring, with complaints going to the EEOC, a federal agency. Nobody can see a motive, and an employer could always say a candidate fell short in some way. Courts then turned to disparate outcomes: define identity groups, compare their results, and treat any gap as discrimination. That enforces the blank-slate belief that groups do not differ, and it made proportional hiring the only defense an employer could prove.

```
complaint to the EEOC
      |
      v
employer must show no discrimination
      |
      v
only proof available: group shares match
      |
      v
hire to the numbers -> quotas, affirmative action, DEI
```

- Most complaints ended with the company paying the complainant.
- Filing a complaint cost the complainant nothing.
- A qualified candidate outside a protected group could be passed over.
- Protected groups grew from race to sex, orientation and more.

## Title IV and the schools

Title IV desegregated public schools, building on the Supreme Court's 1954 school ruling in Brown v. Board of Education. Title VI reached private schools by withholding federal funding from them, and a 1976 Supreme Court decision extended the rules to almost all education. Many parents moved their children into homeschooling or small community schools. Parents who expected more disorder, bullying and crime in integrated classrooms, and slower teaching, would act exactly this way.

- The official account blamed the parents' hatred.
- Homeschooling was then a legal grey area.
- Federal troops were sent to force attendance.
- Parents faced fines, jail or loss of custody.

## The verdict

Ending segregation by law was a fair goal, and the Act replaced it with a mandate in the other direction. A group that others value finds people willing to deal with it without force, so forced association shows what others think of the group. People today are more racially afraid and hostile than they have been in a long time.

- Freedom of association was traded away and has not come back.
- The enforcement machinery has since been extended.
- Black family figures from before and after the 1960s show no winner.
