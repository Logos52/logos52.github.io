# Moral Language as Leverage

A standard is demanded in one direction and not in the other, and the asymmetry is stable enough to be a feature rather than an oversight. Societies are told they must be diverse by people whose own remain homogeneous, told to redistribute by people who do not, told that discrimination is intolerable by people who practise it without embarrassment. Pointing this out changes nothing, which is the part worth explaining.

The usual account is hypocrisy, and it does not fit. Hypocrisy is unstable — it costs the hypocrite something when exposed, so it either gets hidden or gets abandoned. This does neither. It is exposed constantly, at no cost, and continues. That pattern indicates something other than a moral failure.

## Three ways a society keeps order

Behaviour can be regulated internally, socially, or physically, and which one predominates is a fact about a society rather than about its members.

**Guilt** is regulation by the pain of violating one's own principles. Law arrives only where that fails. This is the arrangement most legible to the people inside it and the rarest across the record: it requires that individual principles be similar enough across a population to function as a shared instrument, which in practice has meant advanced, generally religious, and substantially homogeneous societies.

**Shame** is regulation by the judgement of a group whose opinion the individual cannot escape. More common, and it needs less — but it still needs a group cohesive enough that its judgement reaches most people most of the time.

**Fear** is regulation by expected retaliation. The calculation is whether an act will be answered with force. It produces the least prosperous arrangements and it is the most robust, because it requires nothing of a population beyond the capacity to anticipate consequences.

That last property is the load-bearing one. Guilt and shame both have preconditions. Fear has none, so it is what a society reverts to when the others stop working — and the condition that stops them working is heterogeneity past the point where shared principles or a single group's judgement can reach everyone. Nothing has to be decided for this to happen. It is what remains.

## Why value attracts a claim

Anything valuable and difficult to produce creates a choice for those who want it: acquire the capacity, or acquire the thing.

Where the two parties are inside a common moral order, the second option carries costs — guilt, or the judgement of a group both belong to. Where they are not, those costs are absent, and the calculation is only about difficulty. Taking a finished thing is easier than developing the capacity to make one, and the gap in difficulty is often enormous.

This applies to craftsmanship, to infrastructure, to hard-won resources, and to functioning societies. A settlement built on marginal land by people who arrived with nothing attracts no interest while it is marginal, and attracts claims once it is not. The claims arrive in the language of entitlement and shared belonging, and they arrive at the point where there is something to claim.

**The people making them are not usefully described as villains.** They are responding to an incentive that exists whether or not anyone intends it, and a claim that costs nothing to make and might succeed is a rational claim. Treating this as wickedness misdescribes it and produces the wrong response.

## Vocabulary without the commitment underneath

If moral argument does not bind across the boundary, the question is why moral argument is the form the claims take.

Because it works. A vocabulary developed inside a guilt-regulated society is an instrument that operates on people who hold the underlying commitments, and it operates on them regardless of whether the person using it holds those commitments too. The words do the work by themselves.

That is what makes the asymmetry stable. There is no inconsistency to resolve, because consistency was never the point — a tool is not hypocritical for being used only where it works. Demanding that the standard be applied reciprocally assumes a commitment that would make the demand unnecessary if it were present.

**The test is the same one that separates confusion from selective reasoning anywhere.** Someone who holds a principle applies it when it cuts against them. Someone using it as an instrument applies it in one direction and finds reasons why the other direction is different. That test is cheap, it is falsifiable, and it does not require attributing a motive to anyone — the behaviour is observable across cases.

## What follows, and what does not

Two responses are available and only one of them is coherent.

Argument aimed at producing consistency does not work, and its failure is not a failure of eloquence. A person who does not hold the commitment cannot be argued into acting as though they do by being shown that they are not.

What remains is the assessment itself: recognising which claims are made inside a shared moral order and which are made across a boundary, and responding to each on its own terms. That is a discrimination between kinds of claim rather than between kinds of people, and it collapses if it becomes the second.

What none of this establishes is how any particular claim should be judged. A claim advanced instrumentally can still be correct, and the incentive account explains why it was made rather than whether it is right. Those are separate questions and the analysis here answers only the first.

## Open questions

A moral vocabulary this specific is not obvious from outside the tradition that produced it, and its use as an instrument spread quickly. Where was it learned, and by what route?

If fear-regulation is what a society reverts to when shared principles no longer reach everyone, is there an arrangement that holds a heterogeneous population to something better, or is the reversion structural?
