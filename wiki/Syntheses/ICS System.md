# ICS System

ICS is the learning system this wiki is organised around. It treats learning as a set of trainable habits in five areas and improves whichever area is weakest first. It explains why two people using the same method get different results, and it tells a learner where to spend practice time instead of collecting more techniques.

- Learning is habit change, so practice matters more than theory.
- Spend about five hours practising for every hour of theory.
- Five areas decide results, and the weakest one gets fixed first.
- A skill counts only when it works on a bad day.
- Teachers can give what to do, and practice builds how and when.
- Fix quick, common problems before slow, deep ones.
- Judge a technique by the thinking it produces.

## The five areas

Each area is a separate set of habits, and each can be trained. Strong thinking habits let some people succeed with poor methods, which is why copying a top student's method rarely works for someone else. Self-regulation lets a learner keep performing when the teacher, the material or the schedule is poor, and a strong thinker without it does well only when conditions are easy. Mindset interventions in research show small average effects, concentrated in lower-achieving students, so this area is trained through practice with mistakes instead of lectures about growth.

| Area | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| Deep processing | Relating, comparing and simplifying information while taking it in |
| Mindset | How you respond to mistakes, setbacks and challenge |
| Self-regulation | Noticing how you are learning and changing course |
| Retrieval | Using what you know, and slowing its decay |
| Self-management | Time, priorities, focus and procrastination |

## How it works

Change comes from turning a technique into a habit, and that takes far more practice than reading about it. The rule of thumb is one hour of theory to five of practice, and the practice replaces current study time instead of adding to it. Learning several techniques at once makes mistakes hard to trace, because each technique affects the others. Barry Zimmerman's model of self-regulated learning gives the loop the practice runs in: plan, perform, then reflect and adjust.

- Diagnose the weakest area.
- Learn one technique aimed at it.
- Practise it on real study material.
- Reflect on the thinking it produced, then adjust.
- Reassess the areas every few months.

```
diagnose weakest area --> learn one technique
        ^                         |
        |                         v
   reflect, adjust  <--  practise on real work
```

## Two rules for growth

The first rule is to turn each skill into an asset. A skill is an asset when it keeps paying off in the future, and that needs four things: you understand it, remember it, apply it correctly, and perform it consistently and easily, including when tired. Paying for tutoring or putting in extra hours can pass the next exam, and the reason those were needed is still there afterwards. The second rule is to match each kind of knowledge to its teacher: a teacher can give the declarative knowledge of what to do, while the procedural knowledge of how and the conditional knowledge of when come from your own practice.

- Asset: a skill you own and can use on a bad day.
- Not an asset: help that solves this exam and nothing after it.
- What to do: can be taught.
- How to do it: built by practice.
- When to use it: built by experience.

## Order of work

The system puts quick fixes first: about 80% of common learning problems, such as memory, scheduling and procrastination, respond to skills that pay off within days or weeks. The deeper retraining of how a person processes information comes later and can take months before it pays off. Starting the deep work while the common problems remain tends to fail, because those problems keep getting in the way.

- First: quick fixes for common problems in memory, time and focus.
- Later: slow retraining of thinking habits.
- AI tools can speed this up but cannot build understanding for you.
