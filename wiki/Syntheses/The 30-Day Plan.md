# The 30-Day Plan

The 30-Day Plan is a written plan that breaks a goal six to twelve months away into what to improve in the next thirty days, and how to protect that work from the things that usually stop it. Most people who say they have a goal have only a wish, with no list of actions behind it. The plan puts the actions, the checkpoints and the defences on paper before the month starts.

- Pick one goal six to twelve months out.
- Track the skills and habits that reach the goal.
- Hours studied and pages covered are poor measures of progress.
- Set targets you will reach even in a bad month.
- Name the barriers in advance and write a response to each.
- Change your surroundings instead of relying on willpower.
- Swap new methods into existing hours rather than adding hours.

## The steps

The plan is built backwards from the goal. You picture what a person who would certainly reach the goal is good at, compare yourself with that list, and pick the few gaps that matter most this month. Each gap gets a target for day fourteen and day thirty. The rest of the plan protects those targets from the things that usually break them: bad time slots, automatic habits and distractions within reach.

```
goal, 6 to 12 months away
  -> skills that produce it
    -> 2 or 3 gaps to close this month
      -> day-14 and day-30 targets
        -> barriers named, responses written
```

- The goal must be concrete enough to picture and to measure.
- Less than six months leaves too little time to change the result.
- Past twelve months the result is too hard to predict.
- For a goal that far out, keep only a direction.
- List the skills: time management, focus, study efficiency, consistency.
- Add stress handling, sleep, and the people who support you.
- Rate yourself on each and choose two or three to work on.
- Write each target as a before and after you can check.
- The fourteen-day target should look unimpressive.

## Measures that mean something

A checkpoint is useful only if reaching it predicts the result. Five hours with a poor study method can produce less than two hours with a good one, so hours tell you little. A checkpoint on a skill does predict the result. For time management, a starting point might be planning three days a week and following the plan a fifth of the time, with a thirty-day target of four or five days followed more than half the time.

- Poor measures: hours, pages covered, flashcards made, notes written.
- Useful measures: how well you use a skill that produces the result.
- The skills are under your control and they produce the result.

## Defences

Plans fail in execution more often than in intent. Important work put in a slot where it is often dropped, such as early morning before an early-rising habit exists or straight after a tiring day, rarely happens. Habits that fire in under a second cannot be stopped as they start, so the plan prepares a small response for the moment you notice one. Your surroundings trigger most behaviour, so changing them does more than resisting each urge.

- Put the most important work in your least disturbed time.
- A risky slot gets a short energising activity just before it.
- Find the habits that fire under stress, tiredness, time pressure or bad mood.
- Watch for quietly simplifying a hard technique until it stops working.
- The planned response takes almost no effort, such as phone face down.
- Then choose the next action on purpose.
- Make distractions hard to reach: uninstall, block, bury the phone.
- Keep a log of what triggered each lapse and plan a counter.
- A sign on the door stops interruptions where asking did not.

## Pace

New methods go into the hours you already study, in place of old methods. Adding the new methods on top of the old ones overloads the week. Spend at most one hour on theory for every five hours of practice. Some learners do well with forty hours of practice for each hour of theory.

## Where it fails

Three failures account for most broken plans. Too few automatic habits get identified, so the ones that matter go unanswered. The written responses ask for too much motivation, so they are not used when needed. The surroundings are adjusted a little and then left, so the plan still depends on willpower.
