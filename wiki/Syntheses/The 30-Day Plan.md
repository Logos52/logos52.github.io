# The 30-Day Plan

The 30-Day Plan writes a 6–12 month target, attribute checkpoints at thirty days and fourteen, and a first-person cue-response script for every predictable barrier, ahead of the work. Intention — declaring the want, feeling the commitment, printing the poster — produces outcomes only by luck. The name collides with [[30-Day Challenges]]: those are pre-built practice arcs a plan can schedule. This page is the goal-agnostic protocol. The 30 days mark the first measurable checkpoint, not the whole horizon.

A high-propensity plan, defined by that first sentence, converts a complicated goal into a medium-term target, attribute-based checkpoints, three layers of pre-answered barriers, and a sustainable pace. Run it whenever a goal deserves dedicated planning time. A notes file is enough.

## Anchor, then step

**A few weeks out, missing months of preparation cannot be regrown.** Deadline-to-deadline living keeps each next challenge equally underprepared. Sometimes the strong move is writing off the immediate goal so the next one goes differently.

**Past a year, detailed plans rot.** Skills and perspective change. Hold only a general direction there. Six to twelve months leaves room to prepare, experiment, reflect, and rebuild. That window is a house default, not a finding.

**One or two goals, specific enough to picture and decompose, without obsession.** A grade, a named position, "use the language 80% of the time with family." Precision past that returns little. SMART is fine for beginners and not enough here. [[Reverse Goal Setting]] holds the goal loosely and checks that it still serves the outcome underneath. Budget a deliberate short-term dip: two or three weeks of groundwork with no visible gains. Refusing to trade anything short-term is the pattern that keeps results ordinary.

The pipeline: a 6–12 month target, then attribute-based performance goals stepped to 30 days and 14 days, then barrier pre-emption, then pace, then execute, review, replan.

**Checkpoints attach to attributes, not to hours or pages.** Picture a person certain to hit the goal. List the attributes that make that success near-inevitable. Rate against them. Work at most two or three gaps. Attacking seven tends to fail all of them. Attributes the hours-and-pages list usually skips: time management, focus, efficiency and consistency as distinct, stress / willingness to stay with discomfort, sleep, a support network, plus what the specific goal actually requires. Stress cuts the other attributes. Hours-and-pages metrics mislead. The full method lives on [[Performance Goals]].

Each chosen attribute gets a starting number and a slightly better 30-day state. Three scheduled days at one-in-five follow-through becomes four or five days at three-in-five.

**The next cut is two weeks, sized so a bad week still clears it.** A good week should overshoot by a wide margin. That mark is allowed to look unimpressive. Momentum is the job; an early miss is cheap to fix.

The outcome is a symptom. The process is the only thing under direct control. Results are not promised.

## Pre-answer the failures

**A challenging goal contains barriers by definition.** The plan records a specific counter-action for each. An entry reading "I'll try my best" counts as blank.

**The scripted-action form is *when I notice X, I will do Y*, first person, written before the session.** Limiting habits fire as sub-second autopilot replies to five conditions — time pressure, stress, fatigue, bad mood, being pushed outside the comfort zone — too fast to prevent. Write what actually happens in each condition before composing the script. Near-zero-effort Y is what makes the script runnable at habit speed: phone face-down with the audio still playing; decide over a glass of water. Watch technique drift hardest: under discomfort a new method silently mutates back toward the familiar one, bypassing exactly the part that works. [[How to Unlearn Old or Bad Habits Efficiently]] supplies the cue-response mechanics.

**Willpower forms no barrier around a scheduled block.** Keep important work out of high-risk slots — early mornings never yet demonstrated, the post-commute energy crater — and park trivial work there. When a risky slot is unavoidable, install rails: a five-minute low-willpower energizer, automated do-not-disturb, a conditioned focus space. A calendar picture is not a plan. Only the lived attempt to run the block counts. [[Building a Schedule That Survives]] is the full defense kit.

**Behavior tracks environment more than resolve.** Willpower goes into removing triggers and adding friction — uninstalls, router-level blocks with the new password handed to someone else, the phone buried in the wardrobe — and into reshaping other people's habits. A door sign interrupts the walking-in habit that years of asking never touched. Existing reluctance is the friction: make the unwanted behaviour take more effort than will be spent. [[Environment Design]] carries the model.

Three standard failures: too few limiting habits identified; scripted actions that demand real motivation; environment work abandoned after one do-not-disturb toggle. The section deserves hours. Pre-answered barriers are what make the path predictable. The field cousin of that pre-emption is wish, outcome, obstacle, plan.

**New methods replace old ones inside hours already committed.** Bolting extra practice onto an unchanged routine doubles the load and starves both. Cap theory at 1:5 as a house default: 25 weekly study hours buys at most five for theory, often still too much. Ratios as lean as 40:1 keep producing strong progress, because most learning happens offline on real tasks. Neither ratio is a finding. [[Rapid Skill Acquisition]] calibrates by the automaticity signal rather than a fixed number.

**Plans expire on purpose.** Rerun the whole protocol after each 30-day arc. Attributes pooled from the last cycle are first in line.

"This does not apply to me" is the high-achiever exemption — the line that skips the fundamentals and then watches the method fail.

The case against the protocol is that it is slow to write and will feel like delay. It is the wrong tool for a goal a few weeks out that cannot be regrown. It is useless if the scripts stay at "I'll try my best." The price is hours on barriers, plus a two-to-three-week dip with no visible gains. Quit if two 30-day reruns produce no attribute movement. The checkable expectation is a two-week step that still looks small and still got done.

## The plan expires

The plan is the written answers, and it expires on purpose.
