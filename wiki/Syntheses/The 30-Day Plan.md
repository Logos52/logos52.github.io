# The 30-Day Plan

The 30-Day Plan is a planning protocol. Before the work starts, you write a 6–12 month target, checkpoints on attributes at thirty days and at fourteen days, and a first-person cue-response script for every barrier you can predict. Declaring the want, feeling the commitment, and printing the poster are intention. Intention produces outcomes only by luck.

A high-propensity plan turns a complicated goal into a medium-term target, attribute-based checkpoints, three layers of pre-answered barriers, and a pace you can keep. Run it whenever a goal deserves dedicated planning time. A notes file is enough. The protocol works for any goal.

The name collides with [[30-Day Challenges]]. Those are pre-built practice arcs a plan can schedule. The 30 days mark the first measurable checkpoint, not the whole horizon.

## Core takeaways

- The target, the checkpoints, and the barrier scripts all get written before the work starts. Declaring the want and feeling committed produce outcomes only by luck.
- Set the target 6–12 months out, and keep it to one or two goals specific enough to picture and decompose.
- Checkpoints measure attributes, not hours or pages. Work at most two or three gaps at once.
- Every predictable barrier gets a first-person script in the form *when I notice X, I will do Y*, written before the session.
- Protected blocks and a changed environment do more than resolve does. Willpower goes into removing triggers and adding friction.
- The plan is built to expire. Rerun the whole protocol after each 30-day arc.

## Setting the target

A few weeks out, missing months of preparation cannot be regrown. Living deadline to deadline keeps each next challenge equally underprepared. Sometimes the better choice is to write off the immediate goal so the next one goes differently.

Detailed plans made more than a year ahead stop being usable, because skills and perspective change. Hold only a general direction that far out. Six to twelve months leaves room to prepare, experiment, reflect, and rebuild. That window is a house default, not a finding.

Pick one or two goals, specific enough to picture and decompose, without obsession. A grade, a named position, "use the language 80% of the time with family". More precision than that returns little. SMART is fine for beginners and not enough here. [[Reverse Goal Setting]] holds the goal loosely and checks that it still serves the outcome underneath.

Budget a short-term dip on purpose: two or three weeks of groundwork with no visible gains. People who refuse to trade anything short-term get ordinary results.

The order of work: a 6–12 month target, then attribute-based performance goals stepped to 30 days and 14 days, then barrier pre-emption, then pace, then execute, review, replan.

## Checkpoints on attributes, not hours or pages

Checkpoints attach to attributes, not to hours or pages. Picture a person certain to hit the goal. List the attributes that make that success near-inevitable. Rate yourself against them. Work at most two or three gaps. Attacking seven tends to fail all seven.

The attributes an hours-and-pages list usually skips: time management, focus, efficiency and consistency as separate things, stress and willingness to stay with discomfort, sleep, a support network, plus what the specific goal actually requires. Stress lowers the other attributes. Hours-and-pages metrics mislead. The full method is on [[Reverse Goal Setting|Performance Goals]].

Each chosen attribute gets a starting number and a slightly better 30-day state. Three scheduled days at one-in-five follow-through becomes four or five days at three-in-five.

The next cut is two weeks, sized so a bad week still clears it. A good week should pass it by a wide margin. That mark is allowed to look unimpressive. The job is to keep going, and a miss this early is cheap to fix.

The outcome is a symptom of the process. The process is the only thing under direct control. Results are not promised.

## Writing an answer for every barrier

A challenging goal contains barriers by definition. The plan records a specific counter-action for each. An entry reading "I'll try my best" counts as blank.

The scripted-action form is *when I notice X, I will do Y*, first person, written before the session. Limiting habits run as automatic replies in under a second, and they answer five conditions: time pressure, stress, fatigue, bad mood, and being pushed outside the comfort zone. They fire too fast to prevent. Write what actually happens in each condition before composing the script. The Y has to take near-zero effort, or the script cannot run at habit speed: phone face-down with the audio still playing, or deciding over a glass of water. Technique drift is the hardest one to catch. Under discomfort a new method changes back toward the familiar one, and exactly the part that works gets skipped. [[How to Unlearn Old or Bad Habits Efficiently]] supplies the cue-response mechanics.

## Protecting the blocks you schedule

Willpower does not protect a scheduled block. Keep important work out of high-risk slots: early mornings you have never yet demonstrated, or the energy crater after the commute. Park trivial work in those slots instead. When a risky slot is unavoidable, add supports: a five-minute low-willpower energizer, automated do-not-disturb, a conditioned focus space. A block drawn on a calendar is not a plan. Only the lived attempt to run the block counts. [[Priority 0+1 System|Building a Schedule That Survives]] holds the full defense kit.

## Changing the environment

Behavior tracks environment more than resolve. Willpower goes into removing triggers and adding friction: uninstalls, router-level blocks with the new password handed to someone else, the phone buried in the wardrobe. It also goes into reshaping other people's habits. A door sign interrupts the walking-in habit that years of asking never touched. The reluctance that already exists is the friction. Make the unwanted behaviour take more effort than will be spent. [[Environment Design]] carries the model.

## Three ways the barrier work fails

The three standard failures: too few limiting habits identified, scripted actions that demand real motivation, and environment work abandoned after one do-not-disturb toggle. This part of the plan deserves hours. Pre-answered barriers are what make the path predictable. A related method used in the field is wish, outcome, obstacle, plan.

## Fitting a new method into hours already committed

New methods replace old ones inside hours already committed. Adding practice to an unchanged routine doubles the load and starves both. Cap theory at 1:5 as a house default. Out of 25 weekly study hours, at most five go to theory, and five is often still too much. Ratios as lean as 40:1 keep producing strong progress, because most learning happens offline on real tasks. Neither ratio is a finding. [[Rapid Skill Acquisition]] calibrates by the automaticity signal rather than a fixed number.

## The plan expires

The plan is the written answers, and it is built to expire. Rerun the whole protocol after each 30-day arc. Attributes pooled from the last cycle are first in line.

## What it costs and when to stop

"This does not apply to me" is the high-achiever exemption. Saying it skips the fundamentals, and then the method fails.

The protocol is slow to write and will feel like delay. It is the wrong tool for a goal a few weeks out that cannot be regrown. It is useless if the scripts stay at "I'll try my best". The price is hours on barriers, plus a two-to-three-week dip with no visible gains. Quit if two 30-day reruns produce no movement in the attributes. What you can check: a two-week step that still looks small and still got done.

## How to practice this

1. Write one or two goals for a point 6–12 months out. Make each one specific enough to picture and decompose. Notice whether you can picture a person certain to hit it.
2. List the attributes that make that success near-inevitable. Rate yourself against them. Pick the two or three largest gaps and leave the rest alone.
3. Give each chosen attribute a starting number and a slightly better 30-day state. Then set the two-week cut. Notice whether a bad week still clears it.
4. Write a script for each predictable barrier, in the form *when I notice X, I will do Y*. Notice any entry that needs real motivation to run.
5. Move important work out of your high-risk slots and park trivial work there. Notice whether you ran the block, not whether it sits on the calendar.
6. Remove one trigger and add friction to one unwanted behaviour. Notice whether the behaviour now takes more effort than you will spend.
