# Argument Validation

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

An argument is valid when its form rules out true premises alongside a false conclusion. It is sound only when it is valid and every premise is actually true. Checking an argument therefore takes two separate questions, whether the conclusion follows and whether the starting points hold. The first question needs only the argument's form, while the second needs the world, since a valid argument with a false premise is valid but unsound. Many arguments also hide a premise called the warrant, which links the evidence to the point being made, so finding that hidden link is part of the check. Each test works on any argument, whatever it is about. Together they cover misleading numbers and words, the person making the argument, your own judgment and the way you present your own case.

The two questions call for different work. Validity depends on form alone. Take the argument that Elizabeth owns one of two cars, a Honda or a Saturn, that she does not own the Honda and that she therefore owns the Saturn. If the first two lines are right the last one has to be right, whatever car she really owns. Truth is still worth checking, because the closer someone's picture of a business, a channel or a relationship is to the truth, the better that person does at it.

A fair test also gets the other side right before attacking it. The critic first restates the opposing position so well that its holder would welcome the wording, then lists points of agreement, and only then offers a rebuttal. Being offended is no part of that test.

Each page below gives a test that works on any argument. The pages that test one particular belief sit in [[wiki/Worldviews & the Political Order/Worldviews & the Political Order|Worldviews & the Political Order]].

## When true facts lead to a wrong conclusion

- [[wiki/Worldviews & the Political Order/Validity and Truth|Validity and Truth]]: Valid and sound are separate checks, and finding the hidden premise is part of both. A useful model breaks an argument into six parts, the claim, grounds, warrant, qualifier, rebuttal and backing, where the first three do most of the work and the warrant is the assumption linking the grounds to the claim. A question can hide a premise the same way, as when it assumes an opinion is bigoted before asking about it.

## Numbers and words that mislead

- [[wiki/Worldviews & the Political Order/Per Capita|Per Capita]]: Per capita means per person, the plain division that turns a raw count into a rate. A large group usually has a large count of almost anything, so a raw total says little until it is divided by the size of the group. The rate shows how common something is without explaining why it happens, and controlling for other variables is the one fair objection to using it.
- [[wiki/Worldviews & the Political Order/Who Gets Called Racist|Who Gets Called Racist]]: The case made here is that the word racist ends an argument rather than answering it. Judging by group averages is treated as a tool that is sometimes the only option when information is short, and the right reply to the charge is to ask whether you are right.
- [[wiki/Concepts/Bias and Framing|Bias and Framing]]: Bias is a lopsided weight for or against an idea, and it differs from plain error because it is systematic and can be prevented. Framing is the way information is presented, and a frame supplies the starting tilt that a person rarely gets the feedback to correct.
- [[wiki/Red Team/The Twitter Test|The Twitter Test]]: The test finds the themes in an unfamiliar text by pretending the writer worked under a strict character limit, so every word kept was chosen for a reason. For each word it asks why that exact word is there and what the writer wants you to feel.

## The person making the argument

- [[wiki/Worldviews & the Political Order/Moral Language as Leverage|Moral Language as Leverage]]: The case made here is that words like racism and discrimination get aimed at the West by people who do not apply them at home, because the words win resources and better treatment. Its conclusion is that asking for consistency achieves nothing, so the useful response is to stop being moved by the words.

## Your own judgment

- [[wiki/Red Team/Applied Critical Thinking - Testing Frames|Applied Critical Thinking - Testing Frames]]: Critical thinking is a way to read the games already being played around you, practised like a martial-arts form until the responses are there under pressure. You treat your view as the least-bad option so far, and listen for a better alternative over a list of flaws.
- [[wiki/Red Team/Epistemic Exceptionalism|Epistemic Exceptionalism]]: Epistemic exceptionalism treats your own reasoning as the reference point for everything else, so disagreement always points to someone else's failure. Your own judgment becomes the one part of the loop never tested for error.
- [[wiki/Concepts/Levels of Thinking - The Step Back|Levels of Thinking - The Step Back]]: A level of thinking is one step back from your own reaction, and there are nine of them, from your own needs up to watching your own mind build a reaction. The way up is to sit with a reaction and ask why until you reach where it came from.

## Your own arguments

- [[wiki/Concepts/Five Thinking Habits - Conclusion First|Five Thinking Habits - Conclusion First]]: Five speaking habits, starting with saying the conclusion first, make a person sound clearer. With practice they also make the person think better.
- [[wiki/Concepts/How to Communicate Truth Into Someone Else's Frame|How to Communicate Truth Into Someone Else's Frame]]: Truth reaches another person when they trust that you see their interests and will say what you actually believe. The work starts by reasoning your way to their position and saying yes where it is valid, then adding why your own view also holds.
