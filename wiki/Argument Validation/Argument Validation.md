# Argument Validation

An argument is a conclusion and the facts offered for it. Checking one means asking two separate things: whether the facts are true, and whether the conclusion follows from them if they are. Each page below describes one such check, kept apart from any one belief so it can be used on any argument, including your own.

## Takeaways

- One false fact is enough to make an argument fail.
- True facts can still fail to support the conclusion.
- Whether a conclusion follows is the same for every reader.
- Some checks catch a particular move, such as a total used as a rate.
- Some checks are run on yourself before you accept a claim.
- Pages that apply these checks to one belief sit in the wiki section called Worldviews.

## What the section holds

The checks fall into four groups. The first sets the rules, meaning what makes an argument fail at all. The second holds checks that each catch a particular move, such as a loaded word or a raw count. The third is about the reader's own reaction to a claim, and the fourth is about talking to someone who holds a different view.

- The rules of the check
  - [[wiki/Worldviews & the Political Order/Validity and Truth|Validity and Truth]]: both ways an argument fails, and five common breaks.
- Checks on a single move
  - [[wiki/Worldviews & the Political Order/Per Capita|Per Capita]]: a total shows size, a rate per person shows how common.
  - [[wiki/Concepts/Bias and Framing|Bias and Framing]]: swap the charged word for a flat one and see if a fact changes.
  - [[wiki/Red Team/The Twitter Test|The Twitter Test]]: in short text, ask why each word was chosen.
  - [[wiki/Worldviews & the Political Order/Moral Language as Leverage|Moral Language as Leverage]]: whether the speaker applies the principle when it costs them.
  - [[wiki/Worldviews & the Political Order/Who Gets Called Racist|Who Gets Called Racist]]: whether the judgment changes once individual facts arrive.
- Checks on yourself
  - [[wiki/Red Team/Applied Critical Thinking - Testing Frames|Applied Critical Thinking]]: pause, then find the assumption the conclusion needs.
  - [[wiki/Red Team/Epistemic Exceptionalism|Epistemic Exceptionalism]]: confidence that names nothing that would change it.
  - [[wiki/Concepts/Levels of Thinking - The Step Back|Levels of Thinking]]: the reaction comes first, so ask why until you reach its source.
  - [[wiki/Concepts/Five Thinking Habits - Conclusion First|Five Thinking Habits]]: state the conclusion first, and what would change it.
- Talking to the other side
  - [[wiki/Concepts/How to Communicate Truth Into Someone Else's Frame|How to Communicate Truth]]: explain in their terms, starting from their problem.
