# How Foreign Words Become Chinese

When a foreign name enters Mandarin, it is rewritten one syllable at a time in Chinese characters picked mainly for their sound. The characters keep their own meanings and their own tones, so every borrowed name also gets a meaning, usually nonsense, and a set of tones the original never had. That is why Coca-Cola reads as tasty and joyful in Chinese, why English speakers often fail to recognise their own names in Mandarin, and why Taiwan and the Mainland write the same name differently.

## Takeaways

- Each foreign syllable becomes one character chosen for sound.
- A small set of characters is used again and again.
- Read one by one, those characters usually mean nothing together.
- Every Mandarin syllable needs a tone, so the name gains tones.
- The tone comes from whichever character was picked.
- Taiwan and the Mainland often pick different characters.

## How it works

Mandarin allows no consonant clusters, two or more consonants run together, and only a few consonants at the end of a syllable, so a foreign word is first broken into syllables that Mandarin allows. A cluster like dr- splits into two syllables, and a final -s gets a syllable of its own, so words grow longer. Each syllable is then matched to a character from a table, and in the table a given sound always maps to the same character. The character brings its tone with it, and the original word had none.

```
Boralus   (3 syllables, no tones)
   |  split, add a syllable for -s
bo - ra - lu - s
   |  pick a character per sound
波   拉   勒   斯      bō lā lè sī
(wave, pull, bridle, this: no meaning)
```

- Final -r or -l becomes 爾.
- Final -s becomes 斯, whether it sounds like s or z.
- Final -k becomes 克, final -m 姆, final -t 特.
- The d sound becomes 德.
- 麥克風, microphone, has nothing to do with wheat or wind.

## The tones

Many learners assume that borrowed words take the first tone, the level one, and the counts show no such rule. In a study of about 1,400 English loanwords, the first tone appeared on about 29 percent of syllables, against about 27 percent in Mandarin as a whole. The tone follows the character, and the character is chosen for its sound, its meaning and how common it is.

| Word | Characters | Tones |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coca-Cola | 可口可樂 | 3-3-3-4 |
| druid | 德魯伊 | 2-3-1 |
| Boralus | 波拉勒斯 | 1-1-4-1 |

- The syllable added for a final s is almost always sī, first tone.
- Of 251 added first-tone syllables, 195 were sī.
- Some syllables exist in only one tone, so there is no choice.

## Sound and meaning together

For brands, translators often pick characters that match the sound and also say something good. Coca-Cola became 可口可樂, where 可口 is an existing word for tasty and 樂 means joy. The popular story that the name won a prize contest in the 1930s is false. 可口 appears in Shanghai drink advertising from 1923, and the full name was in print by 1930, the year the company filed the trademark. The name "bite the wax tadpole" was never an official Coca-Cola name.

- Hacker: 駭客 in Taiwan, with 駭 meaning to frighten.
- UFO: 幽浮, roughly ghostly float.
- Viagra: 威而剛, roughly mighty and firm.

## Taiwan and the Mainland

The Mainland has one official naming office, set up in 1956 inside Xinhua, the state news agency, and its dictionary of world names holds about 650,000 entries. Taiwan has no binding standard. Government reference books and the Central News Agency's name database of about 122,000 entries guide usage, and custom settles the rest. So the two sides often write the same name differently, and a different first character can change the tone.

| Name | Taiwan | Mainland |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Obama | 歐巴馬 | 奧巴馬 |
| Trump | 川普 | 特朗普 |
| Sydney | 雪梨 | 悉尼 |
| Boralus | 波拉勒斯 | 伯拉勒斯 |
