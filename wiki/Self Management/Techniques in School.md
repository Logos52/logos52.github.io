# Techniques in School

A class that demands linear notes, or that buries you in homework, can still leave room for methods that actually build knowledge. The aim is to protect time for that work without picking a fight you do not need.

## Working inside the classroom

If the teacher is not checking, use the method you came to use. Linear or passive notes can stay on the surface while you work in your own way underneath.

If that is not possible, spend as little time as you can on unweighted assignments so more of the day is left for real study. Time-boxing, below, is the tool for that.

Class can also be a place to find gaps. Students who have already done a first pass through the material can treat the hour as a live quiz. They can look for what is still unclear and ask questions they would not have thought to ask while sitting passively.

Homework that is mostly a set of questions can be done in class when you already know the material or find it easy.

## Time-boxing

Time-blocking puts a task on the calendar. Time-boxing adds a hard stop. If you set 1 hour aside, the hour ends when it ends. The box is most useful for two kinds of work. It fits tasks that matter a great deal and cannot be finished in one sitting, and tasks that barely matter but still have to be done.

Improving how you learn is often important and not urgent. A boxed session lets you make progress on it without waiting for a free afternoon that never comes.

Compulsory homework that does not count toward a final grade is the other case. You have to do it, and it should not take more time than it is worth. The aim is the best result the box will allow, sometimes called a best-for-time outcome, which also helps against the urge to polish past the point of value.

Two regular patterns explain why the box works. Parkinson's law says a task expands or shrinks to fill the time given. Give it a long stretch and it will use a long stretch. Give it a short one, within reason, and it still gets done. The Pareto principle says about 80 percent of a result often comes from about 20 percent of the work. The same split shows up in how much of a subject actually drives learning, and in how much time certain distractions consume. It also shows up in how much of an essay is written in a small share of the total time.

An Eisenhower matrix sorts tasks by importance and urgency. It shows where an unimportant but urgent task belongs, so that task does not eat the boxed time meant for something that matters more.

## When you can negotiate, and when you cannot

If the teacher seems open to it, you can describe the methods you are trying and ask to use them. Students who have already shown they know the material have sometimes been allowed to study on their own during class.

If none of that works, and you must sit through a class that teaches you nothing new, reduce how deep your first pass through the material is before you arrive. A shallower first pass leaves more to figure out in the room, which raises the mental load of the hour and makes it more engaging. The time you would have spent on a deeper first pass can then go to interleaved retrieval instead. The remaining choice is to keep ahead and accept that class will be dull. Being bored because you already know the material is a good problem to have.

## Open questions

How much of today's homework is actually worth more than the boxed time I would give it?

How can I tell whether a shallower first pass would make class useful, or would only leave me lost?
