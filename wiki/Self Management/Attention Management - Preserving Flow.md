# Attention Management: Preserving Flow

Attention Management runs the day as an ordered list between the day's fixed anchors — leaving the house, dinner, sleep — so a finished block starts the next item instead of a planning reopen. Attention Management scores the day by whether the intended work arrived with the thread still intact; time management scores it by whether the clock was obeyed.

## Direction and depth

Attention is the direction of the mind. Focus is how deeply and steadily that direction holds. The two fail separately, which is why a calendar full of "study" blocks can still produce nothing the day needed.

Strong focus aimed at the wrong object is one failure: deep absorption in a side task, over-polishing a finished draft, or avoidance work that feels like progress because the mind is locked on something. That is focus without this page. The other failure is the reverse: the right task is already chosen, and attention still skips — messages, rereading without processing, stopping every few minutes. That is a correctly aimed day with no depth. The aim is both: pointed at the thing that matters, and held long enough that something comes out.

A working task list has to exist before any of this is useful. Attention Management sits downstream of basic time and task structure; without that structure the day is already chaos, and loosening the clock only names the chaos "flow." [[wiki/Self Management/Priority 0+1 System|Priority 0+1 System]] is the evening sort that names one to three priorities and protects the first of them. [[wiki/Self Management/Task Management|Task Management]] is the intake that keeps the list from being a wish. Conventional time management still earns its keep here: it structures the day, protects those priorities, and stops a fantasy calendar. What it cannot see is a two-hour "study" block that was obeyed on the clock and spent on distraction, switching, setup, decision gaps, or low-energy work. Task completion can be equally hollow — the right items ticked, none of them taken far enough. Focus Management can still produce a good block and leave the next one unstarted. This page asks whether intentional attention survived the chain. Used too early, that question goes vague.

## How a day actually runs

A day has a small number of fixed anchors: leave the house, dinner, sleep, any appointment that involves other people. Between those anchors the clock carries no information. Something will fill the stretch either way; the only question is whether it is the next item on an ordered list. The schedule between anchors is that list, worked top-down. The next item starts the moment the previous one ends. That is what "loosening rigid task timing" actually is — not a mood, a replacement of mid-day clock times with an order.

Knowing where attention should be, noticing when it has left, removing the gap after a task ends, preparing the room before the block, and bridging to the next piece with the answers already written: those five motions are the technique. The gap after a task ends is a [[wiki/Decision Making/Decisional Delays|Decisional Delay]] — minutes that look small and a rebuild that is not. The rest of the setup is the same frontload the neighbouring pages already give at length: ranked list, next action named, materials open, distractions blocked, a stopping line written, a fallback ready for a small unexpected hole. [[wiki/Decision Making/Choice Throttling|Choice Throttling]] is that list under another name. Stopping conditions stay visible here because they are what later stop a good block from becoming an endless one.

A calendar can say "study" for two hours. That is not two hours of study. The nominal block can hide distraction, procrastination, task switching, setup friction, decision gaps, and low-energy work. The leak list matters most on a heavy day, a hard subject, or a day already cut into many pieces.

The frontload is inherited. Two rules belong only here. **Overrun.** If a priority is going well and the output target is not yet reached, the block continues past the clock mark rather than being cut for an arbitrary boundary. Nothing downstream was promised to that mark. **Underrun.** If the task is finished early, the next suitable item starts immediately. Planning is not reopened.

The transition is the danger zone. A bridge is five answers written before the current block ends:

1. What is the next task?
2. What is the first action?
3. What materials does it need?
4. What should be ignored?
5. What counts as enough progress?

The fourth is the non-obvious one. The fifth is the day's scorecard asked at the seam, so the next block knows when to stop. When those five are already available, the next task starts without a planning reopen. A small "what now?" gap is where attention leaks into messages, browsing, food, chores, or easier work — and easier work is the one that still feels like a day well spent.

The day is also not only work blocks. Reclaimable stretches are the times the body is occupied and the mind is idle: walking somewhere, showering, getting changed, standing in a queue. Attention in those stretches defaults to a phone unless a small self-contained task has already been assigned — a handful of cards, one recall attempt, a mental map of what just happened, the plan for the next piece. Dead zones are the counterpart: class, a sport, a meal with people. Those take full attention and are not doubled up. Without the counterpart, reclaiming becomes a licence to split everything.

## What is being scored

The scorecard is intended output plus how long useful attention held, not whether every calendar block started and ended on time. Quality of the intended piece, duration of useful hold, count of unplanned snaps, minutes lost at the seams, frequency of drift from the intended object, and whether a pause restored attention or opened a new leak: those six are the reading.

Those questions stay decorative until there is an instrument. Two are cheap. In the moment: *where is attention right now, and where is it intended to be?* Asking it is the training. A break that was intended is attention where it was meant to be; a break that was not is the leak. At the end of the day: a short pass over the seams — where did a finished task sit while the next one was still being chosen? The first days find nothing. The looking is what makes the finding possible. A single clean pass that reports no problem has been run too early, not too well. The neighbouring Decisional Delays page frames a week-long audit of the same seams; the daily reflex is cheaper and closer to the noticing this page is trying to build.

Efficiency on a task climbs over the first several minutes rather than starting full. A break that lets the mind let go returns the next stretch to the start of that climb. A glance that never leaves the work costs almost nothing; a detour that does can spend the rest of the block on re-entry. The cost is set by how fast the return happens, and it compounds: slow re-entry also shortens the windows that follow, so the productive share of the same three hours collapses twice. That is why the seams are worth this much attention.

## When the clock still binds

The technique fits a day that needs control over transitions, task order, and flow protection — and a person who already knows the priorities and wants output without unnecessary disruption. It is not a claim that a flow-driven day is the fastest possible day.

The clock still binds in five situations, and those take a hard time-box instead:

- the task could expand endlessly
- a deadline requires a hard stop
- the person is prone to stay inside a comfortable polish
- the task is low-value
- the day is already cut by many external appointments

**Parkinson Pressure** is a self-imposed finish time used as a tactical push: an expected completion is set before the block starts, the work aims at it, and an early finish moves on. Work stretches to fill whatever window is left open; a named end compresses it. Constant timers create stress and thin the quality of attention. They are a push for a specific task, not the atmosphere of the day.

|---|---|---|
| Too advanced too early | Time structure is abandoned and the leftover is called flow | The rungs first: Priority 0+1, then a working task list, then this page |
| Vague flow chasing | Whatever feels engaging gets the next hour | Priorities and output targets stay explicit |
| Transition collapse | Each ending opens a planning gap | The five bridge answers are written before the ending |
| Perfectionism | The block continues because staying in flow feels like producing | Stopping conditions plus a tactical finish time |
| Disrupted day | Meetings and appointments keep snapping the thread | Short reprioritization points planned before and after each disruption |

## What this sits on, and what it costs

[[wiki/Self Management/Focus Management - How to Enter & Recover Inside a Work Block|Focus Management: Training the Return Mechanism]] creates the block: entry, noticing drift, return speed. This page organises the day so those blocks connect instead of collapsing into repeated resets. [[wiki/Self Management/Flow State|Flow State]] is the condition both are trying to keep — full absorption in one task, where attention holds without being forced. The interruption-cost breakdown and the entry conditions live there. [[wiki/Self Management/Procrastination - a System Problem|Procrastination: a System Problem]] is what a "what now?" gap becomes when the leak is avoidance rather than indecision; the five diagnostic questions stay on that page.

The technique is powered by a prioritisation system, not merely compatible with one. A good evening sort leaves the day feeling *loose*, with more slack between items than expected. Attention Management is what makes that slack usable rather than wasted. Without the sort, "flow from one priority to the next" has no content. The vault's rungs are Priority 0+1 and Task Management; this page is the one that comes after they already run.

Rest is part of the same technique, not an appendix to it. [[wiki/Self Management/OFF-Rest Timing|OFF-Rest Timing]] is the rule: stop when focus turns dull rather than tired, rest about one third of the elapsed work, and expect the cycles to shorten as the day goes on. The failure mode of "preserve flow" is reading it as "do not stop." A break chosen on the dull-not-tired rule is not a flow break. [[wiki/Self Management/The Energize Test|The Energize Test]] is the matching question for which work belongs in which stretch of energy.

The strongest case against the page is the person it most attracts. Someone tired of a rigid calendar will drop the anchors, follow whatever still feels engaging, and call the result Attention Management. That is C74 and C75 in the same week. The method also costs judgment: every seam has to be pre-answered, and a week of daily looking will show nothing before it starts to show the gaps. The quit signal is two days of following whatever feels engaging, with no output target written. What should change if it is working: unplanned between-task gaps shrink, and a finished block hands off without a planning reopen.

The chain of blocks is the day once the seams have been answered in advance and the clock only binds at the anchors. One piece ending is then just the next piece starting.

Confirmed at [Parkinson's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkinson%27s_law).
- Gollwitzer, Peter M. (1999). Implementation intentions. Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006) meta-analysis. Pre-committing to a specific cue-and-response raises follow-through versus holding a goal alone; strongest at initiating action and shielding an ongoing goal. Confirmed at [Implementation intention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implementation_intention).
- Csíkszentmihályi, Mihály. *Beyond Boredom and Anxiety* (1975); Nakamura & Csíkszentmihályi six-factor list. Flow as full immersion with focused concentration, merged action and awareness, and distorted time. Confirmed at [Flow (psychology)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_(psychology)).
- Task switching as a laboratory finding: residual cost survives even with warning that a switch is coming (Jersild 1927; Rogers & Monsell 1995). Sub-second effects in trials; the leap to a whole workday is not a finding of that literature. Confirmed at [Task switching (psychology)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Task_switching_(psychology)).
