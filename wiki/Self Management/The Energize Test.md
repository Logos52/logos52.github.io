# The Energize Test

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

The Energize Test asks one question at the end of each day. Did the work leave you better company for the evening, or did it leave you needing the evening to recover? Work that passes usually feels like play while you do it, and the hours disappear. The test also points at a common drain on that energy. Possessions bought as signs of success keep asking for time and attention long after the purchase.

## Core takeaways

- The question is whether the hours after work find you full and present, or worn down and reaching for a feed.
- Work that energizes usually draws on something you have liked for a long time and are good at.
- Things you own keep costing attention after you buy them, and that attention comes out of the work that matters.
- Spending to save time for what matters differs from spending to signal success.
- Money is a lagging sign that a field happened to pay well, not proof you chose well.

## How one purchase turns into a chain

Possessions draw on the attention that good work needs, and one purchase can grow into a chain. In the speaker's own example, one fine watch became several. Watches left unworn needed repairs, so a winding box came next. That box did not solve it, so visits to a jeweler followed. Then insurance grew costly enough that the collection moved to storage elsewhere. All of it went to keep objects that did a job a phone already did. Larger status items, such as cars, a plane or a share of a sports team, brought the same kind of upkeep at a larger scale. Shedding them freed time and money for the work he cared about, and he reports being happier since.

## Buying time and buying status

The test does not ask for austerity. People at the top of their fields spend money freely to make life efficient, such as flying private, when the spending clears time for the thing they are trying to excel at. The line is whether a purchase serves focus or just signals arrival. That line is real but hard to draw in practice, since most purchases do a bit of both.

The test compares two kinds of evening. In one, you come home and spend hours decompressing, irritable and scrolling, because the day used you up. In the other, you arrive with your full self and are better company for your family because of what you did all day. The second outcome is the pass. One night proves little, so the signal is the pattern over many evenings.

## Seeing which work holds your attention

Passing the test depends on knowing at least part of what you are like underneath other people's expectations. Most people carry a core of real preferences wrapped in what school, family and peers told them they were good at. One way to find the core is to look for threads that have run through your whole life. For the speaker behind this test, the threads were games and risk, from a school card game to poker to treating company building as a game with levels and opponents. A second thread was breaking a hard problem down to its root and rebuilding it, which later became the core of his investing.

The practical sign is time. When you do the right kind of work, the day ends and you wonder where it went. Undoing years of choices made for other people's reasons is hard, and the test offers no shortcut for that. The odds improve when you point your career, as soon as you can, at work that makes time fly.

## Two weeks, then one audit

Run the check every night for two weeks before reading anything into it. If most evenings go to recovery, the work is the likely problem.

Separately, audit one past success. Ask whether the result came from your decision or from the moment. The speaker describes a run of deals that soared on a flood of cheap money after the pandemic. He took the rise as proof of his own judgment, took on too much personal risk, and paid for it in the crash. Mistaking a favorable moment for skill is how the next bet grows too large.

## Success arrives late

Wealth is a byproduct and a lagging sign that a chosen field paid well at that moment. Which fields pay shifts over time. A role that pays poorly now may pay well later, and a well-paid one may be automated away. Because the timing cannot be predicted, the advice is to aim at work that makes you happy and let money follow or not. On this view, success is not zero-sum, so one person's success does not reduce anyone else's.

## What acting on the test costs

The check itself costs nothing. Acting on a run of spent evenings can cost a great deal when the job pays the bills. Leaving a path has switching costs and social costs, so the result is a direction to move in, not one change to make tomorrow.

## Four objections

- The advice comes from someone who already became very rich, so it carries survivor bias.
- The line between buying time and buying status is hard to draw, since almost any luxury can be described as saving time.
- The test says little about debt, family obligations or a degree already paid for.
- It assumes a level of financial security that many people do not have.

## How to practice this

- Each night for two weeks, write one line on how you arrived at the evening, full or depleted.
- Next to it, note which part of the day you were absorbed in and which part dragged.
- At the end of the two weeks, list two or three possessions or commitments that took attention that week, and ask what each one is for.

## Open questions

- How do I run this test fairly when my finances do not allow walking away from draining work?
- How do I tell a purchase that buys time from one that buys status before I make it?
- How do I separate my own long-running interests from the ones others told me I had?
