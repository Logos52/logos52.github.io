# Habits, Productive Routines & PEER

PEER is five supports you set up for one habit tonight: the room and the cue, a two-minute start, a written stop, a locked reward, and an optional person. With those in place, the action you want is the one that takes the least effort, and the day does not wait on a surge of motivation.

High-quality work does not come from a daily surge of will. It comes from rooms and sequences that make the move you want the **path of least resistance**, the action that happens when no extra will is applied.

A habit, in the design sense, is a small repeatable action that compounds. It is easy to start and hard to skip. Automaticity grows with repetition. The three-week figure is a myth, not a research finding.

Productive routines are larger sequences of those habits that create protected blocks: a morning, a deep-work stretch, an end-of-day review. The difference between a habit and a routine is scale.

## Core takeaways

- PEER is five supports for one habit: Prep, Easy, Exit, Reward, and Peer. Each one lowers the effort of doing the wanted action.
- Prep sets the room and the cue before the hour they are needed, and removes the competing cues.
- Easy makes the first step small enough to take. Sitting at the desk can be the whole first step.
- Exit is a stop written before the start. Most habit advice leaves this one out.
- Reward sits behind a finished threshold, stays locked until that threshold is met, then fades. It helps you start; it is not what sustains a mature habit.
- Four mistakes kill the design: too many new habits at once, a first step that is already hard, will as the only support, and never defining done.

## What each of the five supports does

**Prep.** The room and the cue are set before the hour they are needed. Good cues are planted, and the competing ones are removed until starting the wrong thing takes more effort than starting the right one.

**Easy.** The first step is tiny enough to take. Sitting at the desk can be the whole of it. What gets lowered is the cost of beginning, and the rest of the work is allowed to follow.

**Exit.** A stop is written before the start. Permission to quit at the minimum step keeps the habit from becoming a session that cannot be repeated tomorrow. Most habit advice leaves this letter out.

**Reward.** Something pleasant sits behind a finished threshold, locked until that threshold is met, then faded. It is a scaffold for starting, not what sustains a mature habit. Once a habit is established, the treat drives it less.

**Peer.** Another person, where that helps, then faded. Written consequences are allowed. A forum is not required.

## What to do tonight

Tonight, for one habit: write a two-minute start, write a stop, and hide the reward until after.

## Four mistakes that kill the design

Too many new habits at once. A first step that is already hard. Will as the only support. Never defining done. The last of those is Exit, skipped.

## Carrying it into tomorrow

The same room and the same first step are what tomorrow runs on, once the stop is written and the reward is still locked. [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management|Self-Management]] then does not have to spend a surge of will on focus, energy, or showing up.

## How to practice this

1. Set the room and the cue tonight, before the hour you need them. Remove the cues that compete with the one you want. Notice whether starting the wrong thing now takes more effort.
2. Write a first step small enough that you can take it, such as sitting at the desk. Notice whether beginning costs you less than it did.
3. Write the stop before you start, at the minimum step. Notice whether you can repeat the same session tomorrow.
4. Put something pleasant behind a finished threshold and keep it locked until you meet that threshold. Fade it later. Notice whether the treat still drives the habit.
5. Bring in another person where that helps, then fade the person out. You can write down a consequence. Notice whether you still show up without them.
6. Start one habit, not several. Check that you have defined done for it. Notice which of the five supports you have not written down.
