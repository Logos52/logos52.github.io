# SpaceX & the Frontier Event

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

A frontier pays out to whoever solves the return trip, and space is now repeating that pattern. A frontier event happens when a new physical place opens up. Three forces then follow. Gatekeepers lose their grip because distance makes rules hard to enforce, so land and orbits go to whoever shows up first. Everyone heading out needs the basics of survival, so money flows to the people selling transport, fuel, water, energy and communication. Selling those picks and shovels becomes the lasting business, and transport is the biggest of them. Space looks infinite, yet the useful part within reach of today's rockets comes down to a few chokepoints. They are low Earth orbit, a belt of about 1,845 geostationary slots, and the ice at the Moon's south pole that can become rocket fuel. That is why the rocket landing on its pad matters less than what it stands for. A company that can bring its rockets back can launch its own satellites cheaply, and its rockets work as the factory for that product. The same physical gap between what people imagine and what rockets can deliver could also feed a bubble.

## Core takeaways

- A frontier pays out to whoever solves the return trip, and reusable rockets are that solution for space.
- When a new physical place opens, gatekeepers weaken and the early arrivals take the best positions.
- The lasting money goes to the sellers of transport, fuel, water, energy and communication.
- Usable space comes down to a few chokepoints: low orbit, a ring of geostationary slots, and ice at the Moon's south pole.
- Fuel made on the Moon would multiply what a rocket can carry.
- A rocket company that launches its own satellites turns its rockets into a factory, and its price is a bet on what those satellites become.
- The gap between what people imagine and what rockets can deliver could also feed a bubble.

## Coming home is the constraint

The rocket landing is a spectacle, and the real story is older. Portugal's prince sent a ship south along the African coast 15 times, and 15 times it turned back short of a cape that no returning sailor had passed. The problem was the square sail, which could only run with the wind, and the wind blew straight down the coast. On the 16th try a captain kept going, swung far out into the Atlantic, and found winds that blew back toward home. Triangular lateen sails then let ships work against the wind, and the caravel built around them became the space rocket of its day. For almost 80 years it let Portugal all but own the open ocean. Once the fear of not coming back was gone, the question became how far ships could go.

A frontier event happens when a new physical place opens up. A new physical frontier has not opened in more than 100 years, since the airplane. The count of rocket launches per year has turned sharply upward, and more rockets will reach orbit this decade than in the previous three decades combined. Past openings of a physical frontier followed one arc, from the oceans and canals to the railroads and the American West. Three forces then follow. Gatekeepers lose their grip because distance makes rules hard to enforce. Everyone heading out needs the basics of survival. Selling those basics becomes the lasting business.

## What happens when a frontier opens

The gatekeepers are already weaker in orbit. Images of every retailer's parking lot once let a trader make 15 to 20 percent a year. Few rivals could copy the trick, because an imaging satellite cost at least half a million dollars and took about ten years to build. Fewer than 200 satellites in total could photograph Earth. Then a team showed that off-the-shelf phone parts could take pictures from orbit. Rocket flights grew from 36 launches a year to 64 launches and then 100 launches. Riding on them, that company has put about 600 satellites up and photographs the whole planet every day. Nobody sold it the band it occupies. It took that band by being there first.

The American West shows where frontier money ends up. In 1867 a settler could get 160 acres free after five years of living on them, or buy a parcel for 4,358 dollars. Nothing about that offer changed except that the Pacific Railroad finished the link between the coasts, and then people went in large numbers, each paying the railroad for the ride. By 1881 railroads made up 63 percent of the US stock market. Technology reached only 52 percent in 2026, the year of the talk, and only under a loose definition.

The hierarchy of needs explains the rush. On a frontier, nobody knows what is safe or how to get to tomorrow, so only survival and security count. At the very start, only survival counts. Everything going to space will need a ride, and anything that stays will need water, energy and communication. Whoever sells those things will find buyers.

## Infinite space, a handful of positions

Space looks infinite, but the part within reach of today's rockets is small, and the best spots go to whoever arrives first. Geostationary orbit shows how tight it gets. The belt holds about 1,845 slots, because satellites packed closer would jam each other's signals, and only about 60 or 70 slots can see the United States well.

## The three bands

Low Earth orbit sits 100 to 300 miles up in the talk's telling, and satellites there circle the planet about every 90 minutes. That is close enough for sharp pictures and signals that take a few milliseconds. The largest internet constellation has more than 6,000 satellites there, and because its owner can launch replacements, it moved them about 100 miles closer to cut signal delay. Amazon, China and OneWeb are racing for the same layer.

Geostationary orbit is a ring 22,000 miles above the equator, where a satellite hangs over one spot on Earth. That makes it the home of satellite TV, missile-warning cameras and weather monitoring. Thousands of low satellites handing a target off from one to the next can now do much of what the high ring does.

The Moon, about 230,000 miles away, holds metals and helium, but the real prize is ice in craters at the south pole that never see sunlight. The ice sits in an area about the size of Switzerland, with far less flat land to build on. Ice is water, and water can be split and liquefied into rocket fuel, which makes that area the only gas station before Mars.

## Why fuel decides what you can carry

Every pound of payload needs more fuel, and that extra fuel needs fuel of its own. That compounding is the rocket equation. The direct plan for the Moon landings needed a rocket twice the size of the Saturn V, because it had to carry enough fuel to lift off twice. The missions used a separate lunar module instead. Fuel made on the Moon flips the math. A rocket that no longer carries its return fuel can carry more cargo, and the talk's example is one shipping container of 30 tons becoming four containers. Whoever lands four times as much as everyone else can crowd out rival bases. A demonstrated system already turns moon dust into solar panels, which are easier to make in the Moon's vacuum and low gravity. Chips launched from Earth plus panels made on the Moon point toward data centers in space, though that is not today's industry.

## What an investor watches

The rockets are the factory. The old business was charging others for a ride to orbit. Now the rockets are mostly a cost of carrying the company's own satellites, and the satellites earn the money once they are up. A buyer at the current price is buying a call option on orbital data centers plus continued constellation growth, and is not paying for launch revenue. Bears say no stock is worth 100 times its revenue. Bulls point to a near monopoly on launch and two new business lines. One side will be badly wrong.

## Where everyday life touches it

Most people already use the near bands without noticing. Satellite TV and weather monitoring come from the geostationary ring. The low layer carries satellite internet, and moving those satellites closer to Earth cuts the delay on every signal. Daily pictures of the whole planet feed traders, who once paid heavily for images of retailers' parking lots.

## What it costs Earth

Space ventures need money willing to wait decades for any return. Only a rich economy with deep markets can supply that patient capital, so the bill for the frontier is paid on Earth long before anything comes back. Launching everything from Earth's deep gravity also carries a steep fuel cost, which is why fuel made on the Moon matters so much.

## Boom and bubble

Space can only be used as fast as rockets launch, and that pace is slower than people can imagine a future. The dot-com bubble grew on the same kind of gap, when fiber was laid across the country and never connected to homes.

## How to practice this

- Pick a new technology and ask which physical or practical limit stopped people from coming back, or from using it at scale.
- List the basic needs everyone using it will have, and who sells them.
- For a company priced on a frontier, separate what it earns today from the option it is being priced on.
- Compare how fast people imagine the frontier growing with how fast its bottleneck, such as launches, can grow.

## Open questions

- How does a launch-rate limit decide which space businesses can grow first?
- How do companies decide which orbit to fill before a rival takes it?
- How would a working fuel depot on the Moon change who can build there?
- How does an economy build up enough patient capital to fund decades-long bets?
