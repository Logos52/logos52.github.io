# Time Beats Timing

Time beats timing is the finding that investors who buy a broad, low-cost fund and hold it for decades end up with more than those who try to pick the right moments to buy and sell. Most attempts to time the market lose money to bad entries, bad exits and fees. For an ordinary saver, it settles the main investing decision: buy the whole market cheaply and leave it alone.

## Core takeaways

- Households that trade more earn less.
- Average investors earn less than the funds they own.
- Signals that seem to predict returns fail on new data.
- About 9 in 10 large US active funds trail the S&P 500 index over 15 years.
- Yearly fees take a large share of wealth over decades.
- The main risk is the investor interrupting the compounding.

## How it works

Compounding needs uninterrupted years. Each exit risks missing the few strong days that produce much of the long-run return, and those days often come right after sharp falls, when people are most likely to be out. Trading also adds costs and taxes. The result is that investor returns trail the very funds they hold, mostly from buying after rises and selling after drops.

- A 1991 to 1996 study of US brokerage accounts found heavy traders earned the least.
- Classic predictors such as dividend yield failed outside the years they were fitted to.
- Before costs, the average active dollar earns the market return.
- After costs, the average active dollar must earn less.
- By end 2024, about 89.5% of large-cap active funds trailed the S&P 500 over 15 years.

## Why costs matter

A fee charged as a percentage of assets is taken every year from a growing balance. Over several decades it takes a large share of the final sum, even though each year it looks small. Index funds charge a small fraction of what active funds charge.

```
$10,000 at 7% for 40 years
  0.05% fee  ->  about $147,000
  1.00% fee  ->  about $103,000
```

- A 1% yearly fee costs about 30% of the ending balance over 40 years.
- A broad, low-cost index fund is the standard holding for staying in.

## Where it gets harder

Holding is a matter of temperament more than knowledge. Building wealth takes risk and optimism, and keeping it takes caution and the patience to sit through falls. Near retirement the order of returns matters too: a crash in the first years of withdrawals does more damage than the same crash later.

- Decide the mix of stocks and bonds in advance and write it down.
- Hold enough cash to avoid selling in a crash.
- Plan withdrawals around the risk of an early crash.
