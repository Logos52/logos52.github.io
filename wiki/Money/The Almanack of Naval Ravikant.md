# The Almanack of Naval Ravikant

The Almanack of Naval Ravikant is a 2020 book in which Eric Jorgenson collected the investor Naval Ravikant's 2018 thread "How to Get Rich" and his public interviews. One half covers getting wealthy and the other covers being happy. The wealth half gives a working plan for earning without trading hours for pay, and the thread itself is free to read online.

## Takeaways

- Seek wealth rather than money or status.
- Wealth is assets that earn while you sleep.
- Status is a ranking, so one person's gain is another's loss.
- Renting out your time will not make you rich.
- Combine specific knowledge, accountability and leverage.
- Code and media are leverage that needs nobody's permission.
- Play long-term games with long-term people.

## How it works

People chase money, wealth and status, and the thread treats them as different things. Money is how wealth changes hands. Wealth is owned things that keep producing, such as a business, a product or shares. Status is where a person ranks against others, so it can only be won at someone's expense, while wealth can grow for everyone at once.

- Specific knowledge: a skill found by following real curiosity.
- It cannot be taught in a course, so it resists competition.
- Accountability: taking risk under your own name.
- Leverage: anything that multiplies the output of a decision.
  - Labour: other people working for you.
  - Capital: money put to work.
  - Code and media: copies cost almost nothing to make.

Code and media are the newest kind of leverage and the most open. A program or a video works while its maker sleeps and needs no manager or lender to approve it.

## Long-term games

Returns build up over time in relationships as well as in money. A deal with a stranger needs checking and a contract. Working with the same trusted people for years means less checking, fewer contracts and more shared risk.

- Pick partners by how they will act over decades.
- Trust built over years lowers the cost of each new deal.

## Happiness

The second half of the book treats happiness as a skill that can be practised. Desire, in Ravikant's definition, is a contract a person makes with themselves to be unhappy until they get what they want. Fewer desires held at once means fewer of those contracts running.

- Choose one main desire at a time.
- Peace of mind comes from fewer open wants.
