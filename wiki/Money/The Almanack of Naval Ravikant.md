# The Almanack of Naval Ravikant

The Almanack of Naval Ravikant is a book that collects one investor's public writing on wealth into a single argument. The argument rests on three things a person can build: specific knowledge, accountability, and leverage. Specific knowledge is knowledge that no training can give you, and it is the kind you would pursue anyway. Accountability means putting your own name on outcomes, so that when an outcome is good, the reward comes back to you. Leverage is anything that lets one piece of work produce a return more than once. Code and media do this without anyone's permission and without hiring anyone.

In this argument money is not the goal. Money is the means by which results move from one person to another. The goal is to reach a point where you no longer have to sell your working week for pay. Status is a different target. The book treats status as a separate pursuit that runs at the same time as the pursuit of wealth.

## Core takeaways

- Wealth is built from three things a person can develop: specific knowledge, accountability, and leverage.
- Code and media are leverage that needs no permission. They can be published without anyone's approval, and copies cost almost nothing.
- The aim of wealth is freedom. The goal is to stop trading time for pay, not to consume more, and not to gain status.
- Compounding applies to trust and reputation as well as to capital. Working over long periods with the same people produces most of the returns.
- Accountability means taking business risk under your own name. Leverage, equity, and trust go to the person who can be hurt when things go wrong.
- Happiness is a separate skill from wealth, and contentment can be trained.

## How the book says wealth is built

The money half of the book is a theory of building wealth without selling your time by the hour. The method is to turn yourself into a product: combine specific knowledge, accountability, and leverage into something the market will pay for, then attach that to something that can scale. Own equity in the result. Do not get paid by the hour.

Work over long periods with people who will also stay for long periods. Compounding applies to trust and reputation, not only to capital. The source claims that almost all returns in life, in wealth, in relationships, and in knowledge, come from compound interest over long periods. The book applies compounding to relationships and knowledge by comparison with money. The comparison holds in those areas, but it is not a measured fact.

## Happiness as a second skill

The book treats happiness as a second skill, separate from wealth. Desire, in the book's account, is a choice to stay unhappy until the thing you want arrives. A wealthy person who has not learned contentment is still anxious, with more money. Contentment is something a person can train. The [[wiki/Money/Investing and Budgeting Mindsets|investing & budgeting reading list]] draws on the book for its money half, not for its happiness half.

## Specific knowledge

Specific knowledge is found by following genuine curiosity. It resists outsourcing and automation, and for that reason it stays scarce. It cannot be bought as a credential. The book's phrase "cannot be trained" is too strong if it is read as "cannot be taught at all"; practice still builds it.

## Leverage, with and without permission

Leverage is a multiplier on output. It can be other people's time, capital, or, now, code and media that copy at near-zero cost. Older forms of leverage often need permission. Newer forms do not; the book calls these permissionless leverage. Code and media can be published or shipped without anyone's approval, and copies cost almost nothing. Labour and capital do not always need permission either. Platforms that distribute work now control who sees it. Creating the work still needs no permission.

## Accountability

Accountability is business risk taken under your own name. Taking on the risk of loss is what earns the reward: leverage, equity, and trust go to the person who can be hurt if things go wrong. Specific knowledge and accountability feed the human axis of the [[wiki/Concepts/Human vs AI Capability Lens|Human vs AI Capability Lens]].

## Money and freedom

Money buys freedom. The goal is to stop trading your time for pay, not to buy more things. Freedom from obligations comes first. Freedom to do what you want comes after.

## What the book adds to this knowledge base

The book adds the freedom endpoint, and permissionless leverage as the way to reach it. Wealth is a skill. Its aim is to stop selling your hours. Its aim is not to be anxious with more money, and not to gain status. Four neighbouring pages already in the knowledge base cover the leverage pattern.

## How to practice this

1. Write down what you know that no training gave you and that you would pursue without pay. Notice whether that knowledge can be outsourced or automated. If it cannot, the book says it stays scarce.
2. List the outcomes at work that carry your own name. Notice whether you can be hurt if they fail. The book says leverage, equity, and trust go to the person who can be hurt.
3. Check which of your work can be published or shipped without anyone's approval. Code and media qualify. Notice whether a platform controls who sees the work once it is out.
4. Look at how you are paid. Notice whether you are paid by the hour, or whether you own equity in something that can scale.
5. Look at the people you work with. Notice whether you expect to still be working with them for a long time. The book says trust and reputation compound over long periods.
6. Pick one thing you want and notice how you feel until you get it. The book says desire is a choice to stay unhappy until the thing arrives.
