# America's Industrial Revival - The Freight Signal

The figures and the freight map are the speaker's claims from the talk, not independently checked facts.

A flatbed truck is a simple deck, about 53 feet long and 11.5 feet wide, with no walls and no roof. It is a poor way to move most cargo. Thieves can reach what it carries, weather soaks it, and it cannot keep produce at the right temperature. The interesting fact is that booking one has become extremely hard. Companies are choosing to pay much more to move cargo this way, including trucker rates that doubled in about 6 months. Talk can be spun. Paying that price cannot. That gap between speech and action is revealed preference, and freight data lives in that world.

Other stories do not cover the move. A crackdown on commercial licenses appears to be only a small slice. Fuel is not enough either. Oil running over 100 dollars a barrel accounted for about 24 cents, or 8 percent, of the change being explained. Front-loading imports before tariffs arrive would heat the coasts first, the way the map has worked for about 3 decades. Freight would show up in Los Angeles, Savannah, or Newark, then travel inland a couple of weeks later. The present map is the reverse. The coasts are barely in the action. The interior is red hot, along the I-35 corridor, through the old rust belt, and down into Texas. The center of the country is no longer only receiving freight. It is generating it.

That pattern has one clean reading. The pipes between mine, mill, factory, warehouse, and job site are full. What arrives at a warehouse is leaving again, which forces another truck to bring more. About 50 percent of flatbed loads are being rejected. Consumer parcels do not travel this way. Flatbeds carry rolls of steel, equipment, and lumber, the class of cargo that cannot go in a box. About 450,000 active flatbed trucks are available for dispatch in the United States. Because those trucks can relocate, a brief spike usually clears in 1 week to 2 weeks. A squeeze that lasts is not a passing jam. It is described as the industrial economy cooking in a way that has not been seen for 20 years, after a prior year that felt abysmal.

A second current is large technology firms spending on data-center buildout at a scale that would have sounded impossible a decade earlier. The talk uses a figure of 150 billion dollars in 2026. That spending dumps cash into trades and places that had been quiet, and it sits on top of a product that costs providers more to supply than users pay. Whether that burst already explains official productivity is left open. A chart comparing the early public internet with a widely used chat model shows the newer line rising faster, which would be a strong signal if it holds and is not noise. Interior building and the information-sector race are described as firing at the same time. That pairing was new in a lifetime during which the middle of the country had been on a downward path.

More than 60 percent of Americans hold money in the stock market, much of it tied to a cluster of about 10 companies spending heavily to compete with one another. The other firms do not move the headline index as fast, and the coast-based market signal can miss what the interior feels. The spending still spreads money across the country. The speaker's line, repeated, is that they have never felt more optimistic about the United States economy.

Remembered shocks are the black swans people can point to, the disasters that arrive in headlines. Another kind lands quietly and then rearranges life. A mold spore on a forgotten dish led to penicillin. Infection deaths in the last year of the Second World War fell from 1 death in every 5 deaths to fewer than 1 death in every 100 deaths. The argument is that a productivity jump of that family, arriving just as the interior starts building again, would be hard to read as anything but bullish if it persists. A further frontier, rockets that land themselves, is named and then left for another discussion.

Footnotes in the same talk add supporting texture. Natural gas is about 15 times cheaper in the United States than in Europe and Japan, and about 4 times cheaper than in China. Energy is what factories want, and it is about 40 percent of the cost of heavy manufacturing, more important than labor in that telling. Cheap, stable power does not cancel every other high cost of making things in the United States, and it does beat planning a factory only to watch energy prices rise fivefold 3 years later. Rail freight is rising alongside trucks, with chemical and grain shipments at 20-year or all-time highs. Large equipment makers are showing all-time-high backlogs and rising revenue, with plants in the interior. Robotics is named as a near-term addition to the same stack.

Bumps are expected, and perfection is not claimed. The trend is what the freight map is being asked to carry.

## Open questions

How would you notice, from the next month of flatbed rejections, that the interior surge had started to fade?

What would have to stay true about energy prices for a new factory planned today to still make sense 3 years from now?
