# All-In Summit and Moon Packet 2026-09-16

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

The intake for 14 to 16 September 2026 held four interviews from a technology summit and one video essay on South Africa. Most of the summit material bears on one argument, whether AI doom talk is a real warning or a story that slows America down, and on who should test frontier models before release. Three guests gave three different answers on testing. The interviews also made several forecasts with dates attached, which are worth checking later. A set of policy details from the Vice President's interview and some SpaceX operating detail remain unplaced.

The chip maker's chief executive kept lab safety and whistleblowing serious while calling the extinction talk unscientific. He listed forecasts that failed. Radiologists were supposed to be gone in 5 years, and AI was supposed to write 90 percent of code within 6 to 12 months. Half of entry-level jobs were supposed to vanish within 6 to 9 months. Early open models were also called too dangerous to release. The President phoned in to call the doom narrative a hoax and to describe data centers as the oil of the next 20 to 25 years.

The Vice President took a middle line. He accepted that the lab founder at the center of the debate was earnest, but pointed out that companies wanting defensive tools against that lab's hacking-capable model were being refused access. A company asking the government for regulation while it withholds defensive tools, in his reading, has the order wrong.

## Who should test the models: three answers given

The three guests who spoke to testing gave three different designs.

- The SpaceX and Tesla founder wanted the major AI labs to test one another's models ahead of release, with early access. Any lab that finds a flaw warns the builder, and goes public if it stays unfixed. The test harnesses could be open source, and the scheme is a small enough ask that China might accept it. A co-host added that existing product-liability rules already cover unsafe AI.
- The Microsoft chief executive wanted outside testers with broad access and no cozy deals over who tests whom.
- The chip maker's chief executive wanted third-party evaluators that work like financial auditors, with several of them so that no single one gets captured.

## A name the captions did not resolve

The chip maker's chief executive credited a whistleblower with courage, and the captions spell the name "Coxin." The packet keeps that spelling because nothing else in the intake confirms it.

## Mythos

The intake names Mythos once. The Microsoft chief executive said his company's fast cyber harness beat even Mythos on a cyber benchmark, and that his models perform similarly in coding and knowledge work. His advice to companies was to use every model while depending on none of them. The intake says nothing more about Mythos.

## Not placed on a page

Several details also went to no page.

- The Vice President on work visas wanted the program kept for real geniuses and not for replacing a 60,000 dollar accountant with a 45,000 dollar foreign worker, backed by a 100,000 dollar fee.
- The Vice President on fraud said the government cannot tell who is on the food-aid program, and that California bills the federal government 7 to 9 billion dollars a month for it.

- The SpaceX president described starting as the seventh employee, running the company with player-coach managers, and land near rumored data-center sites jumping from 3,000 dollars an acre to 180,000 dollars an acre.
- One line in the video essay notes, about the former president later joining others near Robert Mugabe, was cut off in the capture.
