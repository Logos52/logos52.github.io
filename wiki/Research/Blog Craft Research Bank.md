# Blog Craft Research Bank

Verified research bank, gathered by parallel research agents; every quoted line was verification-checked at collection time. Raw reference material, not a wiki-register page.

================================================================================
LANE: supermemo-guru
SUMMARY: Woźniak's default opening is a copular definition in which the page title is the grammatical subject of the first sentence — "Spaced repetition is...", "Incremental reading is...", "Learntropy is..." — with no runway, no example, and no attempt to earn attention before delivering the payload. The structural reason is architectural rather than stylistic: supermemo.guru is a dense link graph of ~2,500 pages, every page is entered cold from a link inside another page, and each paragraph was authored as a separately-scheduled SuperMemo element, so an opening cannot assume a reader who has read anything above it and must survive being read out of order. His technical pages push this further by opening under a section heading — "Formula", "Outline of the model", "Definition" — putting the equation or the two-variable claim above all prose and demoting explanation to a section literally titled "Explanation". Where he does write essays, the provocation is loaded entirely into the title ("Teaching is dead", "School damages your brain") which frees the first sentence to be flat, mid-argument, or even self-undermining — the brain-damage chapter opens by conceding that the peer-reviewed evidence does not exist. First-person narrative appears exactly once, in the book-level Introduction to "Problem of Schooling", quarantined under a heading so no chapter has to carry biography; and the site's unfinished pages ship publicly behind honest banners ("This text is incomplete and unverified. It is being developed incrementally") rather than waiting for a finished linear draft, which is the incremental-writing philosophy visible as page furniture.

SOURCE: Piotr Woźniak — "Spaced repetition" (glossary entry, supermemo.guru/wiki/Spaced_repetition)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: Spaced repetition is a learning method based on computing optimum intervals that should separate review of individual pieces of knowledge to achieve a desired level of retention. SuperMemo pioneered the use of optimization methods in spaced repetition. Over the last two decades, spaced repetition has evolved into incremental reading.
MOVE: Copular definition — "X is a Y that does Z" — with the page title as the grammatical subject of sentence one, then provenance, then trajectory.
WHY: The reader arriving cold knows within one clause what the page is about and what class of thing the term belongs to; no orientation cost, no wait. It cannot read as a hook because it makes no promise and withholds nothing — the payload is the first sentence rather than a reward for continuing. Sentences two and three add only origin and direction, so the paragraph is a complete answer that happens to also be an entrance.
CRAFT: The page carries the site's standing boilerplate for this genre, verbatim: "This glossary entry is used to explain SuperMemo, a pioneer of spaced repetition software since 1987" (supermemo.guru/wiki/Spaced_repetition). Woźniak explicitly types his pages — glossary entry vs. article vs. book chapter — and the type dictates the opening shape.

SOURCE: Piotr Woźniak — "Incremental reading" (supermemo.guru/wiki/Incremental_reading)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: Incremental reading is a system of tools and strategies used in assisting reading, learning and retention of written knowledge.

Incremental reading enhances the speed of reading, while simultaneously improving comprehension, applicability, and knowledge retention.

Incremental reading simplifies and accelerates the process of transforming written articles into knowledge that lasts for life.

Incremental reading uses spaced repetition to ensure high recall with minimal effort.
MOVE: Definition, then an anaphoric stack: four consecutive one-sentence paragraphs that each restate the term as subject and add one new predicate. Claims are stacked, not chained.
WHY: Each paragraph is independently true and independently quotable, so the reader can stop after any one of them and leave with something intact — the opposite of a hook, which is engineered to make stopping feel like loss. The repetition of the full term instead of a pronoun means no sentence depends on the one above it for its referent. The effect is a specification being read out, and the flatness is what makes the accumulating claims feel like inventory rather than salesmanship.
CRAFT: On the companion page "Incremental writing" Woźniak states his own production method for the site: "Nearly all texts at SuperMemo Guru have been written in SuperMemo using the incremental writing approach" (supermemo.guru/wiki/Incremental_writing). Paragraphs are authored as separately-scheduled SuperMemo elements, which is the mechanical reason they read as free-standing units.

SOURCE: Piotr Woźniak — "Incremental writing" (supermemo.guru/wiki/Incremental_writing)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: Incremental writing is a method of writing, in which ideas are written down, and assembled incrementally. Incremental writing requires no linearity. It adapts to your way of thinking. Many great writers and scientists of the past used a variant of incremental writing using their own systems of notes.
MOVE: Defines the method, then immediately states its constraint-removal ("requires no linearity") as a property, not a benefit-pitch. Precedent is cited in sentence four rather than used as an opening appeal.
WHY: By naming what the method does not require before naming what it gives you, the opening frames itself as a description of a mechanism rather than a case for adopting one. The short declaratives carry no intensifiers, so a reader skeptical of productivity writing has nothing to brace against. Precedent arrives after the definition, where it functions as context instead of as authority-borrowing.
CRAFT: The linked page "Advantages of incremental writing" carries Woźniak's most direct statements on writing process: "When the fun of writing is gone, the writer's block can ensue"; "Strike the iron while it's hot. Process inspiration incrementally, and pick the pieces that raise most enthusiasm at any given moment"; and on structure, "It is vital to keep all ideas well prioritized in a TO-DO branch of knowledge tree, while the article grows independently in the ARTICLE branch" (supermemo.guru/wiki/Advantages_of_incremental_writing).

SOURCE: Piotr Woźniak — "Learn drive" (chapter of "I would never send my kids to school", supermemo.guru/wiki/Learn_drive)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: [Banner] This text is part of: "I would never send my kids to school" by Piotr Wozniak (2017)

[§ Definition] The term learn drive is essential for proving the inefficiency of schooling.

The learn drive is a natural tendency of the brain to seek new information. The learn drive is generated by a guidance system hard-wired in the brain.
MOVE: States the term's load-bearing role in the argument before defining it — a declaration of what the definition is for — then gives the definition in one bolded sentence.
WHY: Telling the reader what work the term must do converts an ordinary definition into a visible piece of machinery, so the reader tracks it as a component rather than filing it as vocabulary. It avoids performance by being nakedly instrumental: the sentence admits the definition is being built to carry an argument, which is more disarming than any attempt to seem neutral. The §Definition heading also means the opening is addressable — a reader can be sent to it directly.
CRAFT: The §Terminology section that follows is a stated principle about naming: "I purposefully avoid the term curiosity, which is often used in psychology. Without precise understanding of the mechanisms of the learn drive, my whole reasoning against the present school system would collapse." He then rules out curiosity, novelty seeking, boredom susceptibility, openness to experience, need of cognition, and intellectual engagement because "They all carry a baggage that may obscure the picture" (supermemo.guru/wiki/Learn_drive).

SOURCE: Piotr Woźniak — "Learntropy" (supermemo.guru/wiki/Learntropy)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: Learntropy is the attractiveness of any signal from the point of view of the learn drive system.

Lectures can be boring or attractive. Learntropy expresses their attractiveness from the point of view of an individual.
MOVE: Defines a coined word in one sentence with no apology, no scare quotes, and no announcement that it is a coinage; the second paragraph supplies a two-clause concrete instance and immediately re-states the definition in plainer words.
WHY: Treating an invented term exactly like an established one signals that the reader's job is to use it, not to evaluate whether it was needed. The plain-language restatement in paragraph two does the work an example usually does, without becoming a scene — it is a re-articulation, not an anecdote. Nothing is withheld for later, so there is no reveal-flip available.
CRAFT: His own justification for coining rather than borrowing appears further down the same page: "In other words, it is highly imprecise to evoke the term of entropy in the context of efficient learning. We need a similar term: learntropy" (supermemo.guru/wiki/Learntropy) — the coinage is defended by the imprecision of the nearest existing word, matching the naming principle stated on "Learn drive".

SOURCE: Piotr Woźniak — "Forgetting curve" (supermemo.guru/wiki/Forgetting_curve)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: [§ Formula] Forgetting curve describes the decline in the probability of recall over time (source: Wozniak, Gorzelanczyk, Murakowski, 1995):

R=exp(-t/S)

where:
R - probability of recall (retrievability of memory)
S - strength of a memory trace (stability of memory)
t - time
MOVE: Opens on a section heading called "Formula" and puts the equation and its variable glossary above all prose; the explanatory section is titled "Explanation" and comes second.
WHY: The most compressed statement of the idea is placed first and the prose is demoted to gloss, which tells the reader exactly how much text they need depending on what they came for. A citation inside the first sentence does the credibility work that a hook would otherwise attempt through voice. There is no runway at all — the page refuses to spend a sentence preparing the reader for the equation.
CRAFT: This ordering recurs across his technical pages: "Two component model of memory" likewise opens under a heading ("Outline of the model") with "The two component model of long-term memory asserts that two independent variables are sufficient to describe the status of unitary memory in a healthy human brain:" followed by the two variables, and only then sections for Biological function, Origin, and Significance (supermemo.guru/wiki/Two_components_of_long-term_memory).

SOURCE: Piotr Woźniak — "Teaching is dead" (chapter of "Problem of Schooling", supermemo.guru/wiki/Teaching_is_dead)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: I have argued for years that the role of teachers will keep waning. The year 2022 should be considered the official end of teaching. The breakthrough came unexpectedly from ChatGPT.
MOVE: Starts mid-argument. Sentence one is a status report on a position the author has already held for years; sentence two is a dated verdict; sentence three names the cause. No setup, no stakes-building.
WHY: Entering a long-running argument already in progress treats the reader as a peer catching up rather than a prospect being warmed up, which is the structural opposite of a hook. Attaching a specific year to the claim makes it falsifiable in the first three sentences and gives it the texture of a record rather than a provocation. The provocation lives entirely in the title, which frees the first sentence to be flat.
CRAFT: The page carries the series banner "This text is part of: 'Problem of Schooling' by Piotr Wozniak (2017-2026)" — the date range is live and moving, marking the parent work as continuously revised rather than published-and-closed, consistent with the incremental-writing method he describes elsewhere.

SOURCE: Piotr Woźniak — "School damages your brain" (chapter of "Problem of Schooling", supermemo.guru/wiki/School_damages_your_brain)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: If you look for peer-reviewed research showing that coercive schooling results in brain injury or brain damage, you will not be successful. You need to connect the dots or rely on experts in the field.
MOVE: Opens by conceding the evidentiary gap that the title's claim runs into, then states what the reader will have to do instead. The concession precedes any argument.
WHY: Leading with the weakness of your own evidence buys more trust in two sentences than any amount of confident framing, and it pre-empts the objection the title guarantees. It is the inverse of a reveal-flip: instead of "seems X, actually Y", it says up front that the strong version cannot be proven yet. The reader is told the epistemic terms of the page before being asked to travel on it.
CRAFT: Four sentences in, the page states its own deliverable outright: "This text is an attempt to shortly synthesize (1) the research in psychology and psychiatry, and (2) the research in brain science (at the synaptic and architectural level)." Declaring the shape of the piece in its opening block is a recurring habit — compare "13 years of school in a month", whose opening paragraph ends "This short text quantifies the abysmal inefficiency of schooling" (supermemo.guru/wiki/13_years_of_school_in_a_month).

SOURCE: Piotr Woźniak — "Problem of Schooling", §Introduction (book front matter, supermemo.guru/wiki/Problem_of_schooling)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: All my life I have worshiped free learning (i.e. self-directed, and self-paced self-learning). I love learning with SuperMemo. I have long forgotten my 22 long years of schooling. In the past, each time someone asked me about my formula for the perfect modern education system, I would say I have no idea.
MOVE: First-person origin statement — a standing disposition, a count of years, and an admission of prior ignorance — placed under an explicit "Introduction" heading at the book level, not at chapter level.
WHY: This is the one place on the site where narrative is permitted, and it is quarantined by a heading that tells the reader it is front matter, so the chapters themselves never have to carry biography. Admitting "I would say I have no idea" establishes that the book's thesis was arrived at rather than assumed. Notably, even here the first sentence is a flat statement of a lifelong position rather than a scene — the summer heat and the vow appear only after the disposition has been declared.
CRAFT: The same page publishes its own annotation conventions, e.g. "Excerpts: mark the most remarkable or influential words taken from other authors. At times, putting things in my own words would not do justice to the original" and "Motto: some witty idea or quote from a wise man, usually from ages ago. A quote that sets the theme for a chapter." It also flags which chapters postdate the 2017 print version: "Chapters that are missing in the original printable version from 2017 are marked with '*' below".

SOURCE: Piotr Woźniak — "Effective learning: Twenty rules of formulating knowledge" (supermemo.com/en/blog/twenty-rules-of-formulating-knowledge, dated 06.12.1999)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: Dr Piotr Wozniak, February, 1999 (updated)

This article will help you overcome one of the greatest difficulties you will face when trying to accelerate learning: formulating knowledge

The speed of learning will depend on the way you formulate the material. The same material can be learned many times faster if well formulated! The difference in speed can be stunning!
MOVE: Byline, then a one-line statement of what the article will do for the reader, then the thesis as a fact about the world. The utility promise is separated into its own paragraph and names the difficulty by its plain name at the end of the sentence.
WHY: Stating the article's function outright ("This article will help you overcome...") gives the reader a decision to make immediately — read on or leave — which is the honest inverse of a hook's attempt to remove that decision. The thesis paragraph makes a causal claim about material and speed, not about the reader's inner life, so it dodges the census-claim trap entirely. The exclamation marks are the one performative element in the whole corpus, and they land on measurements rather than on the reader.
CRAFT: The opening block also states the piece's ordering principle: "The rules are listed in the order of importance. Those listed first are most often violated or bring most benefit if complied with!" The 2026 rewrite on the wiki opens instead with the thesis as bare fact — "The way we formulate knowledge in learning has a monumental impact on memory. Thoughtfully crafted questions simplify learning, while well-organized knowledge ensures it stays with you for a lifetime." (supermemo.guru/wiki/20_rules_of_knowledge_formulation) — showing him revising a utility-promise opener into a world-fact opener over 27 years.

LANE: naval
SUMMARY: Across three decades and three formats — 2005–2016 written blog posts, the 2018 tweetstorm, and the 2020 Almanack — Naval opens the same way: a flat declarative claim about how the world works, delivered unhedged and without any apparatus around it, usually inside the first ten words. He does not use hooks, questions, or scenes because his openings carry no rhetorical intent at all; they are the conclusion stated first, with the argument arriving afterward as elaboration rather than as build-up. The distinctive structural devices are (a) defining the terms before arguing, (b) an unhedged absolute repaired by a scope-narrowing fragment in the next sentence rather than by a hedge inside the first, (c) a chapter-epigraph aphorism standing alone as the door, and (d) since 2019, offloading all setup onto Nivi so Naval's first line is always a verdict on someone else's framing. His stated principles line up exactly with the specimens: the tweetstorm is written to be "information-dense, very concise, high-impact, and timeless" (Almanack p. 30); "the best authors respect the reader's time" and his favorite writers are all "high density" (nav.al/density, 2025); and his working template for prose is Scott Adams's post on brevity, headlines, and active voice (Almanack p. 219). The most useful negative control for the researcher is "Happiness Is Learned," the one opening built from personal experience — it still refuses scene, sensory detail, and moment, and instead gives a date and a self-reported number, which is the minimum needed to show that a change occurred.

SOURCE: Naval Ravikant — "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)" tweetstorm, @naval on Twitter/X, May 31 2018 (status 1002103360646823936)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: Tweet 1: "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky):"

Tweet 2: "Seek wealth, not money or status. Wealth is having assets that earn while you sleep. Money is how we transfer time and wealth. Status is your place in the social hierarchy."
MOVE: Title-as-promise, then a definitional stack — the opening unit defines the three terms the whole argument will run on before any argument happens.
WHY: The first tweet is not a hook, it is a label: it tells you the deliverable and its one disqualifier ("without getting lucky") and spends zero words earning attention. The second unit then does the single least glamorous thing available — it defines wealth, money, and status in three parallel clauses — so the reader is handed the vocabulary rather than a feeling. There is no build-up to withhold, so there is nothing to perform; the compression itself is the signal that the writer respects your time.
CRAFT: Naval's own account of the form, in The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, "Building Wealth," p. 30 (official free PDF, navalmanack.com): "The tweetstorm tries to be information-dense, very concise, high-impact, and timeless. It has all the information and principles, so if you absorb these and you work hard over ten years, you'll get what you want." Verified from the PDF text; the same tweetstorm is reproduced in the book with capitalization changed to "(Without Getting Lucky)".

SOURCE: Naval Ravikant — "Why You Can't Hire" (nav.al, Dec 13 2011)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: "There isn't a shortage of developers and designers. There's a surplus of founders."
MOVE: Corrects the reader's framing in one sentence and supplies the replacement in the next — a supply/demand restatement, not a suspense reversal.
WHY: This is the closest Naval gets to a flip, and the difference from the rejected "feels like X, but actually Y" move is instructive: there is no build-up, no "you'd think," no marker of surprise, and the correct claim lands six words into the piece rather than at the end of a paragraph. Both sentences are flat present-tense assertions about the world; the reader is not walked into a trap and released, they are simply told the market is being described from the wrong side. The economy — nine words then seven — makes it read as a correction of fact rather than a rhetorical turn.
CRAFT: Naval names the template he writes against, in The Almanack, "Naval's Recommended Reading," p. 219: on Scott Adams's "The Day You Became a Better Writer" — "Even though I am a very good writer and I've been writing a lot since I was young, I still open up that blog post and put it in the background anytime I'm writing anything important. It's that good. I use it as my basic template for how to write well… He teaches you in one small blog post the importance of surprise, the importance of headlines, the importance of being brief and directed, not using some adjectives and adverbs, using active not the passive voice, etc."

SOURCE: Naval Ravikant — "The 80-hour Myth" (nav.al, Nov 29 2005)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: "Let's get serious. Nobody works eighty hours a week. Not eighty real, productive hours."
MOVE: Rules a widely-repeated claim false as a flat absolute, then narrows the scope in a fragment — the qualifier arrives as a third sentence rather than as a hedge inside the first.
WHY: "Nobody works eighty hours a week" is an unhedged universal negative, which is exactly the kind of sentence a cautious writer would soften into uselessness; Naval instead lets it stand and repairs the scope in the next breath ("Not eighty real, productive hours"). The reader gets the position at full strength first and the boundary condition immediately after, which is faster to read than one heavily-qualified sentence. "Let's get serious" does the work a hook would do without performing — it announces a register (we are going to stop pretending) rather than manufacturing curiosity.
CRAFT: On why he ships unhedged absolutes and takes the exception-hunting on the chin, The Almanack, "Learn to Love to Read," pp. 115–116: "Actually, my notetaking is Twitter. I read and read and read. If I have some fundamental 'ah-ha' insight or concept, Twitter forces me to distill it into a few characters. Then I try and put it out there as an aphorism. Then I get attacked by random people who point out all kinds of obvious exceptions and jump down my throat." The margin aphorism on the same page: "Pointing out obvious exceptions implies either the target isn't smart or you aren't."

SOURCE: Naval Ravikant — "Build a Team that Ships" (nav.al, Apr 27 2012)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: "I started my first company 15 years go, and I still can't manage. I suspect that very few people can. With AngelList, we want a team of self-managing people who ship code."
MOVE: Credential plus admitted incompetence as the premise, then the design constraint that follows from it — cause stated in three sentences, then straight into a bulleted list of practices.
WHY: The opening buys authority and spends it in the same sentence: fifteen years in, and the confession is that the skill was never acquired. That converts what could have been a management-advice post into a report of a workaround, which is a lower-status and therefore more credible position, and it does it without any self-deprecating routine — one clause, no elaboration. Note that the published text contains an uncorrected typo ("15 years go"), still live in 2026; the piece was optimized for shipping the idea, not for polish, which is itself consistent with its subject.
CRAFT: Naval's stated standard for what earns a reader's attention, from nav.al/density, "The Best Authors Respect the Reader's Time" (Sep 24 2025): "at this point in my life, I want to read high-density works. You can call it the TikTok Disease or the Twitter generation, but it's also just being respectful of our time. We already have a lot of data. We have some knowledge. Now we want wisdom."

SOURCE: Naval Ravikant — "Life Formulas I" (nav.al, Feb 8 2008)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: "These are notes to myself. Your frame of reference, and therefore your calculations, may vary. These are not definitions – these are algorithms for success. Contributions welcome."
MOVE: Declares the document's genre, status, and reader contract before any content — an epistemic preamble, not an introduction.
WHY: Four sentences establish who the writing is for (himself), how sure it is (your mileage varies), what type of object it is (algorithms, not definitions), and that it is unfinished (contributions welcome) — and then the post is nothing but equations, with no connective prose at all. Because the frame is set explicitly, the aphoristic body needs no cushioning; the reader has already been told not to read it as authority. This is the opposite of a hook: it lowers the stakes of the piece so the compressed content can be taken plainly.
CRAFT: The Almanack, pp. 115–116, on the mechanism that produces this register: "my notetaking is Twitter… If I have some fundamental 'ah-ha' insight or concept, Twitter forces me to distill it into a few characters. Then I try and put it out there as an aphorism." "Life Formulas I" is the pre-Twitter version of the same instinct — the formulas here (Wealth, Leverage, Specific Knowledge) reappear ten years later as tweetstorm lines.

SOURCE: Naval Ravikant — "American Spring" (nav.al, Jan 15 2016)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: "A one party system ran the United States, and the United States ran the world."
MOVE: Opens the argument in past tense as if reciting settled history — a narrative assertion with no citation, no attribution, and no signal that it is contentious.
WHY: The sentence reads like the first line of a chronicle rather than the first line of an opinion piece, and the parallel construction ("ran… ran…") makes it sound recited rather than argued. Every subsequent paragraph is one more past-tense move in the same chain, so the essay's persuasive work is done entirely by sequence — no "I think," no evidence-marshalling posture, no invitation to disagree. It avoids performance by refusing the essayist's usual apparatus altogether; the reader either follows the causal chain or doesn't.
CRAFT: The Almanack, "How to Think Clearly," p. 95, margin aphorism: "Clear thinkers appeal to their own authority." Also on the same page: "The advanced concepts in a field are less proven. We use them to signal insider knowledge, but we'd be better off nailing the basics."

SOURCE: Naval Ravikant / Eric Jorgenson — The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, Part I: Wealth → "Building Wealth" → "Understand How Wealth Is Created" (pp. 29–30)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: Chapter epigraph (p. 29, standing alone on the page): "Making money is not a thing you do—it's a skill you learn."

Section opening (p. 30): "I like to think that if I lost all my money and you dropped me on a random street in any English-speaking country, within five or ten years I'd be wealthy again because it's just a skillset I've developed that anyone can develop."
MOVE: A one-line aphorism as the door, then the claim's strongest personal warrant as the first body sentence — thesis, then the falsifiable bet that backs it.
WHY: The epigraph is a category reassignment in eleven words (money-making moves from the "things that happen to you" bucket to the "skills" bucket), and the body sentence immediately stakes something on it — a specific, checkable wager with a time bound. The reader gets the claim and the author's exposure to being wrong about it in the first two units, which is a substitute for the credibility a hook tries to borrow. Nothing is withheld and nothing is dramatized; the structure repeats across every chapter ("There's no shortcut to smart." opens Building Judgment, p. 93; "Don't take yourself so seriously. You're just a monkey with a plan." opens Learning Happiness, p. 127).
CRAFT: Naval on where the compressed lines come from — they are carried, not composed at the desk. The Almanack, p. 30: "I came up with the principles in my tweetstorm (below) for myself when I was really young, around thirteen or fourteen. I've been carrying them in my head for thirty years, and I've been living them."

SOURCE: Naval Ravikant / Eric Jorgenson — The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, "How to Think Clearly" (p. 95)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: Pull-quote at the head of the section: "'Clear thinker' is a better compliment than 'smart.'"

First body sentence: "Real knowledge is intrinsic, and it's built from the ground up."
MOVE: Ranks two values against each other in the first line (which compliment is higher), then states the mechanism as a bare fact — a preference declared, then its ground.
WHY: The opening does not ask what clear thinking is or why it matters; it asserts a ranking, which forces the reader to either accept a new scale or notice they hold a different one. The body sentence that follows is deliberately unremarkable prose — "built from the ground up" — and the whole section proceeds by worked example (Feynman rebuilding mathematics from the number line) rather than by argument. Neither sentence contains a question, a second person address, or a promise about what the reader will learn.
CRAFT: Naval's stated test for whether an explanation is finished, same page: "if someone is using a lot of fancy words and a lot of big concepts, they probably don't know what they're talking about. I think the smartest people can explain things to a child. If you can't explain it to a child, then you don't know it. It's a common saying and it's very true."

SOURCE: Naval Ravikant / Eric Jorgenson — The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, "Happiness Is Learned" (p. 128)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: "Ten years ago, if you would have asked me how happy I was, I would have dismissed the question. I didn't want to talk about it.

On a scale of 1–10, I would have said 2/10 or 3/10. Maybe 4/10 on my best days. But I did not value being happy."
MOVE: A dated personal baseline with a number attached — the before-measurement, not a scene.
WHY: This is the one register in the corpus that starts from personal experience, and it is worth studying precisely because it still refuses the scene-opener: there is no room, no moment, no sensory detail, no dialogue. "Ten years ago" and "2/10 or 3/10" do the entire job — a time index and a self-reported score, which is the minimum apparatus needed to establish that a change happened and is therefore learnable. The last clause ("But I did not value being happy") is the actual thesis of the chapter, delivered as a flat admission rather than as a revelation.
CRAFT: Naval on openings from the reader's side — his own drop-rule for books, The Almanack, p. 116: "I'll start at the beginning, but I'll move fast. If it's not interesting, I'll just start flipping ahead, skimming, or speed reading. If it doesn't grab my attention within the first chapter in a meaningful, positive way, I'll either drop the book or skip ahead a few chapters. I don't believe in delayed gratification when there are an infinite number of books out there to read."

SOURCE: Naval Ravikant & Nivi (Babak Nivi) — "The Best Authors Respect the Reader's Time" (nav.al/density, Sep 24 2025)
OPENING [VERBATIM]: "Nivi: Unlike Schopenhauer, you are an industrial philosopher. Like an industrial designer, your philosophy is designed for the masses. People suggest you read the great books—Aristotle and Wittgenstein and all the supposedly great philosophers.

I've read almost all that stuff, and I've gotten very little value from it. […]

Naval: You're not wrong. I can't stand any of the philosophers you talked about. I don't like Plato either."
MOVE: Opens on the interlocutor's provocation, then concedes it in three words — the post's first move by the author is agreement, not argument.
WHY: Every nav.al post since 2019 opens this way: Nivi states a position at full strength, and Naval's first line is a short verdict on it ("You're not wrong"). LANE: craft-guidance
SUMMARY: Across this craft literature the openings converge on a single move: the first sentence carries the piece's payload rather than clearing ground for it. Graham, Orwell, Strunk, Pinker, Zinsser and Alexander all begin with a flat declarative — a claim about the world, a statement of the author's own practice, a statement of scope, or the plain occasion of the piece — and none of them buys attention before spending it. The stated principles match the specimens exactly: Pinker's classic style ("prose is a window onto the world") and Orwell's windowpane are the same rule, and both name self-presentation as the defect that produces bad openings; Graham's "just say the most important sentence first" is that rule in operational form. Where two of these writers do use a question or an example up front (Graham's "What should an essay be?", Alexander's "start with a concrete example"), it is a question the piece genuinely answers or a case the argument is built from, never a device that withholds. The common enemy across all ten sources is metadiscourse and hedging — writing about the writing, or apologizing for it before starting — which is structurally what a hook is: a paragraph spent on the transaction instead of the subject.
