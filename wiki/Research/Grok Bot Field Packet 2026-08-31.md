# Grok Bot Field Packet 2026-08-31

On 31 August 2026 the company's guides hub listed five practical playbooks and no others. The 15 August packet had compiled official docs and named public writeups. It had not opened that hub. The five pages published there are in-house playbooks, dated 15 August through 27 August 2026. They are the strongest public account here of how people who ship the product run it.

## The five playbooks on the hub that day

The 15 August playbook is written for product managers. Most PMs, it says, manage a product rather than a team that reports to them. An agent team can change that. Weekly priority lists go stale, so one pattern is an attention list: every hour, review mail, chat, meeting notes, and calendar, and write down the projects actually in focus and what happens next. Agents can then filter noise against that list, and you can compare stated goals with where attention really went. The same playbook says agents represented a double-digit percent of internal merged pull requests. The roster it describes includes a chief of staff who stays quiet when nothing changed and an engineering manager who does not code. Five engineering agents start cloud coding agents, alongside a data analyst, a product agent, and a recruiter. External mail, purchases, and deletes still wait for a person.

The 16 August playbook is written for go-to-market work. Onboard an agent the way you would a teammate. Record a task, take over the computer, demonstrate, and turn the recording into a skill. Feed it sent mail and a few pieces of outreach you are proud of before it drafts. When output goes wrong, make a rule or a skill and have the agent add to that skill each time you steer it. Parallel cloud agents can split work. A chief-of-staff agent can look across the roster for chores to automate, parallelize, or drop, and agents in a group chat can call specialists, such as a slides agent.

The 24 August playbook is written by a designer who uses agents to design the product itself. Four named roles cover repetitive production work in a design file, motion prototyping, open-ended experiments, and engineering questions. An experiments agent made three ambient ways to reach an agent (tucked into a laptop notch, peeking from a desktop corner, following the cursor), delegated motion and implementation, and brought back prototypes to live with. None of those three shipped as explored. The point was to make the idea real enough to judge. Motion work used the real production asset rather than a screenshot. Short comparative notes, such as "hold the look longer" or "bounce is too exaggerated," replaced guessing whether a transition should last 280 milliseconds or 340 milliseconds before seeing it. A production agent filled 20 starter-card portraits from one component and placed tool logos by inspecting the actual file through a connector, rather than eyeballing spacing.

The 25 August playbook describes a six-agent mobile studio around a game that had just passed 1,000 downloads across two phone stores in the past week. About 15 percent of the job is the game itself, it says, and about 85 percent is acquisition, creative, client engineering, backend, release, and QA. Each agent has a job description, connections, a cloud computer, routines, skills, and the ability to hand work to another agent without routing every pass through a person. A mobile orchestrator routes work and owns the store listings. An analytics agent is the only one allowed to declare a finding. A creatives seat turns those findings into advertisement specs and does not buy the media.

The 27 August playbook treats multiple projects as human teams would. Each project gets a channel and a board entry. A projects manager agent creates the project, opens the channel, and staffs it. Existing agents are reused first. The writer proposes at most 5 agents besides the project manager, for a channel cap of 6 agents, which is described as an arbitrary limit. A new agent appears only when no current seat fits, and only once a person says yes. When an agent gets stuck, it marks the task blocked and pings the channel.

## What this packet does not do

The packet does not turn these playbooks into a spec that overrides first-party docs. It does not add later guides that were not on the hub that day. It does not decide which personal examples a reader should copy.

## Open questions

If my next project channel reused bots I already had, and added a new one only after I said yes, how many extra specialists would I still invent?

Which part of my work is an attention list that should emerge from the day, rather than a priority list I wrote on Monday?
