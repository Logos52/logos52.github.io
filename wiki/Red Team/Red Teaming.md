# Red Teaming

Red teaming is a disciplined practice for finding what is wrong with a plan before reality does, and for stress-testing the reasoning behind a frame or a claim. It looks as hard at the people making the plan as at the plan itself. The US Army's University of Foreign Military and Cultural Studies (UFMCS), set up under TRADOC in the Afghanistan era, teaches four woven principles: self-awareness, groupthink mitigation, cultural empathy, and applied critical thinking. None of the fourth works without the first. Plans usually fail through many small shortcuts that each look close enough; red teamers counter them with tools that make people write before they talk, hear the most junior voice first, and imagine the plan has already failed. The same habits apply off the battlefield—deadlines, persuasive copy, and arguments where your own reasoning becomes the reference point.

## What training installs

The school's aim is reflexes, not a body of knowledge. Under deadline pressure the mind fills gaps with assumptions, judges events by whether they fit prior beliefs, reuses last time's solution, and settles for good enough. Mirror imaging—treating other actors as if they reason like you—is one fruit of that autopilot. The common pattern red teamers catch is cognitive autopilot: answering problem A with solution B because B worked last time, without checking whether this A has the same cause. Quick tools make the checks fit inside real decision time.

A first-hand account from service around 2010 stresses what surprised the student most: cultural empathy and why people act as they do outweighed tactics and weapons. There is no universal normal; you take another group's patterns as they are, without filtering them through your ideals. PMESII-PT and frameworks like Four Ways of Seeing lay out how each actor sees the situation. Done well, red teaming is empathy paired with strict assumption challenge—not aggression as default content. The public *Red Team Handbook* remains one of the best references.

## The plan that fails

The failure red teaming targets is an ordinary planning meeting. Shared training, experience, and chain of command make the room run smoothly. Decisions track what the group thinks the leader wants, what seniors propose, and what everyone already knows. The plan launches and then fails—because the leader was misread, because shared knowledge was wrong, because habits skipped the murky parts, or because a junior person saw the problem and stayed quiet. Each failure grows from effort-saving habits: agreeing wins friends, reusing solutions on lookalike problems, assuming you know more than you do, deferring to loud voices. Groups amplify every habit. Collapse arrives by degrees.

**Self-awareness** means knowing which beliefs guide you, which experiences produced them, and why you decide as you do—so you can slow down and act more on logic than on emotion.

**Groupthink mitigation** counters unseen pressure to agree and mind-guards who steer away from certain lines. Fixes: hear the most junior member first, weigh ideas anonymously, give time to think and write before anyone speaks.

**Cultural empathy** asks why another group behaves as it does, even when the behavior is abhorrent, so the response can be effective.

**Applied critical thinking** takes arguments apart, tests analogies, questions assumptions, and hunts alternatives. It is also how you navigate daily social and verbal games whose signal is often body language and silence, not explicit rules.

## The challenge loop

A challenge runs as divergent thinking, analysis, debate, and convergent thinking—a feedback loop that stops only when time runs out. Tools serve critical thinking or groupthink mitigation throughout.

Four tools span solo and group use:

- **Premortem:** assume the plan failed; each person writes reasons alone; pool lists without filtering.
- **Key assumptions check:** list every premise the plan needs; question each, including premises that were once true.
- **Circle of Voices:** five or six people get 60 seconds of silence, then one uninterrupted minute each before anyone speaks twice.
- **Dot voting:** anonymous cards, limited votes (e.g. 7 votes on 12 options) to surface weight.

Teams beat solo review only with candor, trust, and anti-groupthink habits.

## Testing frames and arguments

Critical thinking outside a war room means holding your current view as the least-bad option you have found and asking whether the other side offers anything better. Listing flaws in your position does not, by itself, prove a stronger alternative exists—a broken democracy can still beat its replacements. Listen for the replacement, not only the complaint. Teams often put the group first even when the next move fails a clear-thinking test; narrative structure (who is hero, villain, what exit is forbidden) explains why alliances stick.

Truth here is accuracy, not a moral badge. The closer your picture to what is going on, the better you function—in business, in public writing, in relationships. An emotional story that confirms what you already feel pulls you away from that picture. Testing a frame is the habit of asking whether a better account is on the table and refusing to treat a defect list as if it were that account.

**Epistemic exceptionalism** is when your reasoning becomes the reference: others' failure to agree proves their defect, never yours. A safety framework says someone must hold the keys; the analysis keeps finding every other key holder untrustworthy, so the framework outputs the person running it no matter what goes in. Under stress, word choice gives the tell—calling a clash of interests a "misunderstanding" presumes agreement would follow from correct understanding. The same loop appears on charts where revenue down and usage up both confirm the thesis. Earned confidence names what would change it; a closed loop cannot.

Run the check before a position hardens into policy or a large bet, especially when only your group can be trusted. Two questions: what answers could your framework produce besides you? What specific evidence would make you conclude you were wrong—not that others failed to understand? Naming financial stake does not repair the loop.

## The Twitter test on persuasive text

For unfamiliar persuasive text, pretend the writer faced a strict character limit so every word is a choice. For each word or image, ask why it is there and what feeling it aims to stir. **Value words** carry emotion (positive vs negative) and show what the text is selling. After themes are clear, tag each technique to the theme it serves—repeated positive words, rhetorical questions, statistics, borrowed authority. Check who paid for the text last; that explains why the themes matter. Practice slowly so the habit runs under exam pressure. Do the steps in order: themes, why they matter, then techniques—otherwise you get a device list with no point.

## Standing for the finding

A finding has no value until someone with authority acts. The finder must explain cost and risk in terms leaders can follow. That is hardest for juniors, because hierarchies deter challenges to how things are done, and original ideas are harder for newcomers to parse. The handbook presents its tools as practice for decision makers, not proven treatments; whether any single tool improves outcomes is unsettled.

## How to practice this

1. Before committing resources, play the adversary against your plan and list unstated assumptions.
2. Describe how the other side thinks without filtering through your normal.
3. Run a premortem or assumptions check alone or with a group that has trust and candor.
4. Hold your view as least-bad until a better account appears; when you lose an argument, write the plain reading before the flattering one.
5. On persuasive text, mark value words and tie each technique to its theme; note who paid.
6. Before a firm stance, ask what evidence would count against you and who besides you your framework would trust.
