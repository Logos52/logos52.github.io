# The Twitter Test

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

The Twitter test is a way to find the themes in an unfamiliar text by pretending the writer worked under a strict character limit. Under a limit, nothing gets in by accident, so every word, phrase and image the writer kept earns its place. For each, you ask why that exact word is there, what point the writer is making, and what feeling it is meant to stir. Value words are the words that carry emotion. They sort into positive and negative, and together they show what the text is selling. Knowing who paid for the text explains why those themes matter to the writer. The last step ties each persuasive technique back to the theme it serves, which leaves a list pairing each technique with its theme. The test is slow on purpose, because it is practice for a skill that should run without effort by exam day.

## Core takeaways

- Read an unfamiliar text as if the writer had a strict character limit, so every word counts as a choice.
- For each choice, ask why that word is there and what it wants you to feel.
- Value words carry emotion and sort into positive and negative, which shows what the text is selling.
- Knowing who paid for the text explains why its themes matter.
- The finished product is a list of techniques, each tied to the theme it serves.
- The walkthrough is slow on purpose, so the habit runs without effort under exam time.

## What to run it on

A minimum word count invites filler, since a writer short of 2,000 words will add words nobody needs. A maximum does the opposite. A writer allowed only 200 words keeps only the words that do work. Reading as if the text sat under a hard cap turns every word into a decision, and a decision has a motive you can ask about.

A worked example uses a past state exam text, an open letter from a local grocery store announcing that it will stop taking cash. The method is the same across exam systems, because only the article and the question change.

## Start with the headline and the picture

The heading promises a better, faster shopping experience, which reads as plainly good and happy. The photo shows a handshake and a smiling person, and since the writer could have picked any photo, that choice adds a sense of cooperation. The greeting calls the readers valued customers in an open letter. "Letter" and "customers" just name what the text is, while "valued" and "open" are there to make readers feel appreciated and to signal that the store has nothing to hide.

## Sorting words by the feeling they carry

Small phrases also get the question of why each word is there. "As you know" spends precious characters to make a statement feel like common knowledge that nobody would question. "Not your average grocery store" makes the store feel unique. The store says it "abolished" plastic bags, a much stronger word than removed, and adds "long before the big stores," which is a comparison that puts the store above its rivals. Further down, cash comes with words like "rummaging," "wads of cash" and pickpockets. Those words make paying with cash feel risky and old-fashioned.

## Naming the devices

Once the themes are clear, you go back through the text and tag each technique with the theme it serves. The example has several.

- Repeated positive words, such as better and faster, set the happy, cooperative tone.
- Value words carry the emotion. Valued, open, embrace and simple are positive, while rummaging and worry are negative.
- A rhetorical question pulls the reader in and puts weight on what follows it.
- A statistic, that cash once made up 70 percent of household spending and now makes up about half that, makes the shift away from cash look real and credible.
- The name of a central bank governor lends authority to the same theme.

The finished list of techniques mapped to themes is what the later writing steps build on, and once it exists the analysis is easy to write.

## Check who gains, last

The text turns out to be a paid advertisement written for the store. That explains why the themes matter: a store making a big change wants that change to look positive, so it uses its words to steer how the reader feels. The question to keep asking is what the writer wants you to feel, and how they are trying to make you feel it.

## Practice slowly, read at speed

The full walkthrough is far too slow for an exam. The point of running it many times beforehand is to reach the stage where you do it without thinking.

## Where this goes wrong

The main risk is doing the steps out of order. Jumping straight to techniques produces a list of devices with no point, because the themes and the reason they matter were never found. Each run should start with the themes, move to why they matter, and only then turn to the techniques.

## How to practice this

- Take a short persuasive text, such as an advertisement or an open letter.
- Go through the headline, the picture and the greeting, asking why each word or image was chosen.
- Mark every value word as positive or negative and name the theme they add up to.
- Find out who paid for the text, then tag each technique with the theme it serves.

## Open questions

- How does the Twitter test change when a text has no word limit at all, such as a long report?
- How does a reader notice value words in their own writing?
- How does knowing who paid for a text change which themes a reader looks for first?
