# The Twitter Test

The Twitter test is a way of reading a persuasive text one word at a time, on the assumption that the writer had a strict length limit and had to justify every word. Under that assumption, a word that carries no fact was kept for the feeling it gives. Running the test shows what a text is working to make you feel before you decide whether to agree with it.

- Read as if every word was paid for.
- Ask why each word is there and what it makes you feel.
- A word with no plain job is there to steer you.
- Ask who gains if you take on that feeling.
- Sort the words that carry a feeling into positive and negative.
- Drill it slowly on easy texts until it runs without effort.

## How it works

Twitter once capped a post at 140 characters, and later at 280. A writer under a cap keeps only the words that do a job, so every word left in is a decision. The test applies that assumption to any text, whether it was capped or not. For each word, phrase and picture, the reader asks why the writer chose it over a plainer one, what feeling it produces, and whose interest that feeling serves.

- Some words do plain work: an article, a name, the audience.
- Other words carry a feeling: "valued", "open", "simple".
- A strong word used where a weak one would do is a choice.
  - "Abolished" where "stopped" would carry the same fact.
- A phrase that fills space and adds no fact is steering.
  - "As you know" makes a disputed point sound already agreed.
- A photo counts too, since it was picked from many.

```
a word in the text
        |
        v
does it carry a plain fact?
   yes --> next word
   no  --> what does it make me feel?
                   |
                   v
           who gains if I feel it?
```

## A worked example

A shop prints a letter telling its customers that it will stop taking cash. The heading promises a "better, faster" experience, the first line reads "An open letter to our valued customers", and the photo shows a smiling handshake. "Open" and "valued" carry no fact, so their job is to make the reader feel honestly dealt with and appreciated. The letter is a paid advertisement, and the shop gains if its customers read the end of cash as good news.

- "Better, faster": two positive words before any fact arrives.
- "Long before the big stores": a comparison that flatters the shop.
- "Wads of cash" and "pickpockets": bad feelings tied to the old way.
- A percentage figure gives the change the weight of data.
- A named central bank official gives it authority.

## How to practise

Done properly, the test takes about a minute per sentence. That pace is too slow for a timed exam or a news feed, so the test is a drill for building a habit. The aim is to run it on many texts until the words that carry a feeling, the charged words, stand out on their own. Short texts that are openly persuasive make the best practice, because a wrong reading there costs nothing.

- Start with ads, open letters and press releases.
- Mark each charged word and write the feeling beside it.
- Group the feelings into two or three themes.
- Link each technique to the theme it serves.
  - Repetition, statistics, named authorities, rhetorical questions.
- Ask why that theme matters to the writer.

One more check is to swap a charged word for a flat one and see whether any fact changed.

## Limits

The test assumes that every word was chosen, and real writers also pad, repeat themselves and use words out of habit. A charged word still shows what the text does to the reader, even when the writer did not plan that effect. The test reads single words, so a bias carried by which facts were left out needs a different check.

- A charged word shows an effect on the reader, whatever the intent.
- Omitted facts do not show up in a word-by-word pass.
- Plain writing with few charged words returns few hits.
