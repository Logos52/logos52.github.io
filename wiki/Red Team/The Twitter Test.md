# The Twitter Test

The Twitter test is a way of reading a short text one word at a time. It starts from the assumption that the writer worked under a hard character limit, so every word still there was kept in place of some other word. Of each word that stays, the test asks three things: why this exact word, what feeling the word is meant to produce, and whose gain that feeling serves. The name comes from a hard character cap. The live product is not required, and the cap has been raised.

A writer padding toward a minimum adds filler. A writer squeezed by a maximum cuts everything that fails to advance the goal. The words left over are how the text tries to make you feel something, and the three questions show that work before you take on the writer's frame. [[Applied Critical Thinking - Testing Frames]] is the fast filter at the level of frames. The Twitter test works at the level of single words, on what feeling those words do.

## Core takeaways

- Under a character limit, every word still in the text was chosen over another word. The test reads each word as that kind of choice.
- The three questions are: why this word, what feeling it is meant to produce, and whose gain that feeling serves.
- A value word carries feeling on top of its dictionary meaning. Sorted into positive and negative, those words show what the text sells and what it wants abandoned.
- The sorting and the numbers belong to the text, not to the subject. The ad's 70% figure is the ad's figure, not a finding about cash.
- A finished pass produces a technique-to-theme list plus a named stake for the author. A pass that only lists devices is not finished.
- The word-by-word walk is for practice on low-stakes texts. Live reading stays theme first, technique second.

## What to run it on

Run it on ads, open letters, op-eds, press releases, landing pages, and fundraising emails. Take one short text at a time. One supermarket flyer is enough to learn the walk.

## Start with the headline and the picture

Read the headline and the image before the body. The writer put the most work into them.

A grocery-store ad for going cashless shows how this goes. The headline repeats positive words, "better, faster". The photo shows people smiling and shaking hands. Both set the reader's mood before the reader has read a sentence. The photo is a handshake and not the storefront. The facts of the offer can stay exactly as they are while the feeling changes.

## Sorting words by the feeling they carry

Check every word. The ones worth looking at are the words with no job to do. The ad says "valued customers" where "customers" would serve. It says "Open" where the store's hours were not the point. A **value word** carries feeling on top of its dictionary meaning. Sort those words by charge: positive or negative.

"As you know" uses scarce words to make a contested claim feel like something everyone already agrees with. Ask what each digression is there for. "Long before the big stores" is there to set up a comparison. A feeling that comes back more than once is a theme.

"Abolished" where "removed" or "got rid of" would serve is a deliberate escalation. The positive words gather on what the ad sells. The negative words gather on what the ad wants the reader to abandon. In this ad that split is cashless against cash. The grouping is the ad's. It is not a fact about cash.

## Naming the devices

Tag each device as you meet it: repetition, rhetorical questions, statistics, named authorities. Each has a standard use. Repetition raises the feeling. A rhetorical question imitates a conversation that is not happening. A statistic supplies certainty from outside the text. A named authority supplies authority from outside the text. The statistic in this ad is that 70% of household spending is no longer in cash. It is there to make "everyone has already moved on" sound credible. That number is the ad's number. It is not a finding about cash.

## Check who gains, last

Do the agenda check at the end. A paid ad written by the business it praises has one job. The themes should trace back to what that business gains. One full pass produces a **technique-to-theme list**, with each device tagged by the feeling or the agenda it serves. The same pass produces a statement of what the author has at stake. ## Practice slowly, read at speed

The word-by-word walk is training mode. Do not use it on a live text. Practice on low-stakes texts: flyers, newsletters, product pages. A supermarket flyer and the three questions is this method in use.

[[Theme-First Text Analysis]] is the companion page. Its order is the live order: theme first, then technique. The walk on this page tags devices first because that is how the eye gets built. In live reading the leftover words stand out without the walk, and the order stays theme first.

The goal is to stop needing the walk. Loaded words stand out on their own. The agenda is visible without a pass. Either the words left over read that way after practice, or they do not and you take on the frame.

## Where this goes wrong

Treating a text as written under a hard cap when it was not can invent an intention the writer never had. The method builds a reading eye. It does not prove a conspiracy. The first pass is slow, and the grocery-store walk is what that costs.

The sign to quit is a real op-ed that still requires the walk. By the time the walk is done there, the frame is already in.

A flyer pass counts as done when it produces a short technique-to-theme list and names one stake. A pass that only lists devices does not count.

This method works at the level of single words. The filter that works at the level of frames is [[Applied Critical Thinking - Testing Frames]].

## How to practice this

1. Pick one short text: a supermarket flyer, an ad, a press release, or a fundraising email. Notice that one flyer is enough to learn the walk.
2. Read the headline and the image before the body. Notice what mood they set before you have read a sentence.
3. Check every word in the body. Mark the words that carry feeling on top of their dictionary meaning. Notice which words have no other job.
4. Sort the marked words into positive and negative. Notice that the positive ones gather on what the text sells. Notice that the negative ones gather on what it wants abandoned.
5. Tag each device you meet: repetition, rhetorical questions, statistics, named authorities. Notice what feeling each one is there to produce.
6. Ask last who wrote the text and what they gain. Check that every theme traces back to that gain. You are done when you have a technique-to-theme list and one named stake.
