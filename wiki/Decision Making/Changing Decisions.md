# Changing Decisions

Changing a decision is hard for reasons that sit in the mind before any spreadsheet appears. Sunk cost is the pull to stay because you have already spent effort or money on the current path. Status quo bias is the pull to leave things as they are. Loss aversion is the pull to avoid a loss even when an equal or slightly smaller gain sits on the other side. The gain often has to look 3 times or even 10 times larger than the loss before the change feels worth it. Those pulls are old, risk-averse habits. They are not a proof that the original choice was right.

No choice is sure to be the best one available, especially when you made it with little information. Sometimes the better move is to change it.

## New information concentrates the odds

A well-known puzzle shows why. There are 3 doors. A prize sits behind one of them, and the other two doors hide nothing you want. You pick the first door. Someone who knows what is behind each door then opens a losing door among the two remaining doors you did not pick, and asks whether you want to switch to the door that remains. Your first pick still has about a 33 percent chance of being right, the chance it had when you chose with no extra information. The remaining closed door now carries about a 66 percent chance. Switching is the stronger bet.

The same pattern is easier to see at a larger scale. Guess a number in a range that runs up to 1 million. That first guess has 1 chance in 1 million. If every other number is then ruled out except your guess and one other, almost all of the leftover chance sits on that other number. Your original guess is still worth 1 chance in 1 million. The new information did not improve the first pick. It piled the remaining probability onto the option that survived.

A career chosen in the teenage years often looks like that first pick. The picture of the work is vague, sometimes unrealistic, and alternatives were not really explored. Later, once you are closer to the work or already in it, you learn what it actually involves and what you need from it. That learning concentrates the odds, so a change of direction can be the right move.

## Persistence and a change of mind

People are often told that success means digging in and never quitting. Successful people do know when to persist. They also know when to quit or change the decision. You need persistence when the choice still fits your goals, values, and priorities. A fast course change, once the choice no longer matches those aims, shows someone who can still think clearly about the facts in front of them.

You are not stuck on a rail you cannot leave. Even when the change feels frightening, it is a chance to correct an earlier choice that was made with less information. Looking back at old decisions, and accepting that they were not perfect, is part of a process that tends to produce better results over time.

## Open questions

How much of my wish to stay with this choice comes from what I have already spent on it, rather than from what I now know?

If someone opened a door I could not see when I first chose, what would switching look like in this case?
