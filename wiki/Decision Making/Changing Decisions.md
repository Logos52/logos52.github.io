# Changing Decisions

Changing a decision means dropping a course already chosen, such as a degree, a job or a project, and taking a different one. Most decisions are made with less information than arrives later, so the first choice is rarely the best one available. Knowing what keeps a person on the old course, and how new information shifts the odds, makes it easier to tell persistence from being stuck.

## Core takeaways

- No decision is permanent.
- A choice made with little information is rarely the best one.
- Four pulls keep people on a course after it stops fitting.
- New information can make the untaken option the better bet.
- Stay when the course still fits your goals and values.
- Change when it no longer fits, and treat that as a correction.

## The four pulls

Four pulls keep a person on a chosen path, meaning reasons to stay that have nothing to do with where the path leads. Sunk cost, status quo bias and loss aversion are biases with a research record, and grit is a habit that society praises. Each one makes staying feel safer than it is. Naming the pull while it is happening is the first step to overriding it.

- Sunk cost: staying because of what is already spent.
- Status quo bias: preferring whatever is already in motion.
- Loss aversion: a loss feels about twice as heavy as an equal gain.
- Grit with no exit test: pushing on without asking whether to stop.

## How new information shifts the odds

A game show puzzle named after the host Monty Hall shows the effect. There are three doors, a prize behind a single door, and you pick door 1. The host knows where the prize is, opens a door you did not pick to show it is empty, and offers you a switch. Your door kept the one in three chance it had when you chose it, so the last closed door now carries two in three.

```
pick:    [1]   2    3      your door 1/3
host:    [1]   2    x      opens an empty door
now:     [1]   2           door 1: 1/3, door 2: 2/3
```

- The odds of the first pick were fixed when it was made.
- Information that arrives later lands on the options still open.
- With a million numbers instead of three doors, switching almost always wins.
- The same holds for choices made young with a vague picture.
- A career chosen at seventeen, then learned from inside, is the same case.

## When to stay and when to change

Resilience matters when the decision still matches what you want from it. The same effort spent on a course that no longer matches is rigidity. Quitting at the right time is a skill, and successful people use it often. Looking back at a past choice and accepting that it was imperfect tends to improve later choices.

- Check the decision against your current goals, values and priorities.
- If it fits, keep going through the hard parts.
- If it does not, change without treating it as defeat.
- Write down what you now know that you did not know then.
