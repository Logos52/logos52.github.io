# Positional Decisions and Expected Value

A positional decision is a choice made because it leaves you in a better place for the choices that follow, with more skill, more options and more access to opportunities. When the future is too uncertain to predict, asking whether a choice improves your position is more useful than asking whether it will turn out well. Expected value is the check that tells you whether a way of choosing pays off over many decisions.

## Takeaways

- Ask whether a choice puts you in a better position.
- Nobody can plan more than a few moves ahead.
- Expected value judges a way of choosing across many repeats.
- Short runs of results are mostly luck.
- Choosing only to avoid discomfort shrinks your options over time.
- Protect against the biggest downside before anything else.

## How position works

Chess shows the idea. After the second or third move the possible games branch so widely that planning further is guesswork. Strong players make moves that are good whatever the opponent does next, such as bringing pieces into play. The same applies to careers, study and money: a good position means that when a chance appears, you are able to take it.

- A better position has more influence and more open options.
- Make moves that help across many possible futures.
- Let the possibilities narrow over time before committing.

## How expected value works

Every way of choosing has an expected value, meaning the average result over many repeats. A casino game shows it: pay $5 to roll a die, and win $10 on a six. Over six rolls you pay $30 and win back $10 on average. A player can win the first few rolls, and the longer they play, the closer they get to the real odds.

```
cost:   6 rolls x $5  = $30
return: 1 six   x $10 = $10
average result:        -$20
```

- A positive-value process wins over time despite single losses.
- A negative-value process loses more the longer you use it.
- A week or a month is too little data to judge.
- Judge a way of choosing over years.

## Choosing for comfort

Choices made mainly to avoid fear feel safe each time. Taken together they close off opportunities, and the position gets worse. Feelings are a fair input, but a process run on feelings alone has negative expected value. The positive alternative is to take on hard things in small steps, build skills and a tolerance for discomfort, and let the position improve.

- Choices made only for comfort leave fewer options later.
- Position usually improves in small steps.
- A windfall is wasted if the way of choosing stays the same.

## Protecting the downside

Before weighing gains, find the largest thing the choice could cost you. Ask how much that loss matters to you and whether you can protect against it. A significant loss you cannot protect against means no. If you do not know, gather information until you do.

- Name the biggest thing you could lose.
- Decide how much that loss matters to you.
- Ask whether you can protect against it.
  - Yes: set up the protection, then go ahead.
  - No, and it matters: do not go ahead.
  - Unsure: find out before deciding.
