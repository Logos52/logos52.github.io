# Choice Throttling

Too many options make it harder to choose. A long restaurant menu has this effect. A list of products with many prices and many features has it too. The easy move, when the pile feels like too much, is to choose nothing. That pattern is choice overload. Choice throttling is a way to control what arrives. Put a gate on the options and let them through one at a time.

Take the large decision and break it into single questions that, stacked together, make the final call. Each question should be one you can answer yes or no. After each answer, whole branches drop away, and you only keep thinking about the path that is still open.

A university choice can start with a single filter. Are you willing to leave your city or state? If the answer is no, unless one named school is the exception, every distant option closes. The next question might be whether you are willing to apply for a particular scholarship and do what it requires. Yes means you can list those steps and do them. No means you return to the previous branch and split what remains into new single questions. You move from a wide, open field to a narrower and narrower path.

The same chain works on work decisions. If a whole category of approach violates something you will not do, you do not then compare every variant inside that category. The principle closes the branch.

## What the chain needs, and what it is not

Your ability to run the chain depends on how clearly you can see your own values, how well you can reflect, and how much you actually know about the options and their consequences. Those are skills you can train. A simpler question is not always a faster one. Answering a single yes or no about which values matter most can take months.

Some decisions are still too large or too tangled for this method alone. Daily and mid-sized choices are where the method is most useful. For the most important and complicated calls, a more careful framework sits on top of it.

## Open questions

Which part of this decision am I still treating as one giant choice, and what would the first yes-or-no filter be?

How many options am I still weighing even though I already know I would not take that branch?
