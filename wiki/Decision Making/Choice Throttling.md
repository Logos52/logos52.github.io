# Choice Throttling

Choice throttling is a way to make a tangled decision by breaking it into a chain of yes or no questions, each narrowing the options left. A choice with many moving parts, such as which university to attend or whether to leave a career, becomes a set of steps small enough to answer one at a time.

## Core takeaways

- Ask one yes or no question at a time.
- Order the questions from broad to narrow.
- A yes leads to the next question or to concrete steps.
- A no sends you back to the previous branch.
- Drop whole branches you would never take on principle.
- A simple question can still take months to answer honestly.
- It suits everyday decisions, and the heaviest ones need a fuller method.

## How it works

Faced with many options at once, people often choose nothing at all, an effect called choice overload. A shop with six price tiers and ten features per tier can lose the sale this way. Choice throttling puts a gate in front of the options, so that only one question is open at a time. The structure is the same as a decision tree, or the identification key a biologist uses to name a plant.

- Write the broadest question that splits the options.
- Answer it yes or no.
- Follow that branch to the next question.
- On a dead end, go back one branch and try the other variables.
- Stop when the real options are down to one or two.

```
move to another city?
   no --> only local universities
   yes --> apply for a scholarship?
              yes --> list the application steps
              no  --> back: other variables
```

## How to order the questions

Put first the questions that remove the most options. A question about something you refuse to do on principle removes a whole branch at once, and skipping it leaves you weighing options you would never take. In a worked case of leaving a medical career, the questions ran in this order: fulfilment, then money, then status, then fear, then income during the change. The fear question became a yes once it was clear that the move could be reversed by going back.

- Principles first, since they cut the most.
- Then the variables that matter most to you.
- Then the risks, asking whether each can be reduced.
- Last, the practical checks, such as money for six months.

## Where it fails

The method is only as good as the answers fed into it. A person who does not know their own values will answer the key questions badly or slowly. The method also has a limit on the size of decision it can carry. For decisions with large, long-term consequences, a slower method that weighs chances and downsides is the better tool.

- Answers depend on self-awareness and on information about the choice.
- A values question can take months to settle.
- Choice overload itself is weak: fifty experiments averaged near zero effect.
- The method's value comes from putting the questions in order.
- The heaviest decisions need a fuller framework.
