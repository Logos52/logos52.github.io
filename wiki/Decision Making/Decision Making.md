<h1>Decision Making</h1>
</div>

The quality of a decision is fixed at the moment it is made, before any outcome exists to judge it by. The pages in this section put that claim to work on four frictions that keep returning: what to do next, when to commit, when to change course, and how to tell afterwards whether a choice was good or just lucky. Handled at the right weight, choosing under uncertainty preserves time, focus, and adaptability instead of draining them.

## What Counts as a Good Decision

A decision splits into two objects: the process that chose, and the outcome that followed. Only the first is yours. An outcome arrives mixed with luck, timing, information that had not landed, and conditions that changed after the choosing — it mostly measures what the decision never controlled. The process is the improvable part, and improving it takes a written loop rather than an intention, because it is a black box of sub-processes, some running below awareness.

Outcomes still count as feedback about the process — one piece of information, never the verdict — and a sound process kept running collects the better outcomes on the probabilities, so one disappointing result is no reason to quit it. The pull the other way is measurable: across five experiments since replicated with modern samples, the same decision, described identically, was rated as better thinking — its maker as more competent — when the outcome happened to be good, by judges who said outcomes should not count and counted them anyway. The outcome leaks in while the judge is trying to keep it out. Improving process also outpays analysis: across 1,048 major strategic decisions tracked over five years, process explained roughly six times more of the variance in quality than depth of analysis — what a better process beats is more thinking about the same decision. The review that runs afterwards — six questions separating what was known then from what is known now, and the failure table for hindsight — is [[wiki/Decision Making/Judging a Decision by Its Process|Good Decisions]].

## How Much Thinking a Decision Is Worth

Not every decision deserves the same amount of thinking — a heavy framework on daily choices produces paralysis, small-choice speed on a major commitment produces damage — and two independent dials set the weight. Stakes come first, through one gate: the largest thing there is to lose gets named, and a trivial worst case skips the apparatus. Reversibility is the second dial, not a synonym for low stakes: a one-way door is walked through slowly and with consultation, a two-way door fast, because a wrong two-way-door decision costs only the walk back. A high-stakes but reversible choice — a study method tried for a fortnight, a job that can be left — gets made fast and reviewed on a date; a small irreversible one gets more thought than its size suggests.

| Tier | The decision | Looks like | The tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Low consequence, low complexity | daily choices, small preferences | the everyday filter or a throttled question chain |
| 2 | Execution | what next, when to switch, protecting focus | planning — the choice pre-made |
| 3 | High uncertainty, real consequence | study plans, careers, commitments | the loop below |
| 4 | New information | changing course, quitting, recommitting | re-evaluation; consistency is owed nothing |

The everyday filter: a clear yes or no is acted on at once, and the grey middle usually matters less than it feels — grey means more variables in play, more of them actionable, so the capacity to cope either way shrinks the stakes. The filter stops where a consequence could be serious; until then it trains decisiveness, its keep earned on choices that do not matter. Indecision gets no pass either: not choosing is a choice, opportunity traded for comfort while the clock runs, defensible only when made knowingly.

The tiers are measured, not aesthetic: strategies get switched by task, and simple heuristics land near full formal accuracy at a fraction of the effort, with no single heuristic best across all conditions. One premise fails — option count alone does not paralyse; fifty pooled experiments put the mean effect near zero. [[wiki/Decision Making/Choice Throttling|Choice Throttling]] earns its seat differently: an ordered chain of closed questions deleting whole branches unexamined and showing which information is missing. The tier-2 gap between tasks — audited, then removed by planning — is [[wiki/Decision Making/Decisional Delays|Decisional Delays]].

## The Five Recurring Frictions

Beneath most weak decision patterns sits one confusion: comfort mistaken for quality. Five shapes of it keep returning, each with an owning page.

| The feeling | What is happening |
|---|---|
| Changing course feels costly | the current option is kept for being current |
| More research feels safer | information is raising confidence faster than accuracy |
| The thinking will not close | overthinking has looped — thought circling one feeling without moving |
| The result seems to settle it | the outcome is being read back into the decision |
| A small choice eats real focus | the weight is wrong for the tier |

The first is measured — people disproportionately keep what they hold, the pull growing with the number of alternatives — and its repair, the four-bias check and the seven-step change review, is [[wiki/Decision Making/Changing Decisions|Changing Decisions]]. The second is a mechanism rather than a character flaw: judges handed more information gained confidence substantially faster than accuracy, never discounting for being unable to use it, so the search feels productive well past where it pays; when to stop waiting is [[wiki/Decision Making/The Uncertainty-Opportunity Tradeoff|The Uncertainty-Opportunity Tradeoff]], where certainty is bought with opportunity because the two fall together. The third repairs with direction rather than reduction — the feeling named in writing, turned into a discrete problem list, an action set per item, the uncontrollable marked as accepted — at [[wiki/Dimensions/Mindset/Perfectionism and Overthinking|Perfectionism and Overthinking]]. Exhaustive search buys the wrong thing anyway: students who searched exhaustively landed jobs paying 20% more and liked them less, with more negative affect throughout — the reason to stop that survives "something better might exist." The fourth is the outcome bias above, repaired at step 7. The fifth has its instrument in the gate and the tiers; its costliest case, a recurring "what to do next," is never only a planning problem.

## The Loop

A decision that clears the gate runs eight steps.

1. **The decision gets a name** — one sentence saying what is actually being chosen.
2. **The consequence level gets assigned.** The biggest downsides get named; each is rated for significance — absolute (unacceptable at any level) or relative (unacceptable only if the opportunity is too small to carry it) — and for cappability, with yes, no, or *I don't know* allowed on both. Significant and uncappable is an automatic no, nothing further to weigh; *I don't know* routes to collecting information, never to guessing.
3. **The variables creating the uncertainty get listed.**
4. **The next move gets picked: decide, gather information, reduce the risk, or wait.** Gathering and waiting are moves with costs, not defaults, so the gathering branch carries a stopping rule: the missing fact, which way it would flip the choice, what it costs to get. A fact that cannot flip the choice is not worth buying — "more research" becomes a timed task or a decision, and the timed form is the two-front sprint on [[wiki/Decision Making/The Uncertainty-Opportunity Tradeoff|The Uncertainty-Opportunity Tradeoff]].
5. **The cap on the biggest downside gets installed and executed, not imagined.** Protection can also reshape a risk instead of declining the decision:

```text
A new study method risks a few hours a week for two months. If that loss is too
significant, the trial runs concentrated — a fortnight, or one weekend, with early
feedback — so less time is exposed and the loss lands in an acceptable shape.
A reshaped downside can flip a no into a yes.
```

6. **The choice goes to the option that improves position or carries the better expected value** — a working stance, not a finding — no study shows it beats outcome-estimating for individuals — held because it leaves more usable options standing afterwards. One look goes outward first: what happened the last three times a plan of this shape was made? Inside views run optimistic — cost forecasts landing about 44% over on rail and 20% on roads, demand overestimated by about half — and a personal history is free. The stance is [[wiki/Decision Making/Positional Decisions and Expected Value|Positional Decisions and Expected Value]]; the arithmetic and its bands are [[wiki/Decision Making/Expectancy in Wicked Environments|Expectancy in Wicked Environments]].
7. **The reason gets written down before the outcome is known, with a probability and a falsifier.** Outcome knowledge raises the remembered likelihood of that outcome and silently re-weights which facts seemed relevant, so the pre-outcome record is the only copy of the belief that survives. The probability lets the record be wrong — without it the outcome has nothing to correct, and the log decays into a diary — and the number trains: one hour of probability practice with feedback improved forecasting accuracy by roughly 12%, 6%, and 7% in years two, three, and four against untrained controls, mostly by cutting overconfidence.
8. **The outcome returns as feedback about the process.** [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management/Kolbs Experiential Cycle|Kolbs Experiential Cycle]] runs this step — act, observe, form a principle, test it next — and [[wiki/Dimensions/Mindset/Marginal Gains|Marginal Gains]] picks the direction: the next small compounding improvement.

## The Checks Worth Keeping

This layer borders [[wiki/Red Team/Red Teaming|Red Teaming]] and does a different job: Red Teaming stays an identity and a stance; this is the working layer for daily, academic, and career choices. The formal artifacts — matrices, dissent prompts, stakeholder lists, alternative hypotheses, premortems, explanation formats — a model now generates on demand, so the scarce skill is deciding whether any of the output matters. Four checks transfer, running inside the loop rather than beside it as separate rituals.

The assumptions check asks what has to be true for this choice to work. The dissent check ships repaired: assigned devil's-advocacy strengthens the position it was aimed at, while dissent from someone who holds the opposing view produces more original thinking, more genuine consideration of the opposite, and more attitude change — so the objection comes from a person who believes it, or the two strongest counter-reasons get written down with what would follow if each were true. Two, not ten: a longer list makes the alternative feel harder to imagine, and backfires. The perspective check asks who the choice lands on and what they see that is invisible from inside it — evidence substantial for asking real people, much thinner for imagining them. The premortem — the plan assumed dead, the story of how written out — surfaced about 30% more correct reasons for a future outcome: more failure paths seen, not better prediction, and in practice less motivated reasoning on a decision already leaned toward.

## Where This Sits

Decision making files under [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation|Self-Regulation]] — noticing in the moment that a strategy has stopped working — and [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management|Self-Management]], the systems that act without a daily spend of will. In a study system the load is six recurring decisions: what to study next, whether to keep encoding or switch to retrieval, whether a bad result means the method failed, whether to change the plan, how much time to spend collecting information, and whether a delay is strategic or avoidant. Two are not live: the encoding switch is settled most days — practice testing and distributed practice rate high-utility, rereading and highlighting low — and the bad-result question is answered by the split this page opened with once control is named — the more of the outcome that was controlled, the more the result says about the process, a scale [[wiki/Decision Making/Judging a Decision by Its Process|Good Decisions]] owns. Both point at the larger saving: past deciding faster sits deciding once — a recurring choice converted into a standing rule stops arriving, and what comes back is not the minutes but the decision itself. The cost was never a tank draining anyway — deliberation spends time and drops threads — and a choice made in advance takes no time at all.

The dropped thread is the expensive part. An unresolved "what next?" becomes a doorway into [[wiki/Self Management/Procrastination - a System Problem|Procrastination: a System Problem]], a break in [[wiki/Self Management/Flow State|Flow State]], or a drain on attention: attention stays with the prior task, especially one left unfinished, and the residue does not fade on its own — though writing down where the work stands and what comes next removes the measurable part. Inside one block, entry, drift-catching, and recovery belong to [[wiki/Self Management/Focus Management - How to Enter & Recover Inside a Work Block|Focus Management: How to Enter & Recover Inside a Work Block]]; across the seams, [[wiki/Self Management/Attention Management - Preserving Flow|Attention Management: Preserving Flow]] keeps the next block from starting at a standstill. Every one of these choices was as good as it would ever be at the moment it was made. The ones this section serves best stop being made at all.
