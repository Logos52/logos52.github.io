<h1>Decision Making</h1>
</div>

The quality of a decision is fixed at the moment it is made, before any outcome exists to judge it by. The pages in this section apply that claim to four recurring problems: what to do next, when to commit, when to change course, and how to tell afterwards whether a choice was good or just lucky. When the amount of thinking fits the decision, choosing under uncertainty saves time, focus, and adaptability instead of using them up.

## What Counts as a Good Decision

A decision has two parts: the process that made the choice, and the outcome that followed. Only the process is yours. An outcome is mixed with luck, timing, information that had not yet arrived, and conditions that changed after the choice was made, so it mostly measures things the decision never controlled. The process is the part that can be improved. Improving it takes a written loop, and an intention is not enough, because the process is made of sub-processes that cannot be observed directly, and some of them run below awareness.

Outcomes still count as feedback about the process. An outcome is one piece of information about the process and does not by itself decide whether the process was good. A sound process that stays in use gets the better outcomes over many decisions because the probabilities favour it, so one disappointing result is no reason to stop using it. The tendency to judge a decision by its outcome has been measured. In five experiments, since replicated with modern samples, judges rated the same decision, described in the same words, as better thinking, and rated the person who made it as more competent, when the outcome happened to be good. The judges said outcomes should not count, and their ratings counted them anyway. The outcome affected their judgment while they were trying to exclude it. Improving the process also does more than adding analysis. Across 1,048 major strategic decisions tracked over five years, process explained roughly six times more of the variance in decision quality than depth of analysis did. A better process therefore does more for a decision than more thinking about the same decision. The review done after a decision, with six questions that separate what was known then from what is known now and a table of the failures caused by hindsight, is on [[wiki/Decision Making/Judging a Decision by Its Process|Good Decisions]].

## How Much Thinking a Decision Is Worth

Decisions do not all deserve the same amount of thinking. A heavy framework used on daily choices causes paralysis, and the speed suited to small choices causes damage when used on a major commitment. Two independent factors set how much thinking a decision gets. The first factor is stakes. The stakes check is one question: what is the largest thing that could be lost? A decision whose worst case is trivial skips the full procedure. The second factor is reversibility, and it is a separate question from whether the stakes are low. A decision that cannot be undone, a one-way door, is made slowly and with consultation. A decision that can be undone, a two-way door, is made fast, because a wrong two-way-door decision costs only the work of reversing it. A high-stakes choice that can be reversed, such as a study method tried for a fortnight or a job that can be left, is made fast and reviewed on a set date. A small choice that cannot be reversed gets more thought than its size suggests.

| Tier | The decision | Looks like | The tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Low consequence, low complexity | daily choices, small preferences | the everyday filter or a throttled question chain |
| 2 | Execution | what next, when to switch, protecting focus | planning: the choice made in advance |
| 3 | High uncertainty, real consequence | study plans, careers, commitments | the eight-step loop |
| 4 | New information | changing course, quitting, recommitting | re-evaluation; being consistent with the earlier choice is not a reason to keep it |

In the everyday filter, a clear yes or no is acted on at once. A choice that is neither a clear yes nor a clear no usually matters less than it feels. An unclear choice has more variables in play, and more of them can be acted on, so the ability to cope with either result lowers the stakes. The filter is not used where a consequence could be serious. Below that level it trains decisiveness, and choices that do not matter are where that training is worth doing. Indecision is not exempt either. Not choosing is also a choice: it gives up opportunity in exchange for comfort while time passes, and it is defensible only when it is made knowingly.

The tiers are based on measured findings. People switch decision strategies depending on the task, and simple heuristics come close to the accuracy of full formal methods with a fraction of the effort. No single heuristic is best in all conditions. One premise is false: the number of options alone does not cause paralysis, and a pooled analysis of fifty experiments put the mean effect near zero. [[wiki/Decision Making/Choice Throttling|Choice Throttling]] is included for a different reason. It is an ordered chain of closed questions in which each answer removes whole groups of options without examining them, and the chain shows which information is missing. The tier-2 gap between tasks, which is first audited and then removed by planning, is covered on [[wiki/Decision Making/Decisional Delays|Decisional Delays]].

## The Five Recurring Problems

Most weak decision patterns come from one confusion: treating what feels comfortable as what is good. This confusion takes five recurring forms, and each form has its own page.

| The feeling | What is happening |
|---|---|
| Changing course feels costly | the current option is kept only because it is the current one |
| More research feels safer | information is raising confidence faster than accuracy |
| The thinking does not end in a decision | overthinking has become a loop: thought returns again and again to one feeling and makes no progress |
| The result seems to settle it | the outcome is being used after the fact to judge the decision |
| A small choice uses up a lot of focus | the amount of thinking does not fit the decision's tier |

The first form has been measured. People disproportionately keep the option they already have, and the tendency grows with the number of alternatives. The fix, a four-bias check and a seven-step change review, is on [[wiki/Decision Making/Changing Decisions|Changing Decisions]]. The second form comes from how information affects judgment and is not a character flaw. Judges given more information gained confidence substantially faster than they gained accuracy, and they never lowered their confidence to account for information they could not use. As a result, searching feels productive long after it stops improving the decision. When to stop waiting is covered on [[wiki/Decision Making/The Uncertainty-Opportunity Tradeoff|The Uncertainty-Opportunity Tradeoff]]: uncertainty and opportunity decrease together, so certainty costs opportunity. The third form is fixed by giving the thinking a direction, not by trying to think less. The feeling is named in writing and turned into a list of separate problems, each problem gets a set of actions, and anything that cannot be controlled is marked as accepted. The method is on [[wiki/Dimensions/Mindset/Perfectionism and Overthinking|Perfectionism and Overthinking]]. Exhaustive search also does not lead to more satisfaction. Students who searched exhaustively for jobs got jobs paying 20% more, liked those jobs less, and had more negative affect throughout the search. This finding is a reason to stop searching that still holds against the thought "something better might exist." The fourth form is outcome bias, the judging of a decision by its outcome, and step 7 of the loop fixes it. The fifth form is handled by the stakes check and the tiers. Its most costly case is a recurring "what to do next," and that case is never only a planning problem.

## The Loop

A decision that passes the stakes check goes through eight steps.

1. **The decision gets a name**: one sentence saying what is actually being chosen.
2. **The consequence level gets assigned.** The biggest downsides are named. Each is rated for significance, either absolute (unacceptable at any level) or relative (unacceptable only if the opportunity is too small to justify it), and for cappability, with yes, no, or *I don't know* allowed as answers to both. A downside that is significant and cannot be capped is an automatic no, with nothing further to weigh. *I don't know* leads to collecting information, never to guessing.
3. **The variables creating the uncertainty get listed.**
4. **The next move gets picked: decide, gather information, reduce the risk, or wait.** Gathering and waiting are moves with costs and are not defaults, so gathering information comes with a stopping rule that names the missing fact, which way it would change the choice, and what it costs to get. A fact that cannot change the choice is not worth paying for. A plan to do "more research" becomes a timed task or a decision, and the timed version is the two-front sprint on [[wiki/Decision Making/The Uncertainty-Opportunity Tradeoff|The Uncertainty-Opportunity Tradeoff]].
5. **The cap on the biggest downside gets put in place and carried out, not only imagined.** Protection can also change the form of a risk so that the decision does not have to be declined:

```text
A new study method risks a few hours a week for two months. If that loss is too
significant, the trial runs concentrated — a fortnight, or one weekend, with early
feedback — so less time is exposed and the loss lands in an acceptable shape.
A reshaped downside can flip a no into a yes.
```

6. **The choice goes to the option that improves position or carries the better expected value.** This is a working stance and not a finding: no study shows that it works better than estimating outcomes for individuals. It is held because it leaves more usable options available afterwards. One outside comparison is made first: what happened the last three times a plan like this was made? Estimates made from inside a plan tend to be optimistic. Cost forecasts came in about 44% over on rail and 20% over on roads, and demand was overestimated by about half. A person's own history with similar plans is free to check. The stance is on [[wiki/Decision Making/Positional Decisions and Expected Value|Positional Decisions and Expected Value]], and the arithmetic and its bands are on [[wiki/Decision Making/Expectancy in Wicked Environments|Expectancy in Wicked Environments]].
7. **The reason gets written down before the outcome is known, with a probability and a falsifier.** Once people know the outcome, they remember it as having been more likely than they thought at the time, and they change, without noticing, which facts they think were relevant. So the record made before the outcome is the only copy of the original belief that stays unchanged. The probability makes it possible for the record to be wrong. Without a probability, the outcome has nothing to correct, and the log becomes a diary. Giving probabilities also improves with training: one hour of probability practice with feedback improved forecasting accuracy by roughly 12%, 6%, and 7% in years two, three, and four compared with untrained controls, mostly by reducing overconfidence.
8. **The outcome is used as feedback about the process.** [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management/Kolbs Experiential Cycle|Kolbs Experiential Cycle]] is used for this step: act, observe, form a principle, and test it next time. [[wiki/Dimensions/Mindset/Marginal Gains|Marginal Gains]] is used to choose the direction: the next small compounding improvement.

## The Checks Worth Keeping

Decision making overlaps with [[wiki/Red Team/Red Teaming|Red Teaming]] and has a different purpose. Red Teaming remains an identity and a stance. Decision making is the practical method for daily, academic, and career choices. An AI model can now generate the formal artifacts on demand: matrices, dissent prompts, stakeholder lists, alternative hypotheses, premortems, and explanation formats. The skill that is now scarce is deciding whether any of that output matters. Four checks carry over into decision making. They are run as part of the loop, not beside it as separate routines.

The assumptions check asks what has to be true for this choice to work. The dissent check is used in a corrected form. Assigned devil's-advocacy strengthens the position it argues against. Dissent from someone who actually holds the opposing view produces more original thinking, more genuine consideration of the opposite view, and more attitude change. So the objection comes from a person who believes it, or the two strongest reasons against the choice are written down, each with what would follow if it were true. The list has two reasons, not ten, because a longer list makes the alternative feel harder to imagine and has the opposite of the intended effect. The perspective check asks who the choice affects and what they can see that cannot be seen from inside the choice. The evidence for this check is substantial when real people are asked. It is much thinner when the people are only imagined. In a premortem, the plan is assumed to have failed and the story of how it failed is written out. This produced about 30% more correct reasons for a future outcome. It shows more ways the plan could fail and does not improve prediction. In practice it also reduces motivated reasoning about a decision the person already favours.

## Where This Sits

Decision making is filed under two dimensions: [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation|Self-Regulation]], which is noticing in the moment that a strategy has stopped working, and [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management|Self-Management]], which is the systems that work without needing willpower every day. A study system has six recurring decisions: what to study next, whether to keep encoding or switch to retrieval, whether a bad result means the method failed, whether to change the plan, how much time to spend collecting information, and whether a delay is strategic or avoidant. Two of the six are usually not open questions. Whether to keep encoding or switch to retrieval is settled on most days: practice testing and distributed practice are rated high-utility, and rereading and highlighting are rated low. Whether a bad result means the method failed is answered by separating the process from the outcome and naming how much of the outcome was under control. The more of the outcome that was controlled, the more the result says about the process. That scale is covered on [[wiki/Decision Making/Judging a Decision by Its Process|Good Decisions]]. Both cases point to a larger saving than deciding faster, which is deciding once. A recurring choice that is converted into a standing rule no longer comes up, so the whole decision is removed, along with the minutes it took. The cost of deciding is also not a limited reserve that gets used up. Deliberation costs time and makes people lose track of the work they were doing, and a choice made in advance takes no time.

Losing track of the work is the expensive part. An unresolved "what next?" can lead into [[wiki/Self Management/Procrastination - a System Problem|Procrastination: a System Problem]], interrupt a [[wiki/Self Management/Flow State|Flow State]], or reduce attention. Attention stays with the previous task, especially a task left unfinished, and this leftover attention does not go away by itself. Writing down where the work stands and what comes next removes the part of the effect that can be measured. Entering a work block, catching drift, and recovering inside one block are covered on [[wiki/Self Management/Flow State|Focus Management: How to Enter & Recover Inside a Work Block]]. For the transitions between blocks, [[wiki/Self Management/Flow State|Attention Management: Preserving Flow]] covers how to keep the next block from starting slowly. The quality of each of these choices was fixed at the moment it was made. Recurring choices that are converted into standing rules are no longer made at all.
