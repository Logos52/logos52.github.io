# Decision Making

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

A good decision is judged by the position it leaves you in if you keep deciding that way, which is its expected value, rather than by whether the last single outcome went your way. That standard runs through five recurring problems. Too many options freeze a choice, and a chain of yes-or-no questions closes off whole branches until one path is left. Small gaps spent working out what comes next add up to between 30 minutes and 2 hours a day, and they shrink once a daily look-back makes them visible. A first choice made with thin information often deserves revisiting when new facts arrive, even though sunk cost, habit and the fear of loss push you to stay. Overthinking is repaired by more targeted thinking, which turns a feeling into a problem list and then into one action you can take now. After each decision, a structured look back turns what happened into the next small improvement.

## What Counts as a Good Decision

The test for a choice is the position it leaves you in. Chess players think this way early in a game, placing pieces so that later attacks become possible before any capture is visible. Card games show the same idea through chance, because two strong starting cards can still lose a hand while the stronger start stays statistically better. Each decision carries a plus or a minus, and the sign describes what happens if you keep making that kind of choice.

A simple game makes the arithmetic plain. You pay 5 dollars to roll a six-sided die and win 10 dollars if it lands on the 6-pip face. Across 6 games you would spend 30 dollars and expect to take back 10 dollars, for a loss of 20 dollars. Early wins can make the game feel good, but the results move toward that long-run value as the plays add up.

The same arithmetic separates a weak process from a strong one over years. Across a week or a month there is not enough data, so people judge a choice by its last outcome. Across years, better decisions improve the position through checking risks, gathering information, testing openings and reducing what can go badly. Feelings belong in that process, since how you feel is part of what you value. A process with feelings as its only input has a negative expected value, so the more decisions you make that way, the more likely you are to end up worse off.

## How Much Thinking a Decision Is Worth

The amount of thinking should fit the size of the decision. A chain of single yes-or-no questions is most useful for daily and mid-sized choices, where each answer drops whole branches and leaves one path open. The most important and complicated calls need a more careful framework on top of that chain.

A simpler question is not always a faster one, though. Answering a single yes or no about which values matter most can take months, because running the chain depends on how clearly you see your own values and how well you know the options. Those are skills you can train.

## The Five Recurring Problems

Each recurring problem shows up first as a feeling, and the feeling hides what is actually going on.

| The feeling | What is happening |
|---|---|
| Changing course feels costly | sunk cost, status quo bias and loss aversion pull you to stay, and a gain often has to look 3 to 10 times larger than the loss before change feels worth it |
| Waiting for more certainty feels safer | the opening that made the option worth considering tends to close while more is learned |
| The thinking does not end in a decision | overthinking is running without targets, and the repair is to name the feeling, list its root problems and break them into tasks |
| The result seems to settle it | a single outcome is standing in for the long-run value of the choice |
| Too many options freeze the choice | choice overload makes choosing nothing the easy move |

New information can make changing course the stronger bet. In the three-door puzzle, a host who knows where the prize is opens a losing door, and the remaining closed door then carries about a 66 percent chance against the first pick's 33 percent. The new information piled the leftover chance onto the option that survived, which is why a career chosen in the teenage years can deserve a change once you know what the work involves. [[wiki/Decision Making/Changing Decisions|Changing Decisions]] separates that kind of change from simple quitting, since persistence fits a choice that still matches your goals and values.

Overthinking takes a different repair. [[wiki/Dimensions/Mindset/Perfectionism and Overthinking|Perfectionism and Overthinking]] treats perfectionism as a symptom of anxiety and insecurity, and its four steps end in one small action you can take now. The smaller and more specific the pieces, the more likely there is something you can actually do.

The between-task question of what to do next gets its own fix. [[wiki/Decision Making/Decisional Delays|Decisional Delays]] asks for a short log at the end of each day of every point where you stopped to wonder. Once you can see a delay starting, you go straight into the next task.

## The Loop

Every decision ends in feedback, and the loop is how that feedback changes the next attempt. [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management/Kolbs Experiential Cycle|Kolbs Experiential Cycle]] is a structured way of looking back at what happened, where each pass should teach more about what the problem actually is and end in a next step you can try. Turning a problem over and over without changing anything is rumination, which the cycle is built to avoid.

The look back needs a direction, since many problems can compete at once, and a beginner can face dozens of possible directions. [[wiki/Dimensions/Mindset/Marginal Gains|Marginal Gains]] supplies it by treating progress as a series of small improvements, so the next step is easy to hit and aimed at the goal that matters. In the picture used for the pair, marginal gains are the road and the cycle is the vehicle that travels it.

## The Checks Worth Keeping

A plan can also be tested before it runs. [[wiki/Red Team/Red Teaming|Red Teaming]] is a structured way to find what is wrong with a plan before reality does. It looks as hard at the people making the plan as at the plan itself.

## Where This Sits

Decision making draws on two dimensions of learning. [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Regulation|Self-Regulation]] is watching, steering and refining how you work while it happens. [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management|Self-Management]] covers the habits and surroundings that make steady action possible.

An unanswered question about what to do next also spills into other work. Each switch it forces costs part of the focus that builds into a [[wiki/Self Management/Flow State|Flow State]], and a stalled start is the subject of [[wiki/Self Management/Procrastination - a System Problem|Procrastination - a System Problem]].

## Open questions

- If I made this kind of choice for 20 more rounds, would I expect my position to be stronger or weaker?
- How does a small yes-or-no filter change the way a large decision feels?
