# Judging a Decision by Its Process

Judging a decision by its process means grading how a choice was made, using what was known at the time, instead of grading it by how things turned out. Every result depends partly on luck and on people outside your control. A careful choice can end badly, and a careless one can end well, so the result alone gives a wrong grade and teaches the wrong lesson.

- A decision splits into how it was made and what happened.
- You control only how it was made.
- A good process can still produce a bad result.
- Grading by the result is called resulting.
- Hindsight makes the result look more predictable than it was.
- One bad result is too little to judge the process by.
- A sound process pays off over many decisions.

## How it works

Split any decision into the process and the result. The process covers the information gathered, the options weighed and the reasoning used, including the parts done by instinct. The result is shaped by the process and also by chance, by other people and by events nobody saw coming. Grading the whole decision by the result mixes the two and teaches false lessons.

```
             good result      bad result
good process  deserved         bad luck
bad process   good luck        deserved
```

- A lucky win after a sloppy process teaches the sloppy process.
- An unlucky loss after a careful process teaches you to abandon it.
- Both errors come from reading the result as the grade.

## How much the result tells you

The result says more when you controlled more of what produced it. An exam is close to fully in your hands with the right preparation, so a bad mark says a lot about the method. A move to a new city depends on many things you cannot control, so a bad year there says much less. Weigh the result by how much of it was yours.

- High control, as with exams: the result is strong evidence.
- Low control, as with most life choices: the result is weak evidence.
- Results often rise and fall before they trend upward.

## How to review a decision

Write down the reasons for a choice before the result is known. After the result arrives, memory adjusts what seemed knowable, and the written reasons are the only record of what you knew at the time. Review the reasons against the result, then decide whether the process needs to change. Do not drop a sound process after a single bad result.

- Before: write what you know, what you expect and why.
- After: compare the result with what you wrote.
- Ask what was knowable then and what is only known now.
- Change the process only if the review finds a fault in it.
