# Expectancy in Wicked Environments

Expectancy is the average result of a choice if you could make it many times: the chance of a good result times its size, minus the chance of a bad result times its size. In a game with fixed rules the sum can be done exactly. Most life decisions have no fixed rules and too many factors, a setting called wicked, so the sum needs three simplifications before it is usable.

## Core takeaways

- Expectancy is chance times size, good results minus bad ones.
- Games like chess have clear rules, so the sum is exact.
- Most life choices have unclear rules and no clear win.
- Drop the small factors, since they roughly cancel out.
- Weigh only the few factors that matter most.
- Grade chance and size in rough bands instead of exact percentages.
- Shrinking the biggest downside can turn a bad bet into a good one.

## How the sum works

Take a game you play 100 times. Each time you have a 75% chance to win $10 and a 25% chance to lose $50. Over 100 games you win about $750 and lose about $1,250, so you end $500 down. Cut the loss to $20 and the same game ends $250 up.

```
75 wins   x $10 = +$750
25 losses x $50 = -$1,250    total: -$500
25 losses x $20 =   -$500    total: +$250
```

- A high chance of winning can still lose money overall.
- The size of the loss matters as much as its chance.

## Kind and wicked settings

A setting with clear rules for winning and losing and fast, accurate feedback is called kind, and chess and golf are examples. A setting with no published odds, no defined win and feedback that is late, missing or misleading is called wicked. Choosing a career, a partner or a city happens in a wicked setting. There the exact sum cannot be done, and waiting for it means never deciding.

- Kind: rules clear, feedback fast and accurate.
- Wicked: win undefined, feedback late or misleading.
- Accept that the perfect choice cannot be computed here.

## The three simplifications

The sum becomes usable once most of the factors are removed and the rest are graded roughly. Good information has to come first, so that a major downside is not dropped by accident. Rough bands work better than false precision, because nobody knows whether a chance is 78% or 79%. Running a choice through these steps with imperfect numbers still gives better choices than skipping them.

- Step 1, cut the small factors.
  - Small gains and small losses roughly cancel.
  - Anything left over can be handled when it comes up.
  - Even a certain result can be ignored if it is minor.
- Step 2, keep only the biggest gains and the biggest losses.
- Step 3, grade each in bands.
  - Size: imperative, significant, convenient, insignificant.
  - Chance: guaranteed, very high, somewhat, low.

## Changing the balance

Once the few big factors are on paper, look for ways to move them. A downside can be made less likely or smaller, and either change can turn the total from negative to positive. In the game above, cutting the chance of a loss from 25% to 5% has the same kind of effect as cutting its size from $50 to $20.

- Ask how to make the worst outcome less likely.
- Ask how to make it smaller if it happens.
- Check the upside too: how likely, and how large.
