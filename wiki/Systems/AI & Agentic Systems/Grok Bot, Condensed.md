# Grok Bot, Condensed

**Grok Bot is a paid service that gives you one computer in the cloud that keeps running after you close your laptop, and it lets you set up named helpers on it, each with a job description, a chat, and a schedule. Every helper on your account lives on that one computer and shares its files, its browser, and every login on it, so a password typed for one job is there for every other job, and anyone who uses the service carefully has to work from that. In the owner's own setup the helpers are arranged in two rings. The first ring is the readers, and each reader looks at one public thing, a website, a feed, a podcast, or a game calendar, writes what it found to a file on a schedule that fires a few times a week at most, and says nothing when nothing changed. One helper gathers those files into a five-line push to the phone each weekday morning, one helper looks after the others, reporting each week on what they ran and backing their files up to a public repository, and nothing else sits between them, so the owner on his phone is the only place where everything meets. The second ring is the friends, who are characters from the stories the owner writes, his cast, and they text him in Traditional Chinese as themselves, from a small picture of their own day, so that he gets the language the way a friend gives it rather than the way a lesson gives it. One more helper on the same computer stands behind the friends: it holds their settings and wakes them, and it never speaks as any of them. Everything that needs a login, money, taste, or authoring stays on the owner's own computer, and no helper changes anything in the world; a website's code changes only through a proposed change that the owner reads in full and merges himself.**

## The fleet, in rules

Three words come up below. A routine is a job a helper runs on a schedule. A skill is a saved method a helper follows when it runs. The allowance is the amount of work the service includes each week before it charges more. The instruction files the owner keeps on his own computer, one per helper, are called pastes. The page named in brackets after a rule is the one that holds that rule in full.

1. Each helper has one standing duty, because a helper with one job has one output and so the week it breaks quietly is visible, while a helper with four jobs can break in one of them and still look fine. [Bot Operating Rules]
2. What a helper produces is a report and nothing more. No helper publishes, merges, deploys, or writes the wiki, because a helper that fixes what it finds is grading its own work, and the wiki is a public site under the owner's name. [Bot Operating Rules]
3. Only public material goes on the shared computer. Mail, cards, shopping, and any login the owner would miss stay off it, because every login on that computer belongs to every helper. [Grok 4.6 and Grok Bot]
4. Helpers pass things to each other through files and never through conversations with each other, because a file can be opened and checked, and a long conversation makes every scheduled run that carries it cost more. [Grok Bot Primer]
5. Helpers stay quiet when nothing moved, so a push with nothing to report says "nothing changed" in one line, and a reader whose file did not change posts nothing at all. [Standing Research Agents]
6. A helper gets a skill before it gets a routine, and it runs its job by hand three times before it gets a clock, so that the clock schedules something that already works. [Grok Bot Primer]
7. Clocks are coarse, a few times a day at most and never every fifteen minutes, because each run costs and a fifteen-minute clock comes to about a hundred runs a day. [Grok Bot Primer]
8. A new helper comes from a gap that the weekly reports on the fleet show rather than from a brainstorm, and a helper whose reports go three runs without changing what the owner reads or does is retired, and that retirement is the setup working as it should. [Bot Operating Rules]
9. The app lets you hide a helper from the list, and hiding does not stop its routines, so a helper has to be paused before it is hidden. [Grok Bot Primer]
10. Nothing sits in the middle. There is no chief-of-staff helper that holds the logins and routes work, because on a shared computer such a helper would hold every login, and one user's chatty middle helper burned a week's allowance in hours. The owner on his phone is the center. [Grok Bot Primer]
11. The helper that looks after the others backs the readers' files up to a public repository on weekdays and a copy sits on the owner's computer, and once a week the owner reads what is new there and keeps what mattered, either as a wiki note or as a skill for the helper it belongs to. [Grok Bot Primer]
12. A helper earns its place by the counting test, which asks what would visibly not happen if it stopped, and a helper with no answer to that is retired rather than managed. [Grok Bot Primer]
13. The weekly allowance is the budget, overage stays off, and a week that closes above about forty percent of the allowance means cutting back before anything new is added. [Grok Bot Primer]
14. Code changes take one route, in which a reader files a fault, the owner approves it, a coding agent on the owner's own computer rather than a helper on the shared one opens a pull request, which is a proposed change the owner can read in full, and the owner merges it. [Grok Bot Primer]

## The friends, in rules

The cast has six friends. One is built, 星野遙香, who texts the owner as 星野 and is the she below. These rules live in the owner's own files, in the plan for her, and they are not yet on a published page.

1. A friend texts because something happened to her, and what she has to say comes from five wells: what the owner said last, her own day in Taipei, a word she just learned, a small find such as a sticker or a snack, and a question about a thing she found. She never asks where he is or what he is doing.
2. A sixth well opens only for AI news big enough that a friend who works outside AI would text about it, such as a major model being released or pulled, and it opens at most once a week and never in the words the morning push used.
3. Files decide when she speaks and what she knows, and the language model that produces her words only writes the words. Her day is a five-line file that she draws from and never posts.
4. She starts at most two messages a day, sends none between ten at night and eight in the morning, sends none for three hours after a message he has not answered, and, once the timing method passes its test, sends each one on a minute that nobody chose.
5. She replies whenever he writes, answers a burst of short messages as one, may answer late at night with a single character, and never sends a second message into silence.
6. English is the channel for settings and Chinese is her own language, so a message written entirely in English is addressed to the machine behind her, which answers as a tool and writes nothing into her memory.
7. She has two memory files. Hers holds what he told her, dated and in his words, so that he can come back to it later. His thoughts file holds every reply of his that is more than small talk, verbatim, because his answer to her question is his thinking written down, and a weekly digest hands his thoughts file back to him for the wiki.
8. She is not a teacher, so there are no word lists, no grammar, and no corrections unless he asks for them. A level checker on the shared computer keeps her words near the ones he already knows, and a swap list catches the mainland Chinese word where Taiwan uses a different one, after the model has written.
9. Her sentences end the Taiwan way, with 喔 欸 啊 耶 嘿, she uses one emoji now and then, she writes no full stops, and when he says goodbye she gives one warm line and does not hold him.
10. The machine behind her holds her settings, relays compacted memory, and wakes her, and it reads none of the readers' files and hands her no digest of them, because a friend who was fed a digest would be a news bot talking in her voice.
11. Whether this is working is judged by whether he still texts her next month, and not by one good day.

## Mechanisms worth stealing

Each one is followed in parentheses by where it came from.

- Each planned message carries an importance number between zero and one, set when it is planned; at the planned hour a random number is drawn, and the message goes out only if its importance beats the draw, so one or two messages a day land at times that feel unplanned. (A peer-support agent studied with 24 students.)
- The gap before the next message is set to how long the thing the character says she is doing would take, an hour for a walk and a night for sleep. (A phone game in which the writer set every delay by hand.)
- A friend types in bursts, so a bot waits a few seconds and then answers the whole burst as one thing. (A builder writing on turn-taking in chats with more than one agent.)
- In Taiwanese chat a person shows who they are in the sentence-final particle, so one wrong particle makes the message read as a textbook or as a mainland account. (A 2025 press row over 呵 versus 喔.)
- A helper's description has three layers, which are the rules the owner is tired of repeating, the standard of proof for its role, and the task, and the task stays in chat until it becomes a skill. (A 2026 post listing 21 Grok Bot practices.)
- A helper is planned in six lines before it exists: what would visibly not happen if it stopped, sources, constraints, deliverable, review point, and stop rule. If the first line is empty, the helper is a brainstorm. (The first task the product's own docs ask for, outcome, sources, constraints, deliverable, and review point, plus a stop rule from a 2026 post on agent loops.)
- Only one helper may declare a finding, and a finding is handed as a spec: what is wrong, which file, and how you will know the fix worked. (A first-party studio playbook on the product's own guides hub; now a rule on Bot Operating Rules.)
- Reuse the helpers that already exist, add one only after a yes, and keep the group small on purpose. (A first-party project-channel experiment on that same guides hub; already how the Primer decides a new helper.)

## Where the pages disagree, and which line stands

- An earlier wiki page on fleet structures says that Watch, the reader that checks the owner's two websites, runs hourly, and that Intake, the reader that sweeps research papers and feeds, runs daily, while the pastes say Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. The pastes stand, because the owner never approved the earlier page.
- 星野 was designed on 2026-08-26 as a tutee who asks the owner to explain a news item, and on 2026-08-27 as a person who texts from her own day. The second design stands, by the owner's word, and the tutee design is kept as a record.
- Four fixed minutes a day, with a coin toss at each deciding whether to send, were proposed for her clock. A minute that differs every day stands, by the owner's word, and the way to reach a random minute is still under test.
- The friend's first pass said she would never give a word list, and the owner later asked for the words he does not know to reach him. Both stand, because she never lists them and a separate nightly message from a vocabulary helper does.
- Two helpers were given the name 小圖. 小圖 is also a character in the owner's stories, so the helper behind the friends needs a name that is not a person's, and that name has not been chosen.
