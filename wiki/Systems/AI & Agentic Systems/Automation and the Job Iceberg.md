# Automation and the Job Iceberg

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Automation almost never wipes out a whole kind of job. Over the last 60 years of U.S. census job titles, only the elevator operator disappeared because of it. Bank tellers, pilots, writers and accountants were all predicted to vanish, and each group grew instead. The machines took over tasks, and the roles took on more work and more responsibility. The better question for any role is whether it can absorb what cheaper tasks make possible. Beneath the visible economy sits a large iceberg of things people would want but nobody makes, because making them costs too much. Cheaper tools bring some of that demand to the surface as new work. The record looks backward, though. It says nothing about any one person's job, and the safest reading is that people who use the new tools will displace those who refuse them.

## Core takeaways

- A job is the work of turning an input into an output, and the tasks along the way are not the job itself.
- Automation of tasks has usually expanded the role, as with tellers and pilots.
- The elevator operator vanished because the role had nothing new to take on.
- People mostly work to buy time and the freedom to say no, not because they love the tasks.
- Cheaper tools open up demand that never existed as work before.
- Most current job titles are recent, so new work keeps appearing.
- A title surviving does not protect an individual who refuses the new tools.

## The four predicted extinctions

| Predicted extinction | What happened |
|---|---|
| ATMs would end bank tellers | Teller numbers rose as ATMs spread, roughly doubling over about 50 years to a peak in 2007. |
| Spreadsheets would end accountants | The profession expanded instead. |
| Autopilot would end pilots | Pilot numbers grew with more planes, bigger planes and more flights. |
| Word processors would shrink newsrooms | There are many times more writers today. |

A newer prediction about radiology also failed. Radiologists were once expected to disappear within five years. AI now reads scans, yet the demand for radiologists is higher than ever. Recent forecasts that most code would be written by AI within a year, or that half of entry-level jobs would vanish, have also not come true.

Every job turns some input into an output. A nurse turns a sick patient into a healthy one. A writer turns information into an article, and a programmer turns a slow process into a faster one. Between input and output lies a long series of steps, such as clicking, copying, sorting and waiting on hold.

When people watch an AI tool do one of those steps, they often fear for the whole job. Yet the step is only a task, and many tasks are ones nobody likes. Only 4 percent of new rideshare drivers are still driving after a year. Amazon must hire about 150 new warehouse workers each year to keep 100 positions filled. Every coding tool, from compilers to autocomplete, has existed to let programmers write less code.

## Bank tellers took on more

When the first cash machine appeared at a London bank, the crowd assumed tellers were finished. Instead, teller numbers climbed alongside the machines. Freed from handing out cash, tellers expanded what they did at the counter. The job changed shape rather than disappearing.

## Pilots stayed in the seat

Autopilot has existed since the 1920s, and modern planes can land themselves in zero visibility. Pilots now spend much of a flight monitoring screens. Even so, air travel grew from a luxury into something hundreds of millions of people use each year, and passengers still want a trained human up front. When a bird strike, a medical emergency or a violent passenger arrives, a person makes the call and carries the responsibility. Automation took most of the flying and multiplied the number of flights.

## Elevator operators had nothing to absorb

For almost a century, every passenger elevator had a person working the lever. The title last appeared in the 1960 census. Part of the reason was cost, since an attendant in every small hotel lift was expensive and could not be expanded. Another part was complexity. Modern skyscrapers needed software to decide which car stops at which floor with many riders aboard, a task a person in the car could not manage. Nothing new could be added to a job confined to a box moving up and down.

## Why people hold a job

Surveys about lottery wins show how people feel about work. Asked about a $10 million win, more than half say they would at least leave their current job. Fewer than a quarter say they would stay in work no matter what they were paid.

Money is best understood as optionality, the freedom to choose what to do with your time and to say no. People buy a house so they do not have to build one and buy food so they do not have to grow it. Seen this way, people work to gain time, not the other way round. On the hierarchy of needs, security sits just over food and shelter, and a job is how most people meet it.

## Demand that never became work

Some things people would want never get made, because nobody can justify the cost. A small stacking pyramid in five colors is one example. Making even one in plastic would need custom molds costing about 17,000 dollars in total. To break even on a first run of 1,000 pyramids, each would have to sell for about 30 dollars apiece. So it was never produced, no one purchased it, and official market figures show a blank.

Home 3D printing hit the same wall. The printers were cheap, but few people could design objects in 3D software, so the predicted printer in every home never arrived. A design file is just code, though. With an AI coding tool, the pyramid took about 30 minutes from idea to printer, and no designer lost work. The term for this is competing against non-consumption. The rival is not another product but people choosing to do nothing because no option fits.

## Job titles that did not exist 50 years ago

About 60 percent of the jobs people hold today did not exist 50 years ago. Examples include wind turbine technician, cybersecurity analyst, conference planner and mental health counselor. Researchers at MIT reviewed eight decades of census records and marked each new job title as it appeared. They then sorted inventions into those that make existing jobs more valuable and those that simply replace workers. New jobs cluster where the first kind of invention flows in. Film editing is one case, since digital tools made editing far more powerful and fed a global streaming industry.

## What the record cannot tell you

The argument from history only looks backward. The presenter says he leans toward the view that AI will augment work, warns that he is biased, and asks viewers to check his reasoning. He also notes that in his own testing, far more is impossible for AI than possible. Past waves automated narrow sets of tasks, and nothing in the record proves the current one will behave the same way.

## A surviving title does not protect a person

A job title surviving says nothing about whether a given person keeps their place. The likeliest outcome is that AI will not take your job, but someone using AI will. That may soon be like applying for an office job while refusing to use a computer.

Small gains add up. Take two people doing the same job, one gaining 10 percent more output each week from AI. Over a year that gap adds up, and if the gains compound it grows much faster. The gains also come from the dull parts, like finding a figure in a long document, not from the hard creative work, which stays human.

Two questions help judge a role. The operator test asks whether the role has anything new to take on once its tasks get cheaper. The pilot test asks whether people still want a responsible human present when things go wrong. A role that passes both looks more like the pilot than the operator.

## How to practice this

- Write your job as an input and an output, then list the tasks in between.
- Mark which tasks you would gladly hand off and which carry real judgment or responsibility.
- Name one thing your role could take on if half those tasks became free.
- Think of one thing you or your customers want that nobody makes because it costs too much, and ask whether cheaper tools change that.
- Pick one dull task this week and try an AI tool on it.

## Open questions

- How do I tell whether my role can absorb more work or is closer to the elevator operator?
- How would I measure whether AI is actually raising my weekly output?
- How do I find the unmade demand in my own field?
- How would I recognize early that this wave is different from past ones?
