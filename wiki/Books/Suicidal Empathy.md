# Suicidal Empathy (Book)

Gad Saad's *Suicidal Empathy* (2026) follows directly from *The Parasitic Mind*. The earlier book described cognitive pathogens, ideas that harm the mind that holds them. This book describes affective dysregulation, feeling that is out of proportion and unchecked, and how that feeling makes those ideas seem morally required.

Empathy is a real and valuable human capacity. The book calls it suicidal when it is separated from truth, reciprocity, proportion, and long-term survival. When people and institutions put the emotional reward of visible compassion ahead of checking their beliefs against reality, they sacrifice citizens, women, children, merit, borders, and the continuity of their civilization. They do this to protect favored narratives and to signal their own moral virtue.

The book describes the specific ways this happens in crime, immigration, science, institutions, and the state. It offers two correctives. The first is calibrated empathy: help that actually helps and does not destroy the helper or the system. The second is deliberate inoculation: a set of practical defenses, given in the final chapter, that the reader applies before empathy is allowed to drive a decision.

## Core takeaways

- Calibrated empathy serves the person helped and protects the helper's long-term interests. Suicidal empathy serves the helper's self-image and harms both the person helped and the wider system.
- Truth is the first thing given up. Once certain facts become emotionally unacceptable, asking about them is treated as a disorder.
- Cultural theory of mind, an accurate picture of another culture's actual beliefs and incentives, is required before empathy toward that culture can work. Projecting one's own assumptions onto that culture is what happens without it.
- Blank-slate explanations, which treat crime, failure, and group differences as products of social forces, are used to excuse people from accountability. They also prevent effective policy.
- Diversity, equity, and inclusion often work as modern indulgences: rituals that buy moral status at the cost of competence and fairness.
- The remedy is to delay the empathy response on purpose, map the consequences explicitly, and put survival, merit, and truth ahead of everything else.

## Calibrated empathy and suicidal empathy

Chapter 1, "A Good Virtue Gone Bad", sets out the two kinds. Empathy evolved to support bonding, care, and cooperation. It can be cognitive, meaning understanding another person's perspective, or affective, meaning feeling what another person feels. Like all evolved adaptations, it can become unregulated.

The book says suicidal empathy is present when:

- The person or group helped is chosen because helping them feels good or raises the helper's status. Whether the help works, and whether they deserve it, does not decide the choice.
- Truth and consequences are put second to the helper's praise of themselves.
- The response is automatic, excessive, or protected from scrutiny for political reasons.

The chapter contrasts this with calibrated empathy, help that actually helps and does not destroy the helper or the system. Suicidal empathy feels good in the moment and does long-term damage.

## Empathy used to stop questions

Chapter 2, "Forbidden Knowledge", covers inquiry. Empathy becomes the reason given for suppressing it. When institutions judge that certain findings, statistics, or questions would harm a protected group, they treat the search for truth itself as cruelty. The pattern is the same each time: visible or possible emotional harm to a favored group is used to set aside the normal rules of evidence, open inquiry, and the testing of claims that could prove them false. The long-term result is policy built on falsehoods that are kept in place on purpose.

## Knowing how other cultures actually work

Chapter 3, "Cultural Theory of Mind", covers a missing skill. Cultural theory of mind means holding an accurate picture of how another culture actually works: its real beliefs and its real incentives. Western societies lack this skill. They assume other populations share their own individualism, secularism, gender egalitarianism, and free speech. Those populations run on different sacred values and different incentive structures. Without cultural theory of mind, empathy is directed at people the helper does not understand. A society cannot protect itself if it refuses to learn the actual beliefs and behavior of the people it imports or defends.

## Offenders, victims, and defenders

Chapter 4, "Blank Slate Felons", covers criminal justice. People acting from suicidal empathy treat offenders mainly as products of social forces. Victims and public safety get less attention. Protective masculinity, and the willingness to use violence in defense of oneself or others, are treated as disorders. Predatory behavior is explained by its circumstances. The same reversal appears throughout: the defender is treated as suspect, and the predator is treated as a victim of circumstances.

## Trade-offs that may not be discussed

Chapter 5, "Settled Science, Taboo Trade-Offs", covers policy debate. When a policy has a beneficiary the culture favors on moral grounds, disagreement is recast as harm, and the complex trade-offs are declared off-limits. The phrase "settled science" is used to end discussion, where it should mean a provisional summary of the evidence so far. Refusing to discuss a trade-off does not remove its costs. It only stops an honest count of those costs and the better decisions that would follow.

## Buying moral status

Chapter 6, "Selling Indulgences", covers institutions. Diversity, equity, and inclusion mandates, mandatory trainings, diversity statements, and corporate virtue signaling are, in the book's account, a modern indulgence system: institutions and people buy moral status by performing empathy toward approved groups. The price is often competence, truth, and their actual mission.

Woke capitalism, in the book's account, adds a second status game, progressive moral display, on top of the normal market game. When the two conflict, people often follow the moral game in the short term. The long-term cost is that universities, corporations, medicine, and science move away from their actual missions.

## Empathy at the level of the state

Chapter 7, "Govern Me Harder, Daddy!", covers the state. Welfare expansion, open-ended foreign aid with nothing required in return, and putting diversity and therapeutic priorities above military effectiveness are each described in the book as empathy at large scale. Each weakens the system it claims to help.

A large bureaucracy and heavy taxation become empathy built into the state, paid for with other people's money and other people's freedom of action. The book presents "wokeness" in the military as particularly dangerous, because when a military can no longer deter attack, attacks follow.

## The five defenses

Chapter 8, "Inoculation Against Suicidal Empathy", gives five practical defenses.

- Delay the good feeling that comes with acting on empathy. Feel the impulse, then force a pause and map the second- and third-order consequences.
- Name the target and the cost. Say who benefits, who pays, and what is being given up.
- Insist on reciprocity and realism. Empathy that ignores the recipient's behavior or the receiving culture's values becomes exploitation.
- Treat truth and merit as things that cannot be traded away. Once they are put below emotional comfort, the system enters a decline that grows at each step until it fails.
- Keep cultural self-respect. A civilization that treats its own survival, achievements, and boundaries as morally suspect has already lost the will to defend itself.

## How to practice this

1. Pick a decision where the emotional pressure to help is strong. Before acting, pause and write down the second- and third-order consequences. Notice which consequences you had not seen while the impulse was at its strongest.
2. Write down who benefits, who pays, and what is given up. Notice whether the payer was in your mind before you wrote the list.
3. Check whether your help takes account of the recipient's behavior and the receiving culture's values. If it takes account of neither, the book says it has become exploitation. Notice whether you have asked the recipient for anything in return.
4. Check whether the decision puts emotional comfort above truth or merit. Notice which true fact would have to be set aside to keep everyone comfortable.
5. Check whether the decision treats your own civilization's survival, achievements, or boundaries as morally suspect. Notice whether you can say plainly that those things are worth defending.
