# Exam Technique

Exam technique is what remains after the material is learned: performing on the day, diagnosing patterns in missed marks, and preparing when the syllabus is too large to treat like a short course. Sleep the night before is not optional. What you studied stays unusable until deeper sleep carries it into longer-term memory. Cramming and all-nighters belong earlier. Sleep loss can cost as much as twenty IQ points, which hurts both recall and hard problem-solving.

Inside the paper, the working method is priority checking: keep a steady pace, handle each item while you are relatively sure, mark the shaky items, and return only to those. Checking every answer the same way wastes time and raises the chance of flipping a right answer to a wrong one. If the first pass felt certain, changing that answer usually makes it wrong.

## Sleep, calm, and pacing

The night before belongs to sleep, not to new encoding. Calm is the next thing to protect, because anxiety produces tunnel vision that makes an obvious answer vanish. Long, slow, deep breaths help, especially a very slow exhale of about ten seconds, which triggers the rest-and-digest response and turns down fight-or-flight. Force the breath to settle until you calm, then come back at full focus.

Nerves that appear only in high-pressure sittings respond to practice under increasingly exam-like pressure beforehand: strict time limits, real conditions, and others present so the session feels competitive. Write a pre-exam checklist so stress does not erase these moves, and decide when you will begin working through it, possibly weeks ahead. Last-minute studying at the exam-room door does little to help.

Do not stay inside a hard question past its time budget. If you are aiming for about one minute per question and cannot crack one in that window, put down your best attempt, star the item, and go on. Lingering usually makes the fog thicker. Coming back later often opens the question up, and a later item can make an earlier one clearer.

## Late-stage study before the sitting

Another way to miss marks is to practise only the question shapes you already handle well. Take what you know and ask how you would set a hard paper if you wanted to throw curveballs. That surfaces question types you would otherwise skip. Writing forty to fifty questions on a subject and swapping them with a study group trains that examiner view. It can beat leaning only on past papers.

Four methods carry much of this final-stage preparation once encoding and retrieval are in place:

- Breaching Questions finds gaps by writing the hardest question you can on each weak spot, one you cannot answer yet.
- The ReCOVer System plans a long answer in four steps (Recall, Chunk, Order, and Verbalise) before any sentences get written.
- Silly Mistake Syndrome treats an error labeled silly as a gap in understanding or application, and sets a five-step attention check under pressure.
- Group Study assigns explicit roles (leader, timekeeper, and fact-checker).

## Diagnosing and fixing exam mistakes

A mistake you call silly is hard to fix, because the label hides its cause. Most of these errors come from weak preparation more than from poor technique in the room. Even an obvious slip usually marks a place where understanding or application is thin. The work starts with diagnosis: read how your errors cluster to name the biggest likely cause, try one fix aimed at that cause, reflect on what else might be behind the rest, and repeat.

Five causes are common:

1. **Test anxiety.** Errors look random, do not line up with difficulty or type, and grow as stakes rise. Practise under rising pressure, with timed papers, shorter time limits, and others in the room. Slow breathing and a calming pre-test routine help. Cramming at the door mostly raises anxiety.
2. **Missing lower-order (factual recall) knowledge.** Errors cluster on recalling specific, separate facts. Add a recall drill, such as flashcards.
3. **Missing higher-order (combining concepts) knowledge.** Errors cluster on questions that join two or more concepts, or on choosing a strategy. Add work that combines concepts in both encoding and retrieval, with mixed topics and forms.
4. **Too little procedural practice.** Errors cluster on step-by-step tasks common in maths, engineering, and software. Add practice where you carry out the procedure yourself in retrieval sessions.
5. **Gaps your retrieval never surfaces.** The same errors come back in practice tests across several kinds. Use the double generation practice test described below.

"Silly" often means missing logic, not weak attention. When a student lacks part of the understanding, they skip information without noticing, and the answer key still makes sense when they read it. That feeling of sense is the trap. Unit errors in maths and physics often stop once the student understands what each unit stands for, because a wrong conversion now clashes with understanding as well as with what they memorised. Effort alone does not fix this. Ignoring the mistake and hoping it will not return fails, as does a vague promise to be more careful.

### Double generation practice test

Practice papers often show the first signs of an error, but the signs are easy to miss if you check the answers too soon or leave a question half answered.

1. Sit a practice test and write out every answer in full, as in the real exam.
2. Mark each place where you were unsure as you go.
3. Build your own answer sheet of ideal model answers, drawing on your notes and other resources.
4. Check your exam answers against the sheet you built.
5. Check your sheet against the official one, if there is one.

You finish with three lists: questions you were unsure about, questions your own sheet shows you got wrong, and model answers the official sheet shows were incomplete. Every item is a likely gap. An answer you got right without confidence stays on the list.

Writing the answers matters. Answering in your head is not enough to expose the illusion of explanatory depth. Building your own answer sheet is the slow step and the most valuable one. Making your own is better than using only the official sheet because it forces lower-order and higher-order thinking at once. As a rough estimate, doing it is worth about as much as five or six practice tests done without it.

## Ultra high-volume exams

An ultra high-volume exam asks you to cover a huge body of fixed material and, often, a second stream of dynamic material that keeps updating. Only about one to two percent of that body will actually be tested, and you do not know which sliver. Past papers show trends but do not make a complete map.

A single-semester exam can shrink study time a great deal once your method improves. An ultra high-volume exam can shrink time only a little. Efficiency shows up as more coverage, more gap-finding, or more depth on the same hours, not as an empty calendar.

Conventional repetition fails the arithmetic. Research-typical retention sits around fifty percent at one week. On a short syllabus you can often cover the forgotten half again. On an ultra high-volume exam, forgetting fifty percent means you never finish the set. There comes a point where repetition consumes all study time and you still cannot relearn everything. Something learned on day one may not be tested until three hundred days later, or after years of preparation. A method that cannot produce enough retention, for long enough, at enough depth, puts you further behind the longer you keep it.

**Hit rate** is the chance that what you are learning will be useful on the paper. One part is whether the topic is tested at all. Another is how wide you make the box around a detail. Learning one-to-one protects you against one shape of question if attention and depth hold. Learning a little wider takes longer and can protect nearby question shapes. Because width costs time, you choose.

Two checks help. **Conceptual relevance** asks whether the detail extends something you already know is important. **Repeated testing trends** ask whether the detail keeps appearing in past papers or whether self-testing shows you need it more often than you expected. Isolated facts go on flashcards. Wider facts get used in short answers, added into an existing map as concrete examples, and worked into the schema.

Encoding skill moves slowly. If you aim at a gold-standard map on every item while skill is still low, coverage stalls. The practical move is to push just past your current level. Even raising retention from forty to fifty percent cuts repetition cost every week, and across years that can save dozens of hours a month.

Self-testing needs a set order. Start with high-volume methods that test broad recall, such as a brain dump or teaching the entire syllabus from memory. Those methods expose broad gaps before they spread. Run a broad retrieval within the first week, and every week, so the pile stays manageable. Later, move toward targeted questions, quizzes, focused explanations, and flashcards in a small daily block. If flashcards take more than about an hour a day, look at whether you are making too many cards or integrating too little.

## Techniques in school

A class that demands linear notes, or that buries you in homework, can still leave room for methods that build knowledge. If the teacher is not checking, use the method you came to use underneath the surface notes. If that is not possible, spend as little time as you can on unweighted assignments so more of the day is left for real study.

**Time-boxing** adds a hard stop. If you set one hour aside, the hour ends when it ends. The box fits tasks that matter a great deal but cannot finish in one sitting, and tasks that barely matter but still have to be done. Compulsory homework that does not count toward a final grade is the second case: aim for the best result the box allows.

Unconstrained tasks expand or shrink to fill the time given, roughly eighty percent of results often come from twenty percent of the work, and sorting tasks by importance and urgency prevents urgent trivia from eating time meant for skill building.

If you must sit through a class that teaches you nothing new, you can reduce how deep your first pass is before you arrive, leaving more to figure out in the room, or keep ahead and accept that class will be dull. Students who have already done a first pass can treat the hour as a live quiz and ask questions they would not have thought to ask while sitting passively.

## Open questions

- How does a later question make an earlier marked item suddenly clearer?
- How would you decide, in about twenty seconds, whether today's new fact is an isolated card or a wider example?
- How much of today's homework is actually worth more than the boxed time you would give it?
