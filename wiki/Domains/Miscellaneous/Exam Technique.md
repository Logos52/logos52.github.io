# Exam Technique

Sleep the night before a test is not optional. What you studied remains shut away and unusable until deeper sleep carries it into higher-level memory, which is sleep-dependent memory consolidation. Cramming and all-nighters belong earlier. The night before, get more than enough sleep, because extra rest also protects problem-solving. Sleep loss can cost as much as 20 IQ points, which hurts both recall and the ability to work a hard question.

Checking every answer the same way wastes time and raises the chance of flipping a right answer to a wrong one. Older advice said never to change an answer. Newer work suggests that going back can move you to a better one, as long as you are selective. The working method is priority checking. Keep a steady pace that saves some time at the end, handle each item while you are relatively sure, and mark the shaky items so you can come back to them.

Do not stay inside a hard question past its time budget. If you are aiming for about 1 minute per question and you cannot crack one in that window, put down your best attempt, star the item, and go on. Lingering usually makes the fog thicker. Coming back later often opens the question up, and a later item can make an earlier one clearer. If the first pass felt certain, changing that answer usually makes it wrong. Return only to the low-confidence flagged ones.

Another way to miss marks is to practice only the question shapes you already handle well. Take what you know and ask how you would set a hard paper if you wanted to throw curveballs. That surfaces question types you would otherwise skip, including the type D items that sit outside your usual drills. Writing 40 to 50 questions on a subject and swapping them with a study group trains that examiner view. It can beat leaning only on past papers, deepens the hard concepts, and carries into university settings where old papers are thin or a poor guide.

Anxiety produces the tunnel vision that makes an obvious answer vanish. Long, slow, deep breaths help here, especially a very slow exhale of about 10 seconds. That breath contracts the diaphragm and triggers the parasympathetic rest-and-digest response, which forcibly turns down fight-or-flight. Force the breath to settle until you calm, leave a little mental room, then come back at full focus. If the nerves appear only in high-pressure sittings, practise under increasingly exam-like pressure beforehand, with strict time limits, real conditions, and friends present so the session feels competitive. Write a pre-exam checklist so stress does not erase these moves, and decide when you will begin working through it, maybe weeks ahead, so the safety nets are already in place.
