# Exam Technique

Sleep the night before a test is not optional. What you studied remains shut away and unusable until deeper sleep carries it into higher-level memory, which is sleep-dependent memory consolidation. Cramming and all-nighters belong earlier. The night before, get more than enough sleep, because extra rest also protects problem-solving. Sleep loss can cost as much as 20 IQ points, which hurts both recall and the ability to work a hard question.

Checking every answer the same way wastes time and raises the chance of flipping a right answer to a wrong one. Older advice said never to change an answer. Newer work suggests that going back can move you to a better one, as long as you are selective. The working method is priority checking. Keep a steady pace that saves some time at the end, handle each item while you are relatively sure, and mark the shaky items so you can come back to them.

Do not stay inside a hard question past its time budget. If you are aiming for about 1 minute per question and you cannot crack one in that window, put down your best attempt, star the item, and go on. Lingering usually makes the fog thicker. Coming back later often opens the question up, and a later item can make an earlier one clearer. If the first pass felt certain, changing that answer usually makes it wrong. Return only to the low-confidence flagged ones.

Another way to miss marks is to practice only the question shapes you already handle well. Take what you know and ask how you would set a hard paper if you wanted to throw curveballs. That surfaces question types you would otherwise skip, including the type D items that sit outside your usual drills. Writing 40 to 50 questions on a subject and swapping them with a study group trains that examiner view. It can beat leaning only on past papers, deepens the hard concepts, and carries into university settings where old papers are thin or a poor guide.

Anxiety produces the tunnel vision that makes an obvious answer vanish. Long, slow, deep breaths help here, especially a very slow exhale of about 10 seconds. That breath contracts the diaphragm and triggers the parasympathetic rest-and-digest response, which forcibly turns down fight-or-flight. Force the breath to settle until you calm, leave a little mental room, then come back at full focus. If the nerves appear only in high-pressure sittings, practise under increasingly exam-like pressure beforehand, with strict time limits, real conditions, and friends present so the session feels competitive. Write a pre-exam checklist so stress does not erase these moves, and decide when you will begin working through it, maybe weeks ahead, so the safety nets are already in place.

## Exam Execution

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Exam execution is the late part of the learning system, when the material is already learned and the remaining work is to perform on the day. Sleep the night before comes first, because what you studied stays unusable until deep sleep moves it into longer-term memory. Calm comes next, since anxiety narrows attention until an obvious answer vanishes. Inside the paper, priority checking and a time budget per question protect the marks you can already earn. Four methods then cover the late-stage work, which are writing questions you cannot yet answer, structuring long answers, fixing mistakes that get called silly and studying in a group with set roles.

Calm is the next thing to protect, because anxiety produces the tunnel vision that makes an obvious answer disappear. A long, slow exhale of about 10 seconds triggers the rest-and-digest response and turns down fight-or-flight. Nerves that appear only in high-pressure sittings respond to practice under more and more exam-like pressure beforehand, and a written pre-exam checklist keeps stress from erasing these moves. Last-minute studying at the exam-room door does little to help.

Once the paper starts, the same calm carries into how you spend time on it. Checking every answer the same way wastes time and raises the chance of flipping a right answer to a wrong one, so the working method is priority checking. You keep a steady pace, mark the shaky items and return only to those. A hard question gets a best attempt and a star once it runs past its time budget, because coming back later often opens it up.

## How to diagnose and fix exam mistakes

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

A mistake you call silly is hard to fix, because the label hides its cause. Most of these errors come from weak preparation more than from poor technique in the exam room, and even an obvious slip usually marks a place where understanding or application is thin. So the work starts with diagnosis. You read how your errors cluster to name the biggest likely cause, try one fix aimed at that cause, then reflect on what else might be behind the rest, and keep repeating the loop. Five causes are the common ones. They are test anxiety, missing lower-order knowledge, missing higher-order knowledge, too little procedural practice, and gaps that never show up in your normal retrieval, meaning whenever you pull information from memory and use it. When the cause is unclear, a practice test helps if you answer it twice. You sit it once as the exam, then write your own model answer sheet, and comparing the two brings hidden gaps into view. Questions you got right without confidence count as gaps too.

## Why "silly" is the wrong label

Two causes sit behind most so-called silly mistakes. The less common one is weak attention to detail. It can be temporary after a short night or a stressful stretch, but more often years of being waved away have turned it into a habit. The more common one is missing logic. When a student lacks part of the understanding, they skip over information without noticing, and the answer key still makes sense to them when they read it. That feeling of sense is the trap, because following a chain of reasoning is easier than producing it with nothing in front of you.

Unit errors in maths and physics show the pattern clearly. Once a student understands what each unit stands for, the conversion mistakes usually stop, because a wrong conversion now clashes with their understanding as well as with what they memorised. An error that clashes with understanding is one you have a better chance of catching.

Effort alone doesn't fix this. Ignoring the mistake and hoping it won't come back fails, just as a vague promise to be more careful fails. Fixing the preparation system comes first, so that fewer gaps reach the exam, and only after that is it worth tuning exam technique.

1. With test anxiety, the errors look random. They don't line up with question difficulty, question type, subject or kind of mistake, yet they grow as the stakes rise. The fix is to practise under rising pressure, starting with timed papers in exam conditions, then cutting the time, then doing them alongside others. Slow, deep breathing before the test and a calming pre-test routine also help, while cramming in the final minutes before the exam mostly raises anxiety for little gain.
2. With missing lower-order knowledge, the errors cluster on recalling specific, separate facts, and the fix is a lower-order retrieval method, such as flashcards, added to your study system.
3. With missing higher-order knowledge, the errors cluster on questions that join two or more concepts, on big-picture ideas, and on choosing a strategy for a problem. The fix is more higher-order learning in both encoding and retrieval sessions, using retrieval that mixes topics and forms.
4. With too little procedural practice, the errors cluster on applying knowledge to step-by-step tasks, which is common in maths, engineering and software work. The fix is more practice that has you carry out the procedure yourself inside your retrieval sessions.
5. With gaps your retrieval never surfaces, the errors are spread across several of these kinds, and the same ones come back in practice tests. The fix is the double generation practice test, which is built to surface exactly these gaps.

Attention to detail also depends on pressure, confidence and self-belief. If your accuracy drops mainly when the stakes are high, five habits target that directly.

1. Speak positively to yourself about how you prepared.
2. Build a routine that brings you to your best state.
3. Train focus with mindfulness practice.
4. Picture yourself doing well, along with the steps that got you there.
5. Bring exam conditions into revision gradually.

## The double generation practice test

Practice papers often show the first signs of an error, but the signs are easy to miss if you check the answers too soon or leave a question half answered. The double generation method is built to stop that.

1. Sit a practice test and write out every answer in full, as you would in the real exam.
2. Mark each place where you were unsure as you go.
3. Build your own answer sheet of ideal model answers, drawing on your notes and other resources.
4. Check your exam answers against the sheet you built.
5. Check your sheet against the official one, if there is one.

You finish with three lists. One holds the questions you were unsure about, one holds the questions your own sheet shows you got wrong, and one holds the model answers the official sheet shows were incomplete. Every item on them is a likely gap, and each one gets restudied with an encoding technique. An answer you got right without confidence stays on the list, because luck may have carried it, and a reworded version of the same question could catch you next time.

Writing the answers matters. Answering in your head is not enough to expose the illusion of explanatory depth, which is the feeling that you understand something better than you can explain it. That illusion is a common reason practice tests still let gaps slip past.

Building your own answer sheet is the slow step and the most valuable one. You may use only the official sheet if time is very short. Making your own is better because it forces lower-order and higher-order thinking at once, since you have to judge what a perfect answer would contain. As a rough estimate, doing it is worth about as much as five or six practice tests done without it. It also teaches you material the question never asked about, because you have to learn the ground around each question to write a full answer.

Without a practice test you can write your own questions, find similar ones in textbooks or online, ask an AI tool to draft some, or swap tests with a small study group. A group works best when you can manage it, since someone else's questions come from a perspective you lack. Skipping the official sheet is fine when there isn't one, and comparing your model answers with a friend's lowers the chance that you both missed the same point.

## Habits on the day

A few habits keep exam performance steady once preparation is sound. Sleep comes first, because memory consolidates during sleep, so plan on spending 9 hours or more in bed each night before exams. During the paper, double-check only the answers you are unsure about so the pace stays good on the rest. Think like an examiner as you revise, asking how an examiner could test your knowledge in the ways you feel least sure of. Slow, deep breathing calms the body's stress response, and with practice it can wear down an anxiety habit. Under exam stress these tips are easy to forget, so a pre-exam checklist with a start date on your calendar, possibly weeks ahead, turns them into a safety net.

## How to prepare for ultra high-volume exams

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

# How to prepare for ultra high-volume exams

An ultra high-volume exam asks you to cover a huge body of fixed material and, often, a second stream of dynamic material that keeps updating, such as news and current affairs. Only about 1 percent to 2 percent of that body will actually be tested, and you do not know which sliver it will be. Past papers show examiner trends, which topics come up more often, but they do not make a complete map. A large public exam changed format in a recent year and caught people who had leaned too hard on past practice. You need a strategy that uses practice questions without depending on them alone.

A single-semester exam can shrink study time a great deal once your method improves. An ultra high-volume exam can shrink time only a little. There is always more content, more testing, or more depth that could fill the hours. Efficiency shows up as a higher return on the same time, meaning more coverage, more gap-finding, or more detail, not as an empty calendar. Measure the method by those returns, not by whether the clock got shorter.

Conventional repetition also fails the arithmetic. Research-typical retention sits around 50 percent at 1 week. On a short syllabus you can often cover the forgotten half again, and you may only need to hold the material from the first day until about 40 days or 60 days later. On an ultra high-volume exam, forgetting 50 percent means you never finish the set. There comes a point where repetition consumes 100 percent of study time and you still cannot relearn everything, let alone take in anything new. The retention window is also much longer. Something learned on the first day may not be tested until about 300 days later, or after 1 to 5 years of preparation. A method that cannot produce enough retention, for long enough, at enough depth, will put you further behind the longer you keep it. At the extreme, no amount of extra hours can fix an equation that does not close.

Hit rate is the chance that what you are learning will be useful on the paper, and it has two parts. One is the chance that the topic is tested at all. The other is how wide you make the box around a detail. Learning one-to-one protects you against one shape of question if your attention and depth hold. Learning a little wider takes longer and can protect you against that question plus nearby ones, closer to a ratio of 1 question protected to about 1.2 questions protected. If the extra width cost no time you would always take it. Because it does cost time, you choose.

Two checks help you choose. Conceptual relevance asks whether the detail extends something you already know is important. A small legislative change that sits inside a larger health debate has that relevance. An isolated notice that does not connect to anything you are building does not. Repeated testing trends ask whether the detail keeps appearing in past papers or whether, when you test yourself, you find you need it more often than you expected. A fact you first treated as isolated can reveal itself as a useful example across several angles once you start testing. Isolated facts go on flashcards. Wider facts get used in short answers, added into an existing map as a concrete example, and worked into the schema, with a card added only if you still worry about forgetting them. Better encoding then makes those cards come up less often, which keeps the daily deck from piling up. Screening a new item takes about 20 seconds. Frequent testing gives you a margin of error on that first call, because a wrong isolation or a weak integration will show up when you retrieve.

Encoding skill sits on a spectrum. The gold-standard version, highly organized knowledge with high retention and depth, is also the most efficient, but skill moves slowly. Going from a skill level you might call 2 points out of 10 points to 10 points out of 10 points can take about 10 months of diligent work, or years. If you aim at that gold standard on every item while your skill is still low, coverage stalls. The practical move is to push just past your current level. Raising retention from 40 percent to 50 percent is only a 10 percent gain on paper, and 50 percent is still modest. Even so, it cuts repetition by 10 percent every day and every week. Across years of review that can save dozens of hours a month. Compare yourself with your current method, not with perfect encoding. If you truly have spare years, you can spend the first year prioritizing the learning skill and turn to exam tactics later. Most people do not have that spare time, so they improve the method in small steps while still moving through the material.

Self-testing needs a set order. Start with high-volume methods that test the big picture, or a mix of big-picture and detail. Those methods force a large generation of what you know, such as a brain dump or teaching the topic to a beginner from memory. Those methods find big-picture gaps, the missing section or the wrong overall picture, before they spread. Filling those gaps early also makes later material easier to place. A set of 20 practice questions is more targeted. High-volume retrieval works best early, when gaps are large enough that you hit one every couple of minutes. Waiting months can stretch that to one gap every 20 minutes, which is too slow. Do a high-volume big-picture retrieval within the first week, and every week, so the pile stays manageable. Leaving it longer can turn the session into something like 15 hours. Later, move toward targeted questions, quizzes, focused explanations, and flashcards. Flashcards run in parallel in a small daily block, perhaps 30 minutes once or twice a day. If flashcards take more than about 1 hour a day, look at whether you are making too many or integrating too little. More than 1.5 hours a day, even on a high-volume exam, usually means content is arriving faster than retention can space it out, which is the same overwhelm described above.

The same calendar will mix both kinds of testing. Last week's material still needs high-volume big-picture retrieval. Material from 6 months ago can take targeted, lower-order checks. Track which layer each batch is in.

Encoding itself has a shape worth watching. Groups that are too specific force you to memorize the group names, the way an unmemorable technical list does not cue its neighbors. Groups that are too generic, such as applying the same interior-versus-exterior split to every topic, or the same history-exam-investigation-management template to every case, stop helping you remember which topic you are in. Aim for a structure that is specific enough to be unique to the topic and intuitive enough that remembering one part cues the rest, the way before, during, and after cue one another. How well you can do that depends on skill. Make a best attempt at your current level rather than waiting for a perfect map. A large map built over 20 hours that then has to be rebuilt because a major concept was missing from the first 30 minutes is the cost of delaying that big-picture check.

People who have done countless thousands of practice questions still often walk into the hall with the feeling that they have missed something. Volume of questions is required. It has to sit on a foundation that lets you move through content and keep it, rather than on a wheel of repetition that does not advance.

## Techniques in School

# Techniques in School

A class that demands linear notes, or that buries you in homework, can still leave room for methods that actually build knowledge. The aim is to protect time for that work without picking a fight you do not need.

## Working inside the classroom

If the teacher is not checking, use the method you came to use. Linear or passive notes can stay on the surface while you work in your own way underneath.

If that is not possible, spend as little time as you can on unweighted assignments so more of the day is left for real study. Time-boxing, below, is the tool for that.

Class can also be a place to find gaps. Students who have already done a first pass through the material can treat the hour as a live quiz. They can look for what is still unclear and ask questions they would not have thought to ask while sitting passively.

Homework that is mostly a set of questions can be done in class when you already know the material or find it easy.

## Time-boxing

Time-blocking puts a task on the calendar. Time-boxing adds a hard stop. If you set 1 hour aside, the hour ends when it ends. The box is most useful for two kinds of work. It fits tasks that matter a great deal and cannot be finished in one sitting, and tasks that barely matter but still have to be done.

Improving how you learn is often important and not urgent. A boxed session lets you make progress on it without waiting for a free afternoon that never comes.

Compulsory homework that does not count toward a final grade is the other case. You have to do it, and it should not take more time than it is worth. The aim is the best result the box will allow, sometimes called a best-for-time outcome, which also helps against the urge to polish past the point of value.

Two regular patterns explain why the box works. Parkinson's law says a task expands or shrinks to fill the time given. Give it a long stretch and it will use a long stretch. Give it a short one, within reason, and it still gets done. The Pareto principle says about 80 percent of a result often comes from about 20 percent of the work. The same split shows up in how much of a subject actually drives learning, and in how much time certain distractions consume. It also shows up in how much of an essay is written in a small share of the total time.

An Eisenhower matrix sorts tasks by importance and urgency. It shows where an unimportant but urgent task belongs, so that task does not eat the boxed time meant for something that matters more.

## When you can negotiate, and when you cannot

If the teacher seems open to it, you can describe the methods you are trying and ask to use them. Students who have already shown they know the material have sometimes been allowed to study on their own during class.

If none of that works, and you must sit through a class that teaches you nothing new, reduce how deep your first pass through the material is before you arrive. A shallower first pass leaves more to figure out in the room, which raises the mental load of the hour and makes it more engaging. The time you would have spent on a deeper first pass can then go to interleaved retrieval instead. The remaining choice is to keep ahead and accept that class will be dull. Being bored because you already know the material is a good problem to have.
