## Sources

- Newbury, C. R., Crowley, R., Rastle, K., & Tamminen, J. (2021). Sleep deprivation and memory: Meta-analytic reviews of studies on sleep deprivation before and after learning. *Psychological Bulletin*. Deprivation before and after encoding harms memory for newly learned material. A later recovery sleep shrinks but does not erase the cost.
- Walker, M. P., & Stickgold, R. (2004). Sleep-dependent learning and memory consolidation. *Neuron*, 44(1), 121–133. Consolidation is doing the work on the night after encoding. An all-nighter is not repaid by sleeping in later.
- Okano, K., Kaczmarzyk, J. R., Dave, N., Gabrieli, J. D. E., & Grossman, J. C. (2019). Sleep quality, duration, and consistency are associated with better academic performance in college students. *npj Science of Learning*, 4, 16.
- Hirshkowitz, M., et al. (2015). National Sleep Foundation's sleep time duration recommendations. *Sleep Health*, 1(1), 40–43. Adults: 7–9 hours of sleep, not a fixed time-in-bed number. Consensus of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and Sleep Research Society (2015): adults obtain seven or more hours.
- Zaccaro, A., et al. (2018). How breath-control can change your life: A systematic review on psycho-physiological correlates of slow breathing. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*, 12, 353. Slow-paced breathing (~6 breaths/min) increases vagal activity and reduces state anxiety.
- Lehrer, P. M., & Gevirtz, R. (2014). Heart rate variability biofeedback: How and why does it work? *Frontiers in Psychology*, 5, 756. Resonant-frequency breathing as the same parasympathetic mechanism.
- Hembree, R. (1988). Correlates, causes, effects, and treatment of test anxiety. *Review of Educational Research*, 58(1), 47–77. Behavioral and cognitive-behavioral treatments reduce test anxiety and raise performance. Avoidance maintains the anxiety.
- Goldfried, M. R., Linehan, M. M., & Smith, J. L. (1978). Reduction of test anxiety through cognitive restructuring. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*, 46(1), 32–39.
