# How to prepare for ultra high-volume exams

An ultra high-volume exam covers years of material, often with a stream of new material such as current events added along the way, and it tests only a small, unknown fraction of it. Methods that work for a single semester, such as rereading and heavy repetition, break down at this scale. The plan has to raise how much you keep from each hour of study, since adding hours cannot cover the loss.

## Takeaways

- At high volume, poor retention means you can never catch up.
- Better methods show as more coverage and depth in the same hours.
- Learn important facts widely and isolated facts on flashcards.
- Test early with broad recall, later with targeted questions.
- Aim each study session one level above your current skill.
- Be solid on core ideas before chasing fine details.
- Decide on purpose what you will leave out.

## Why the usual method fails

Suppose you keep about half of what you learn after a week, which is a common working figure. In a semester exam with a few weeks of material, repeating everything once or twice covers the loss. With years of material the relearning grows until it takes all your study time, and new content still waits. More hours cannot close that gap, so retention itself has to rise. How much you keep depends on how well a topic was taken in the first time, which is called encoding.

```
low retention ──> more relearning each week
      ^                     │
      │                     v
 no time left for  <── relearning fills
 better encoding       all study time
```

- The exam may come a year or more after you learn a topic.
- Past papers show trends, but examiners change the format.
- Keeping the familiar method is the risky choice.

## Choosing how deep to learn

Each fact you learn protects you against some questions. Learned as a bare fact, it answers one question. Learned with its links to bigger ideas, it also answers the related questions, at the cost of a little more time. Two signals say a fact is worth learning with its links: it ties into a concept you already know matters, or you keep needing it when you test yourself. Screening a new item this way takes about twenty seconds.

- Isolated fact: put it on a flashcard.
- Connected fact: an example on your mind map, then a test.
- Frequent testing corrects a wrong call on either side.

## Testing in phases

Testing finds gaps, and the kind of test should change as a topic ages. In the first week after learning a topic, broad recall, such as writing down everything you know or teaching it to a beginner, finds big structural errors early, before later study builds on them. Around three to four weeks, practice papers fit. After that, targeted questions and flashcards hold the details, and different topics sit in different phases at the same time.

- Week one: brain dump or teach it from memory, repeated weekly.
- Three to four weeks: practice papers and exam questions.
- Later: targeted quizzes, AI-made questions, daily flashcards.
- More than ninety minutes of flashcards a day is a warning sign.

## Building the first structure

A careful first attempt at organising a topic saves repeated fixes later. The structure should be simple, intuitive and specific to the topic, so that remembering any part of it brings back the rest. Groups named in jargon have to be memorised one by one. A template reused for every topic, such as history, examination, tests and treatment for every disease, tells you nothing about any single one. Assume the structure is wrong somewhere, and rebuild it when a test shows where.

- Early on, testing does more than careful encoding.
- As skill grows, better encoding cuts the testing needed.
- Raising retention from 40% to 50% saves time every week.

## Sorting what you miss

When a test goes badly, name the kind of gap. Poor flow between ideas means weak understanding of how they connect, and rereading will not fix it. Missing facts or examples point to detail gaps. Awkward writing around sound ideas points to a need for practice. The layers below show where the marks come from.

| Layer | Share of material | Share of marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core ideas | under 5% | about 30% |
| Extended concepts | about 15% | about 20% |
| Details and examples | about 30% | about 20% |
| Fine, scattered details | about 50% | the rest |

- Aim for near-full confidence on core ideas, extended concepts and details.
- Accept mixed confidence on the fine, scattered details.
