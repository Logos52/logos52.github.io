# How to prepare for ultra high-volume exams

An ultra high-volume exam asks you to cover a huge body of fixed material and, often, a second stream of dynamic material that keeps updating, such as news and current affairs. Only about 1 percent to 2 percent of that body will actually be tested, and you do not know which sliver it will be. Past papers show examiner trends, which topics come up more often, but they do not make a complete map. A large public exam changed format in a recent year and caught people who had leaned too hard on past practice. You need a strategy that uses practice questions without depending on them alone.

A single-semester exam can shrink study time a great deal once your method improves. An ultra high-volume exam can shrink time only a little. There is always more content, more testing, or more depth that could fill the hours. Efficiency shows up as a higher return on the same time, meaning more coverage, more gap-finding, or more detail, not as an empty calendar. Measure the method by those returns, not by whether the clock got shorter.

Conventional repetition also fails the arithmetic. Research-typical retention sits around 50 percent at 1 week. On a short syllabus you can often cover the forgotten half again, and you may only need to hold the material from the first day until about 40 days or 60 days later. On an ultra high-volume exam, forgetting 50 percent means you never finish the set. There comes a point where repetition consumes 100 percent of study time and you still cannot relearn everything, let alone take in anything new. The retention window is also much longer. Something learned on the first day may not be tested until about 300 days later, or after 1 to 5 years of preparation. A method that cannot produce enough retention, for long enough, at enough depth, will put you further behind the longer you keep it. At the extreme, no amount of extra hours can fix an equation that does not close.

Hit rate is the chance that what you are learning will be useful on the paper, and it has two parts. One is the chance that the topic is tested at all. The other is how wide you make the box around a detail. Learning one-to-one protects you against one shape of question if your attention and depth hold. Learning a little wider takes longer and can protect you against that question plus nearby ones, closer to a ratio of 1 question protected to about 1.2 questions protected. If the extra width cost no time you would always take it. Because it does cost time, you choose.

Two checks help you choose. Conceptual relevance asks whether the detail extends something you already know is important. A small legislative change that sits inside a larger health debate has that relevance. An isolated notice that does not connect to anything you are building does not. Repeated testing trends ask whether the detail keeps appearing in past papers or whether, when you test yourself, you find you need it more often than you expected. A fact you first treated as isolated can reveal itself as a useful example across several angles once you start testing. Isolated facts go on flashcards. Wider facts get used in short answers, added into an existing map as a concrete example, and worked into the schema, with a card added only if you still worry about forgetting them. Better encoding then makes those cards come up less often, which keeps the daily deck from piling up. Screening a new item takes about 20 seconds. Frequent testing gives you a margin of error on that first call, because a wrong isolation or a weak integration will show up when you retrieve.

Encoding skill sits on a spectrum. The gold-standard version, highly organized knowledge with high retention and depth, is also the most efficient, but skill moves slowly. Going from a skill level you might call 2 points out of 10 points to 10 points out of 10 points can take about 10 months of diligent work, or years. If you aim at that gold standard on every item while your skill is still low, coverage stalls. The practical move is to push just past your current level. Raising retention from 40 percent to 50 percent is only a 10 percent gain on paper, and 50 percent is still modest. Even so, it cuts repetition by 10 percent every day and every week. Across years of review that can save dozens of hours a month. Compare yourself with your current method, not with perfect encoding. If you truly have spare years, you can spend the first year prioritizing the learning skill and turn to exam tactics later. Most people do not have that spare time, so they improve the method in small steps while still moving through the material.

Self-testing needs a set order. Start with high-volume methods that test the big picture, or a mix of big-picture and detail. Those methods force a large generation of what you know, such as a brain dump or teaching the topic to a beginner from memory. Those methods find big-picture gaps, the missing section or the wrong overall picture, before they spread. Filling those gaps early also makes later material easier to place. A set of 20 practice questions is more targeted. High-volume retrieval works best early, when gaps are large enough that you hit one every couple of minutes. Waiting months can stretch that to one gap every 20 minutes, which is too slow. Do a high-volume big-picture retrieval within the first week, and every week, so the pile stays manageable. Leaving it longer can turn the session into something like 15 hours. Later, move toward targeted questions, quizzes, focused explanations, and flashcards. Flashcards run in parallel in a small daily block, perhaps 30 minutes once or twice a day. If flashcards take more than about 1 hour a day, look at whether you are making too many or integrating too little. More than 1.5 hours a day, even on a high-volume exam, usually means content is arriving faster than retention can space it out, which is the same overwhelm described above.

The same calendar will mix both kinds of testing. Last week's material still needs high-volume big-picture retrieval. Material from 6 months ago can take targeted, lower-order checks. Track which layer each batch is in.

Encoding itself has a shape worth watching. Groups that are too specific force you to memorize the group names, the way an unmemorable technical list does not cue its neighbors. Groups that are too generic, such as applying the same interior-versus-exterior split to every topic, or the same history-exam-investigation-management template to every case, stop helping you remember which topic you are in. Aim for a structure that is specific enough to be unique to the topic and intuitive enough that remembering one part cues the rest, the way before, during, and after cue one another. How well you can do that depends on skill. Make a best attempt at your current level rather than waiting for a perfect map. A large map built over 20 hours that then has to be rebuilt because a major concept was missing from the first 30 minutes is the cost of delaying that big-picture check.

People who have done countless thousands of practice questions still often walk into the hall with the feeling that they have missed something. Volume of questions is required. It has to sit on a foundation that lets you move through content and keep it, rather than on a wheel of repetition that does not advance.

## Open questions

How would you decide, in about 20 seconds, whether today's new fact is an isolated card or a wider example?

What would change in next week's schedule if high-volume retrieval had to happen before the pile grew past a single sitting?
