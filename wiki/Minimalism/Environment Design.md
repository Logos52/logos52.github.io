# Environment Design

Environment design means arranging a room, a desk or a phone so that the behaviour you want is the easy default and the behaviour you want to stop is hard to reach. Most daily actions are habits set off by what is in front of you, so changing the surroundings works better than resisting each urge. A cookie jar left on the table gets emptied every day, and one put away in a cupboard may be forgotten for months.

## Core takeaways

- Behaviour follows what is in view more than willpower.
- Remove the cue for a bad habit.
- Add a cue for a good one.
- Make the unwanted action take more effort than it is worth.
- Spend the effort on redesign instead of resisting each urge.
- Hiding objects in storage still leaves their costs.
- Keep a log of triggers and design a counter for each.

## How it works

Much of what a person does in a day is repeated in the same place at the same time, set off by something seen or heard. The action runs before any decision is made, which is why resisting it at that moment usually fails. Changing the cue, or adding a small barrier, stops the action before it starts. Objects in view also compete for attention, so a clear surface leaves more attention for the task.

- A phone in view is a cue.
- A phone in a drawer gives no cue.
- An uninstalled game is too much trouble to reinstall.
- A clear floor is quick to cross and quick to clean.
- A packed cupboard hides the amount owned, and the costs stay.

## How to do it

Treat the redesign as the main plan instead of a helper to willpower. Setting the phone to do-not-disturb, deleting one icon and stopping there leaves the rest of the work to willpower. Keep a sheet nearby, write down every time something sets off an unwanted habit, and plan a fix for each one. Other people act on habit too, and a sign on a closed door can stop a family member walking in where years of asking did not.

- Keep the phone out of reach, such as under clothes in a wardrobe.
- Mute or deactivate social media accounts during work periods.
- Block words at the router and give the password to someone else.
- Open video sites in a private window to hide recommendations.
- Link straight to a video instead of browsing the site.
- Raise furniture on legs so a robot vacuum reaches under it.
- Keep furniture below head height so the room feels open.

## Where it fails

Plans of this kind usually fail in one of three ways. Separately from those, a cluttered room adds competing cues inside a work session, in the same way that objects on a desk compete for attention. Clearing the space before the session starts saves effort during it.

- Too few of the habits that matter were listed at the start.
- The written plans for each trigger needed too much motivation to carry out.
- The changes to the surroundings were too small to matter.
