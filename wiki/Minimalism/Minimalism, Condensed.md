# Minimalism, Condensed

The limiting resource is maintenance attention, not money. Every object, subscription, and commitment draws some of that attention for as long as it stays in your life. Reduction comes before optimization: deciding not to want a thing costs less than owning it and removing it later. The room is part of the same system. Layout prompts action and sets defaults before willpower is invoked. Empty space is slack held in reserve, not waste. Decide how an object will leave before it comes in. The settled doctrine lives in [[wiki/Minimalism/Minimalism as Systems Design|Minimalism as Systems Design]]; spending and investing are in [[wiki/Money/Money, Condensed|Money, Condensed]].

## The costs

Owning an object costs more than its purchase price. The price is paid once. After that, space, cleaning, maintenance, attention, storage, setup, and future decisions keep arriving. Money already spent does not return when you keep the object; you only extend the recurring bill. Storing something postpones a choice and lets the cost compound. The cheapest avoidance is on the demand side: ask what a purchase is supposed to say, and to whom. Much wanting is a signal. Naming the signal often ends the want before any ownership cost begins.

Maintenance attention is the real constraint. Owning less helps even when income is unchanged, because the attention budget is fixed. The honest question is what you are maintaining that no longer supports the life you are building.

## The operations

Reduce first, optimize later. Organizing a system that holds too much improves the wrong thing. Remove items, then arrange what remains.

Product reduction is one pass over one zone. Gather everything from that zone in one place so the true amount owned is visible. Choose what stays by asking what the life under construction needs, not from a list of things to throw away. Remove the highest-cost objects first; otherwise small easy items leave and large burdens stay. One short, intense session beats months of incremental picking. A box marked "without it" and one week of living without those objects makes each removal reversible; if nothing breaks, discard the box. Every discarded object is feedback about a buying mistake. The main product of the pass is one written buying rule.

Decide how an object will leave before it comes in: used up, returned, sold, given away, or discarded. A plan to sell gets a deadline of two weeks even when the money is small. Without a deadline, a chair meant for sale spends a month in the hallway. Before buying, ask how it will leave, whether it can be rented or borrowed, what storage or maintenance debt it creates, and what rule says when it comes out.

## How the room affects behaviour

The environment shapes behaviour before willpower does. A space cues action, uses attention, creates friction, and sets the default path whether or not anyone designed it. The two moves are placement: put the desired action where it is seen, and make the competing action cost one more reach. The resistance you would spend at the moment of temptation is spent once, while arranging the room.

Empty space is reserve capacity. When every surface is full, moving one thing means moving other things first. Operational slack matters more than visual emptiness. What sits in view sets the day's defaults; objects left out are the objects you use, and what you use shapes who you are.

Minimalism clarifies identity. Reduction removes objects that belonged to identities you have given up. What remains shows what the life is for. Wabi-sabi is a separate taste call (beauty in wear and imperfection from real use, not sloppy work shipped on purpose), not a requirement of owning less.
