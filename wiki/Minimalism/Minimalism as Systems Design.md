# Minimalism as Systems Design

> Not yet reviewed by Wedge.

Volume matters more than item count, because volume is what has to be carried and moved. A documented one-room flat of 30 square meters is built around that measure. Its standing rule is to keep only what a person can move without paying a moving firm, and the last move used a borrowed car. [[wiki/Minimalism/Ownership Cost|Ownership Cost]] names the costs that keep arriving after the purchase is paid, including space, cleaning, attention, storage, and the later choice about how the object will leave. Reducing that burden is the work of this group of pages.

The surroundings do part of the deciding. [[wiki/Dimensions/Self-Management|Self-Management]] is the habits, daily routines, and rooms that let action stay steady, so less energy goes to just getting through the day. [[wiki/Self Management/Procrastination - a System Problem|Procrastination - a System Problem]] treats procrastination as a setup problem, and changing the surroundings as the strongest single move, because a temptation that never appears costs no willpower. [[wiki/Self Management/Flow State|Flow State]] starts the same way from the other side of a work block: the outside forces that push you off a task are removed first. [[wiki/Concepts/Style|Style]] begins with a similar first move, which is to own less.

## Ownership Cost Matters More Than Purchase Price

[[wiki/Minimalism/Ownership Cost|Ownership Cost]] counts the burden an object keeps imposing after the bill is paid. The purchase price is the smallest of those costs. Most cases come down to three questions: how often the object actually gets used, what space it occupies while it waits, and whether it would be bought again today.

The documented flat tracks volume rather than count, because a small number of bulky things can still fill a room and block a move. A thing one person cannot pick up makes every later shift harder, which is why the rule is set at what can be carried without a company.

## Reduce First, Optimize Later

[[wiki/Minimalism/Product Reduction|Product Reduction]] is a single pass across one zone. Everything in that zone is brought into one pile, so the true amount owned is visible at once. Keepers are chosen by asking what the life now being built actually requires, and the highest-cost objects go first.

The documented flat then designs around what remains, and it does so by leaving storage furniture out. The flat has no shoe cabinet, and the only cupboard is in the kitchen, so everything owned stays in sight and cannot be hidden at the back of a cupboard. Storage that hides things postpones the choice to keep them or let them go. A surface of visible inventory makes that choice harder to defer.

## Empty Space Is Reserve Capacity

Empty floor is what is left when volume is kept down and storage furniture is left out. In the documented flat, furniture stands on legs or casters so a cleaning robot can pass underneath, including the desk, bed, and stool. Two identical folding desks, each about 120 centimeters by 40 centimeters, go together in about a minute. Set end to end against the wall they form a 240-centimeter run, and pushed together they make a dining table for several people. The layout can change rather than being locked to one fixed plan.

That unused floor is not a leftover. It is the slack that lets a small change be made without first undoing the rest of the room.

## Visible Defaults

[[wiki/Minimalism/Environment Design|Environment Design]] arranges a room so the layout itself settles part of what happens next. The wanted action sits in view. The competing action costs one extra reach. The resistance that would have been spent at the instant of temptation is paid once, while the room is being arranged.

The documented case applies the same idea when it buys furniture. Nothing is taller than its owner, and the heights are kept level, to take the sense of pressure out of the room. The shaver and an electric screwdriver were both chosen because they charge over USB, which cuts the number of chargers and cables. [[wiki/Concepts/Meiwaku|Meiwaku]] asks a related question at the scale of other people: how much bother an action or a pile of things puts on the people nearby.

## How Big the Change Has to Be

The documented flat holds its level with standing rules. Rolling stock keeps exactly five tins or packs of one staple. Each time one is used, a replacement is bought, so the count never moves. The same stock covers daily meals and emergencies, and older items are used first so they do not expire.

Functions were moved out of the room rather than given up. Washing moved to a gym and a public bath near a flat that has no bathtub, which also freed living space. The usual pattern is to buy food the same day and use it the same day, with a small thermal cabinet for foods that cannot sit at room temperature. A projector stands in for a television, and a sleeping bag for a duvet. Face towels stand in for bath towels, and the mirror and window covering were the ones the rental already provided. The wardrobe is eight garments across the seasons, including the ones being worn, among them two summer T-shirts.

[[wiki/Minimalism/Exit Strategy For Objects|Exit Strategy For Objects]] decides the way out before an object is allowed to stay. If the way out is a sale, that sale has a two-week deadline, even for a small sum. [[wiki/Concepts/The Age Of Nonlinear Returns|The Age Of Nonlinear Returns]] treats time and optionality as the scarce resources. An easy exit keeps a way open.

## What the Room Should Feel Like

In the documented flat, the test is practical. The amount owned can be seen at a glance, because nothing is hidden in storage. A cleaning robot can pass under the furniture. The furniture line sits at or below the owner's height, so the room does not feel pressed down. The next action is easier to start when its object is already in view, which is the point of the environment-design sequence.

## The Case Against

[[wiki/Concepts/Style|Style]] starts by cutting the closet down to a few items, colors, and fits, so dressing takes less deliberation. That is a craft in service of daily life, not a display of having little. [[wiki/Minimalism/Wanting Less|Wanting Less]] draws the same line on the demand side. The question to ask at the moment of wanting is what the purchase is meant to signal, and to whom. A jacket that is still wanted as a jacket after that question can be bought. The target of the question was the signal, not the object.

## Objects Kept for Imagined Identities

A lot of wanting is a signal. The thing is bought to display worth, to someone else or to yourself. Naming that motive usually ends the wanting, which means the cost is never incurred. [[wiki/Minimalism/Wanting Less|Wanting Less]] is that question, asked before any ownership cost begins.

## Open questions

- How does a volume rule, rather than an item count, change what a person is willing to bring home?
- How does a room with no storage furniture change the decision to keep an object or let it go?
- How does rolling stock keep both everyday use and emergency use in the same five items?
- How do surroundings take over the work that willpower would otherwise have to do in the moment?
