# Minimalism as Systems Design

Minimalism here is environmental systems design. The real cost of a possession is attention, space, maintenance, friction, and constraints on how you can live—not the purchase price. Volume often matters more than item count, because volume is what must be carried and moved. Reduce what you own first, optimize later, and treat empty floor as reserve capacity. The short rule set is in [[wiki/Minimalism/Minimalism, Condensed|Minimalism, Condensed]].

A documented one-room flat of about thirty square meters keeps only what one person can move without a moving company. Furniture stands on legs or casters so a cleaning robot can pass underneath. There is no shoe cabinet; the only cupboard is in the kitchen, so inventory stays visible and cannot hide at the back of storage. Storage furniture postpones keep-or-cut decisions. Rolling stock holds exactly five units of one staple; each use triggers a replacement buy, so everyday meals and emergencies share the same count.

## Ownership cost

After purchase, an object keeps costing space, money, time, attention, memory, decisions, and exit. The price tag is the smallest line. Three questions settle most cases: how often it is actually used, what it occupies while it waits, and whether you would buy it again today.

High-cost objects deserve review first—furniture, appliances, storage units, rarely used equipment, duplicated tools, awkward clothing categories, subscriptions. Each object with an unmade decision attached is an open loop that will take time again later. An object is worth keeping when its function, meaning, or beauty outweighs its total recurring cost.

## Reduce first, then design the room

Product reduction is one zone, one pile, one concentrated period. What stays is chosen from the life being built, not from a discard list. Highest ownership cost goes first. The pass produces one buying rule; the smaller pile is a side effect.

Environment design arranges the room so the next intended action is the one the room shows. Desired behaviour sits where it is seen; competing behaviour costs one more reach. A room nobody arranged for your task is already arranged for someone else's outcomes—icons, notifications, placement. Emptiness alone is the wrong target; the behaviour the space is for is the target. Six aim questions: what should this space make easy or hard to begin, what belongs in sight, what should be hidden or removed, which object breaks attention, which object helps a start.

Failure modes have named repairs. Visual minimalism without behaviour support means starting from the wrong target. Storage hiding fails because stored objects still bill space and attention—reduce, then store what remains. A half-done rearrangement still depends on resolve at the moment of action; within a week of a real pass, the room should show the next intended action first.

## Exit before entry

An exit strategy is a decided way out: used up, returned, sold, given away, recycled, discarded, rented, borrowed, or served by a library instead of owning. Decide at purchase time. Selling works when demand is real and removal stays fast; give away or discard when selling would keep the object in the house for weeks waiting on a small sum. Large, awkward, low-demand objects dominate exit cost—a cheap buy becomes expensive at removal time.

For objects already owned: choose the cleanest exit, set a short deadline, stop optimizing resale when speed matters, and note why the object failed so the buying rule updates.

## Wanting less

Wanting less is one question at the moment of wanting: what is this purchase supposed to say, and to whom? Naming the signal turns an impulse into a stated decision. A jacket you still want as a jacket after the question was never the target of the test. Every object not bought skips the later bills of space, attention, maintenance, and exit. The check is the next non-routine purchase impulse: name the signal before deciding.

## Wabi-sabi at the margin

Wabi-sabi is an aesthetic stance—naturalness, simplicity, imperfection from age, wear, or deliberate roughness—not a minimalism technique. Wear must have happened through use; it does not license shipping sloppy work. In this vault it is a taste call on the Human track of Human vs AI Capability Lens, linkable when design work needs a named Human standout.

## The case against mere display

[[wiki/Concepts/Style|Style]] cuts the closet to a few items and fits so dressing takes less deliberation—a craft in service of daily life, not a performance of having little. Minimalism and style both refuse objects bought to certify an imagined self.

## Quit signals

If organizing never reduces the pile, you are optimizing a system that still holds too much. If resale delay keeps objects in hallways, the exit deadline was not enforced. If the room looks bare but still blocks the next action, behaviour was not the design target. If every purchase passes the motive test but nothing has an exit path, prevention and ownership rules are out of sync.
