# Opening Doors

A door is the shape of a page's entire opening paragraph. It is decided before any sentence in that paragraph is written. Two doors are in use. The definitional handover names what the thing is, shows the machinery, and closes on what holding it gives you. The payoff-or-ramp names what the reader gets, or eases the reader onto familiar ground until that can be named, and then hands over the method that produces it. Sentence one still takes a move from [[wiki/Writing Craft/Opening Moves Catalog|the catalog]]. The door decides what the rest of the paragraph does with that sentence.

Moves and doors are written down separately because they fail differently. A move fails inside one sentence: the wrong complement, a part in the subject seat, a definition the reader already owned. A door fails across the paragraph, most often when the opening eases the reader in and then delivers everything at once. Nothing in a first-sentence catalog can name that fault, because sentence one was fine.

## Core takeaways

- A door is the shape of the whole opening paragraph, chosen before the paragraph is written. Two are in use.
- The definitional handover gives the term first, then the parts that make it work, then what the reader has once they hold it.
- The payoff-or-ramp gives what the reader gets first, then the method that produces it. Where the payoff cannot be stated cold, the paragraph starts on wide familiar ground and walks to it.
- One question routes most pages: does the reader already own the term? Where they do not, the handover takes it. Where they do, the door opens on what the thing gives.
- Four door-level faults are recorded: the inventory, the procedure recital, the synonym swap, and the register break.
- The two doors were named in the regeneration handoffs of 2026-08-14 and tested on 49 openings across three writers.

## The definitional handover

**What it is, then the machinery, then what it buys.** The term arrives first and stays the subject. The middle of the paragraph shows the parts that make it work. The close says what the reader has once they hold it. Nothing is withheld and nothing is previewed.

It fits where the definition is itself the payload: a house coinage, a technical name, a term whose real content is not on its surface. It is also the default for a value, which gets introduced as something held rather than through the trouble it repairs.

```text
Good faith is a commitment you carry into every dealing that matters: the other person
gets the true picture from you, your real constraints, your real intentions, the
downsides you could have sat on, even when telling them costs you something now. The
cost is the working part: anyone can say cooperative things, and a disclosure that cost
you something is the one the other person can actually believe.
```

```text
An exit strategy is a decided way for an object to leave: used up, returned, sold,
given, discarded. It is named at the door, before the object gets to stay. A sale
carries a deadline with it: two weeks, even when the money is small, because chasing a
small recovery is exactly how a sold-someday chair spends a month in the hallway.
```

## The payoff-or-ramp

**What the reader gets, then the method that gets it.** Two entrances sit under one door because they answer the same question at different distances. Where the payoff can be stated cold, it opens the paragraph and the method follows. Where it cannot, the paragraph starts on wide familiar ground and walks to it. A payoff cannot be stated cold when the subject has no name yet, or when a cold statement of it would be a step the reader cannot take.

It fits where the reader already owns the term, so a definition would deliver nothing, and where a skill is better introduced by what it lets someone do than by what it is.

```text
There is a usable estimate inside almost any messy choice, and this page is how to get it
out. The exact version needs posted odds: chance times size on each side, read the sign.
The choices that matter almost never post them.
```

```text
Most of being considerate is easy to see. Take up less space, make less noise, leave
things cleaner than you found them. Nobody needs a page for that part. Speaking is the
part that is hard to see.
```

The ramp form follows the on-ramp law: the widest view first, each sentence a small step, the thesis arriving only once the reader has enough to hold it. Step height is density rather than word count.

## Choosing between them

One question routes most pages: **does the reader already own the term?** Where they do not, the definition is the payload and the handover takes it. Where they do, a definition spends sentence one on nothing, and the door opens on what the thing gives instead. Half-owned terms take either door, and the definition has to do more work to be worth the sentence.

Two overrides sit on top of that question. A page carrying a core value takes the handover with a run-up, whatever the ownership answer. A page whose subject has no nameable referent at all takes the ramp, because a handover has nothing to hand over until the page has built the ground first.

## Where doors fail

**The inventory.** The opening eases the reader in and then delivers the page's parts as a list. This is the recorded door-level fault, struck 2026-08-15: an opening that had handed over a system's one requirement, then compressed its five stages into a single clause. Sentence one was accepted. The paragraph broke two sentences later. A multi-item enumeration is the steepest step available, because each item is a claim the reader has no ground for yet. Parts belong to the body. The door carries what the thing is, what it is for, and what it gives.

**The procedure recital.** The same fault spread out: the paragraph goes through the method in order instead of handing over what the method produces. It appears wherever a bank supplies a staged loop, and it takes both doors at once when it does.

**The synonym swap.** Two doors written for the same page have to be different entrances rather than the same paragraph reworded. A pair that differs only in its first clause gives the reader of the slate nothing to choose between.

**The register break.** A door in the handover voice stapled to a body of stacked verdicts. The stance that produced the opening governs every section under it, not the opening alone.

## Where the doors came from

The doors were not designed in advance. They were named in the regeneration handoffs of 2026-08-14 and put to work immediately: 49 openings were written to them across three writers, with the owner picking one per page. The accepted set is what the specimens above are drawn from, and the failure list is what the struck set produced. Both doors descend from the same write-act: every page is something its owner holds, handed to a reader he respects. That is why each door closes on what the reader gets rather than on what the page contains.

## How to practice this

1. Before writing an opening paragraph, ask whether the reader already owns the term. If they do not, write the definitional handover.
2. Write a handover in three parts: the term, the parts that make it work, then what the reader has. Check that nothing is withheld and nothing is previewed.
3. Where the reader owns the term, state what the reader gets first, then the method. Notice whether the payoff can be stated cold.
4. If it cannot be stated cold, start on wide familiar ground. Keep each sentence a small step. Judge each step by its density, not its word count.
5. Read your opening for the inventory fault. If the paragraph delivers the page's parts as a list, move them to the body.
6. When you write two doors for one page, compare them past the first clause. If only the first clause differs, one is the other reworded.
