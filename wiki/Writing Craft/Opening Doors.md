# Opening Doors

The opening paragraph of a page can be built in a few different ways, and this wiki calls each of those ways a door. The door decides what the reader gets first, which can be a definition, a claim, a question, or a short run of familiar ground that leads to the subject. The door is chosen before sentence one is written, because the right door depends on what the reader already knows.

## Core takeaways

- Pick the door first, then write sentence one inside it.
- A reader who has never met the term needs it defined.
- A reader who already has the term can take the claim first.
- A heavy or abstract subject needs a gentle run-up.
- An opening says what the thing is, its purpose, and what it gives.
- A list of the page's parts belongs in the body.
- When unsure, use the run-up.

## The doors

The choice depends on what the reader already holds. A reader who has never met the term needs it handed over as a definition before anything else can land. A reader who knows the term can take the main claim in the first sentence. A subject too abstract to define in the first line needs a run-up, a few plain sentences on ground the reader knows, with the subject named at the end.

- Handover: the term defined in words the reader already has.
- Claim: the main finding first, for a reader who knows the terms.
- Run-up: familiar ground first, the page's subject named last.
- Two terms first: when the subject depends on two ideas, define both.
- Why: open on what the thing is for.
- Problem: open on the problem the page solves.

```
reader already knows the term?
  no  -> definable in one plain line?
           yes -> handover
           no  -> run-up
  yes -> claim
```

## What an opening carries

A page on this site describes something the owner has used, tested or built, and the opening hands that thing to the reader. It says what the thing is, what it is for, and what the reader gets from it. The picture of the whole comes before any part. A value is introduced as a value the writer holds, and a skill by what it lets the reader do.

- Whole before parts, the overview before detail.
- Each sentence follows from the one before it.
- One job per paragraph.
- A paragraph of true facts with no single job fails as an opening.

## What fails

Most failures on record come from one day of drafting, 2026-08-13, when the owner struck eighteen openings. Each failure has a plain cause. Writing to a new ban fixed one failure and produced the next. A failed opening therefore gets written again from what the reader needs, and patching it against a ban produces the next failure.

- Machinery first: the page's own analysis before the everyday situation.
- Part first: the sharpest point before the whole.
- Announcement: a label such as "The problem first:" in front.
- Too big a step: a dense claim in sentence one, however short.
- Quote-card line: a sentence built to be quoted.
- Negative framing: a value shown only through what goes wrong without it.
- Inventory: after a good first sentence, a list of the page's parts.
