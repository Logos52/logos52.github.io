# Opening Doors

A door is the shape of a page's entire opening paragraph, decided before any sentence in it is written. Two are in use. The definitional handover names what the thing is, shows the machinery, and closes on what holding it gives you. The payoff-or-ramp names what the reader gets — or eases them onto ground until that can be named — and then hands over the method that produces it. Sentence one still takes a move from [[wiki/Writing Craft/Opening Moves Catalog|the catalog]]; the door decides what the rest of the paragraph does with it.

The two layers fail differently, which is why they are written down separately. A move fails inside one sentence: the wrong complement, a part in the subject seat, a definition the reader already owned. A door fails across the paragraph, most often by easing a reader in and then spending the ground all at once. Nothing in a first-sentence catalog can name that fault, because sentence one was fine.

## The definitional handover

**What it is, then the machinery, then what it buys.** The term arrives first and stays the subject; the middle of the paragraph shows the parts that make it work; the close says what the reader has once they hold it. Nothing is withheld and nothing is previewed.

It fits where the definition is itself the payload — a house coinage, a technical name, a term whose real content is not on its surface — and it is the default for a value, which gets introduced with the dignity of something held rather than through the trouble it repairs.

```text
Good faith is a commitment you carry into every dealing that matters: the other person
gets the true picture from you — your real constraints, your real intentions, the
downsides you could have sat on — even when telling them costs you something now. The
cost is the working part: anyone can say cooperative things, and a disclosure that cost
you something is the one the other person can actually believe.
```

```text
An exit strategy is a decided way for an object to leave — used up, returned, sold,
given, discarded — named at the door, before the object gets to stay. A sale carries a
deadline with it: two weeks, even when the money is small, because chasing a small
recovery is exactly how a sold-someday chair spends a month in the hallway.
```

## The payoff-or-ramp

**What the reader gets, then the method that gets it.** Two entrances live under one door because they answer the same question at different distances. Where the payoff can be stated cold, it opens the paragraph and the method follows. Where it cannot — because the subject has no name yet, or because a cold statement of it would be a step the reader cannot take — the paragraph starts on wide familiar ground and walks to it.

It fits where the reader already owns the term, so a definition would deliver nothing, and where a skill is better introduced by what it lets someone do than by what it is.

```text
There is a usable estimate inside almost any messy choice, and this page is how to get it
out. The exact version needs posted odds — chance times size on each side, read the sign —
and the choices that matter almost never post them.
```

```text
Most of being considerate is easy to see. Take up less space, make less noise, leave
things cleaner than you found them — nobody needs a page for that part. Speaking is the
part that is hard to see.
```

The ramp form is governed by the on-ramp law: bird's-eye first, each sentence a small step, the thesis arriving only once there is ground to hold it. Step height is density rather than word count.

## Choosing between them

One question routes most pages: **does the reader already own the term?** Where they do not, the definition is the payload and the handover takes it. Where they do, a definition spends sentence one on nothing, and the door opens on what the thing gives instead. Half-owned terms take either, and the definition has to work harder to pay.

Two overrides sit on top of that. A page carrying a core value takes the handover with a run-up, whatever the ownership answer. A page whose subject has no nameable referent at all takes the ramp, because there is nothing for a handover to hand over until the ground exists.

## Where doors fail

**The inventory.** The opening eases the reader in and then delivers the page's parts as a list. This is the recorded door-level fault, struck 2026-08-15: an opening that had handed over a system's one requirement then compressed its five stages into a single clause. Sentence one was accepted; the paragraph broke two sentences later. A multi-item enumeration is the steepest step available, because every item is a claim arriving with nothing under it. Parts belong to the body — the door carries what the thing is, what it is for, and what it gives.

**The procedure recital.** The same fault at walking pace: the paragraph goes through the method in order instead of handing over what the method produces. It appears wherever a bank supplies a staged loop, and it takes both doors at once when it does.

**The synonym swap.** Two doors written for the same page have to be different entrances rather than the same paragraph reworded. A pair that differs only in its first clause gives the reader of the slate nothing to choose between.

**The register break.** A door in the handover voice stapled to a body of stacked verdicts. The stance that produced the opening governs every section under it, not the opening alone.

## Provenance

Derived rather than designed. The two doors were named in the regeneration handoffs of 2026-08-14 and put to work immediately: 49 openings written to them across three writers, with the owner picking one per page. The accepted set is what the specimens above are drawn from, and the failure list is what the struck set produced. Both doors descend from the same write-act — every page is something its owner holds, handed to a reader he respects — which is why each of them closes on what the reader gets rather than on what the page contains.
