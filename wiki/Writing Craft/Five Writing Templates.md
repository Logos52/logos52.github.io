# Five Writing Templates

A writing template is a fixed order for the sentences of a section or a whole page. The five here come from five habits of clear speaking: say the conclusion first, give observations before conclusions, restate the question, say what would change your mind, and compress the point before saying it. Each one also works as a check on the writer, because it cannot be filled in without doing the thinking it asks for.

## Takeaways

- Each template fits a single section or a whole page.
- To put the conclusion first, you must know it.
- To give observations first, you must have some.
- To restate the question, you must have understood it.
- To name what would change your mind, you must have looked.
- To compress, you must know which parts are needed.
- Choose a template by what you already have when you start writing.

## The page shape they fit

Pages here follow one shape: the whole subject in brief, then its parts, then the whole again. The closing whole restates the opening one and adds what the parts showed. Starting at the end and restating the question fill the opening, observations fill the parts, and what would change your mind fills the close. Compression usually stands apart as a page of its own.

```
opening whole  <- start at the end
               <- restate the question
parts          <- observations, then meaning
closing whole  <- what would change the conclusion

apart          <- compress it first
```

## The five templates

Each template has a section form and a page form. The section form is a few sentences in a fixed order inside a larger page. The page form is a whole page built on the same order. A reader can tell which template a page uses from its first lines.

- Start at the end: the conclusion in sentence one, then the reasons.
  - A gap in the reasons is stated, and the conclusion still leads.
  - Page form: the claim at full strength in sentence one.
- Observations first: what was seen, then what it means.
  - Page form: a record of every instance, how many, then the change made.
- Restate the question: what is really being asked, split into its parts.
  - Page form: a routing page that sends each part of a question elsewhere.
- What would change your mind: position, case against, what flips it.
  - Page form: a position page, or a decision with what would reverse it.
- Compress it first: one or two lines that carry every important point.
  - Page form: one paragraph for the whole, then lines that stand alone.

## Why the order matters

A reader cannot hold several points at once and join them afterwards. A chain of reasons delivered before the conclusion loses the reader partway, and they judge the thinking as muddled even when it is sound. The conclusion first tells them how to read every reason that follows. Observations first let a reader reach their own view before being told one, which gets more agreement than a bare verdict.

- A reader judges the thinking by how it is presented.
- A restated question catches a misreading before the work is done.
- Naming what would reverse a decision shows the writer is open to correction.
- If a ten-year-old could not follow it, compress again.
