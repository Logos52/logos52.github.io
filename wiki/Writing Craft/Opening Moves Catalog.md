# Opening Moves Catalog

An opening move is a way to write the first sentence of a page. The catalog lists nineteen of them. Fourteen come from writers whose openings were studied line by line: Piotr Woźniak, Gwern Branwen, Paul Graham, Naval Ravikant, Maciej Cegłowski and Scott Alexander. The last five come from rulings by this wiki's owner on openings he struck. A writer who knows the options can pick the one that fits the page instead of reaching for a hook.

## Core takeaways

- Say the most important sentence first.
- A page's type sets the shape of its opening.
- The first sentence says something, and does not announce what is coming.
- It should be worth reading if it were the only sentence.
- Main point first is the default, and a page can depart from it.
- A heavy or abstract subject gets a run-up instead.
- When unsure, use the run-up.

## How to choose

Start from what kind of page it is. A glossary entry opens on a definition, an essay opens on its conclusion, and a page that answers a request opens on the request and the shape of the answer. Then ask whether sentence one can say what the thing is in words the reader already has. If it can, use a direct move, and if it cannot, use a run-up move from the end of the list.

- Gwern's rule: every essay begins with a summary of its findings.
- Once the reader has the conclusion, a hook has nothing left to do.
- A complicated point opens on one or more concrete examples.

## The moves

Each move below is a way to spend sentence one. The first fourteen state something directly. They differ in what they state, which can be a definition, a fact, a claim, someone else's position, or the terms of the page itself.

- 1. Definition: the term, "is", its category, what it does.
- 2. Dated fact: a real thing with its date and size.
- 3. Plain causal claim: what happens, and the condition that causes it.
- 4. Attributed folklore: who holds a sweeping claim, then test it.
- 5. Opposing view at full strength, then plain disagreement.
- 6. Retell the object fairly, then name its one problem.
- 7. Debt: a claim already made, and the explanation it still needs.
- 8. Definition stack: define every working term before any claim.
- 9. Unhedged claim, with its limits in the next sentence.
- 10. Preamble: what kind of material this is, and how settled.
- 11. Own practice: what the writer does, as plain fact.
- 12. Real question, answered in the same or next sentence.
- 13. Scope fence: set aside nearby subjects, then name this one.
- 14. Occasion and deliverable: why this exists, and what it hands over.

## The run-up moves

The run-up moves came out of one day, 2026-08-13, after five openings on one page were struck as too heavy to start. The owner's rulings that day gave most of them, for a subject that cannot be defined in its first line. The remaining move, with a test for spotting an opening that is too heavy, was worked out the same day from the struck openings. These moves give the reader familiar ground before the subject arrives.

- Define the two things the subject depends on first.
- Open on why the subject matters.
- Open on the problem the page solves.
- Priming: a wide view, easy to take in, with the subject named last.
