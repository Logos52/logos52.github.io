# Owner Writing Samples

This file keeps text that the owner wrote himself, word for word, so that a writer can read how he explains things before writing anything for him. He asked for it on 2026-09-14: "i want to keep my writing available for you to read as like samples." Each sample says where it was used. The samples are kept exactly as he typed them, including small typing slips. The copies on the site fix the spelling.

## Home page, Top of mind items, 2026-09-14

He wrote these four after two rounds of lines written by Claude did not work. His words: "better for me to write them myself."

1. An AI model purposefully misunderstands what you ask it to do in order to "reward hack" a simpler solution that technically answers what you want, but using the absolute shortest path possible, in order to evade fully executing on your entire idea
2. In many stories, any good protagonist is a character who lives by a moral code. Later in that story, something tests that code. Do all stories people love happen to work out like that?
3. Pages about exercise, clothing, and travel. These are more about my preferences as a person, so they skew a bit more toward my personal sense of taste.
4. How do you determine the validity of personal beliefs? Analyzing the logical structure and second order effects of holding certain political opinions. These pages skew toward logic, which is my personal version of centrist arguments, which many people consider biased. If you want to avoid being triggered best to keep away from these pages. It's the interent so someone out there will definitely be offended with something. But I enjoy these topics and avoiding them in my opinion is a detriment to our society.

All four are on the home page word for word, with "interent" spelled "internet" (commit 0c218ef).

## Home page, Start here card meanings, 2026-09-14

Claude wrote a plain meaning for each of the five cards, and he edited each meaning into the text below. Each card on the site uses a short title and one or two of his sentences, because a card has room for little more.

1. Understand card: Before AI agents, an engineer was judged on the work the engineer delivered. Now AI agents write much of the code, so an engineer is judged on the setup the engineer builds.
2. Apply card: Vibe coding means asking an AI agent for an app in everyday language. The agent writes the code and runs it. Soon all coding will become vibe coding.
3. Learn card: A study technique, such as taking notes or drawing a mind map, is useful only if it makes you process the material in your mind. You can take notes but if you do not process anything in your mind, it has no effect. To check a technique, check the level of your processing ability while using it. If it did not, it needs adjustment.
4. Decode card: This card links to a page about how Chinese characters are built. Each character is made of smaller parts, and each part has a different functions.
5. Explore card: Every node on this site is an article on the website, with a line between two nodes indicating a relationship. Under the drawing, the Map page lists six subject node clusters. Some nodes are a hubs of content, linking to many nodes related to that one subject.

## Home page, Project Updates summaries, 2026-09-14

Claude wrote a plain meaning for each of the ten summaries, and the owner edited them. His edits cut the last sentence of most of them. His words on the pattern: "you have a weird tendency to trail off your last sentence fragment with some kind of weird gibberish". The text below is on the home page word for word. Under each item is the sentence he cut, kept as a sample of what not to write.

1. The Floor Rose, the Ceiling Is the Job: This is a journal entry about vibe coding. Anyone can now describe an app to an AI agent and get a working app the same afternoon. Someone still has to decide whether the app is correct, safe and worth building.
   Cut: That decision takes a person who knows the work.

2. Doctrine With a Half-Life: This is a journal entry about notes on AI. Advice about AI models goes out of date fast, because each new model changes what works. The entry splits notes about AI into two parts. One part holds ideas that stay true, such as the work people still need to do themselves.
   Cut: The other part holds tips for today's models, and each tip has a date.

3. Automation and the Job Iceberg: In the U.S. census record, machines fully replaced only one job: the elevator operator, last counted around 1960. Most other jobs changed instead of disappearing.
   Cut: A job survives when it can take on the extra work and responsibility that cheaper tasks create.

4. Mindset, Condensed: This page is a short list of rules about mindset. Treat a hard task as a reason to keep working. Treat a mistake as the way you improve.
   Cut: Use motivation to start a habit, and then keep the habit going without waiting to feel like it.

5. Usable Intelligence: Sonnet 5 vs Opus 4.8: This is a journal entry that compares two AI models for one job. The job is writing lessons that must pass an automatic word check. Sonnet 5 passed the check about as often as Opus 4.8, and Sonnet 5 cost 0.6 times as much. So Sonnet 5 became the main writer.
   Cut: Opus 4.8 plans the work and handles what the check cannot verify.

6. Human vs AI Capability Lens: This page is a way to score any skill. Each skill gets two separate scores. One score says how much the skill needs a person. The other score says how much an AI can do it. The two scores together put the skill in one of four groups: Own, Augment, Delegate, or Low-leverage.
   His word: "um actually that one is fine."

7. Design Two-Track Extraction: This page is a method for sorting design techniques from two design books. Each technique gets one question: can an AI agent follow the technique as a fixed rule? The techniques that pass became a list of rules for agents.
   Cut: The rest became a list of judgment calls for people.

8. Story Cards as Memory Scenes: This is a journal entry about the Tsumugu Encoding Dictionary. Every character in the dictionary has two cards. The Form card shows the character's parts and what each part does. The Story card is a short scene that helps you remember the character.
   Cut: After a review of the first 500 characters, the Story card was changed to cover only what the Form card cannot explain.

9. The First Principles of the Story Card: This is a journal entry about how to build a Story card. The rules for a good memory scene come down to two lists. One list has situations a character could be in, the other has memory techniques. Each story card combines one item from each list.
   Replaced: "One list has 30 situations a character can be in. The other list has 42 memory techniques. Every Story card combines one item from each list."

10. The Story-Card Catalog: This is a journal entry that lists the kinds of Story card. The 30 situations and the 42 memory techniques combine into 34 kinds of card.
   Cut by Claude on the same pattern, not named by the owner: "A future AI agent that writes cards picks from these 34 kinds and can add new ones."
