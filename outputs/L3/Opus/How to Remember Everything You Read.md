# Opus - How to Remember Everything You Read

## Core Thesis

Reading produces durable, usable knowledge when it's paired with deliberate digestion. Digestion is the process that converts what was read into long-term memory and understanding — and when it keeps pace with reading, retention is high and knowledge becomes flexible: retrievable in context, applicable to new problems, connectable to other ideas. The goal is knowledge you can think with.

The system that structures digestion is **PACER**: a five-type classification that pairs each kind of information with its own targeted process. Procedural information is digested through early practice. Analogous information is digested by critiquing the analogy — finding where it holds, where it breaks, whether a better one exists. Conceptual information is digested through nonlinear mapping. Evidence is stored during reading and rehearsed later through application. Reference is stored and rehearsed via spaced repetition. Match the process to the type, keep reading and digestion proportional, and retention becomes a structural outcome rather than a function of how hard you tried to memorize.

## Compressed Takeaways

1. **Consumption and digestion must stay balanced.** Overconsumption without digestion produces forgetting — up to 90% of what's read. Slow consumption to keep digestion possible.
2. **The goal is remembering what you'll use, in a usable form.** Perfect recall loses to high-order reasoning every time at adult-stakes tasks.
3. **PACER classifies information; each type has a targeted digestion process.** Using the wrong process wastes time and reduces retention.
4. **Procedural → practice early.** Apply as soon as possible. Don't memorize procedure — build the embodied version.
5. **Analogous → critique the analogy.** Find what's similar, what's different, where it breaks. The critique is the deep processing.
6. **Conceptual → map nonlinearly.** Concepts live in networks; replicate the network, don't memorize the linearization.
7. **Evidence → store and rehearse via application.** Note it during consumption; use it to support and prove concepts during rehearsal.
8. **Reference → store and rehearse via spaced repetition.** Pure recall info belongs in flashcards, not in your mapping time.
9. **Don't try to memorize during consumption.** Reading and rereading to memorize is the most common time waste. Each information type has a later, targeted process for retention.
10. **If you can't digest now, slow down.** Less consumption beats overconsume-and-forget. Every unit consumed without digestion is future-debt paid with interest.

## The PACER Decision Tree

The system runs in two stages. Classification happens at consumption time; digestion happens shortly after.

**Stage 1 — Consume.** As you read or listen, classify each piece of information:

| Type | What it looks like | Digestion process |
|---|---|---|
| **P**rocedural | How to do something — steps, techniques, workflows | Apply / practice early in real context |
| **A**nalogous | Reminds you of something you already know | Critique the analogy — similarities, differences, breakdown points |
| **C**onceptual | Facts, theories, principles, relationships | Nonlinear mapping — chunk map / mind map |
| **E**vidence | Specific examples, dates, statistics, cases that prove a concept | Store now, rehearse later via application |
| **R**eference | Pure detail (constants, names, lookup info) | Store now, rehearse via spaced repetition (Anki) |

**Stage 2 — Digest.** Apply the targeted process for each type. This is where retention is built. Without it, consumption goes to waste.

The key claim: there is no shortcut. Trying to memorize evidence or reference info *during* reading is what eats the time needed for proper conceptual mapping. The order matters. Conceptual mapping during reading; evidence and reference rehearsal *after*.

## Procedural: Practice As Soon As Possible

Procedural information — how to execute a technique, follow a workflow, apply a method — is retained best through embodied practice, not memorization. The earlier the practice attempt, the more of the initial learning carries through. Wait two weeks and most of it is gone.

The constraint this creates is real: if there's no time to practice right now, the right move is either to move on to something else or to stop consuming and wait. Trying to memorize procedure on the spot is the lowest-yield digestion possible for that information type. The only memory worth building for procedural content is the one built through doing.

Each unit of procedural information consumed without a practice attempt is future-debt. The consumption-digestion balance applies here as forcefully as anywhere.

## Analogous: The Critique Is the Work

Analogous information is any information that connects to something already known — whether from outside the domain or from within it. A new problem-solving approach that echoes an old one is analogous. A physiological mechanism that resembles a familiar physical process is analogous.

Most readers generate the analogy and stop. That's where the digestion actually starts. Critiquing the analogy means asking: in what ways are these two things specifically similar? Where do they diverge? Under what conditions does the analogy break down? Is there a better one? The critique forces attention to structure and constraint rather than surface similarity. It pins new information to existing prior knowledge with explicit connections — which is what makes the encoding durable.

The analogy can be added to a conceptual map or used to inform how to structure one. Either way, the critique is the processing; the comparison alone is not.

## Conceptual: Map the Network, Not the Sequence

Conceptual information — facts, theories, principles, relationships between concepts, modes of application — forms the bulk of most learning. A textbook delivers it linearly because communication requires sequence. But the expert's knowledge is not linear. It's a network: any concept connected directly to several others, navigable from any entry point, enabling complex problem-solving because relationships are visible.

The digestion process for conceptual information is nonlinear mapping — building a structure that reflects the network rather than copying the sequence. The map grows during reading: things get added, moved, reorganized, re-clustered. Analogies discovered along the way can inform how clusters are shaped. The goal is not a pretty finished diagram but an honest representation of how the concepts actually connect.

If mapping isn't possible in the moment — no tablet, in transit, time-constrained — slow down. Consuming conceptual information without mapping it produces an obligation to reconstruct it later, which costs more time than it saved.

## Evidence vs. Reference: A Distinction Worth Keeping

Both evidence and reference are "store and rehearse" — but the rehearsal is different, and conflating them is one of the most common sources of wasted reading time.

**Evidence** supports concepts. A specific case, date, statistic, or example that makes a conceptual point concrete. It connects upward into the conceptual map and is useful for arguments, explanations, essays, and problem-solving. Rehearsal is application: use the evidence to prove the concept, write around it, teach with it.

**Reference** is pure recall. Names, constants, lookup details, specific values. It doesn't change conceptual understanding; it just needs to be reproducible on demand. Rehearsal is bare recall: spaced repetition flashcards.

Treating evidence as reference — flash-carding it in isolation — severs the conceptual connection and leaves a fact without a home. Treating reference as evidence — mapping it — wastes mapping time on information that doesn't earn structural treatment. The distinction keeps both processes running at the right cost.
