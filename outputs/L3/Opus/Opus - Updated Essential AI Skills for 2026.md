# Opus - Updated Essential AI Skills for 2026

## The Ladder, At a Glance

An update to a January 2026 AI-skills roadmap, re-cut five months later because the ground moved. The whole video is one structure — a progression from "don't get left behind" to "build things other people pay for." Read the table, then the judgment.

| Level | Frame | Skills | Who it's for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Basics** | Societal minimum | AI-aware investing thesis · prompting · tool minimalism | Everyone participating in 2026 |
| **2 — Intermediate** | Personal leverage | Working *with* agents (web) · running *local* agents | Anyone automating their own life/workflows |
| **3 — Advanced** | Commercial / career leverage | Building agents · building MCPs · AI coding (agentic engineering) | People building for others, or chasing max leverage |

## Core Thesis

The useful spine of this video isn't the tool list — tool lists rot in weeks. It's the **three-tier model of what "essential" means at different ambition levels**, and a repeated stance that runs through all three: *go deep on a few things, don't chase the firehose.* With 10+ releases a day, breadth is a trap; mastery of a small high-leverage set is the sustainable play. That single principle — tool minimalism — is the part most worth keeping.

The second durable idea is the **phase change from "tool" to "agent."** A tool is one request, one response: "write me a paragraph," "plan my itinerary." An agent takes an *overarching goal* and decomposes-then-executes the steps. Everything above Level 1 is really about climbing that gap — first using agents others built, then running them locally on your own machine and data, then building them for other people.

Treat the rest as a calibration reference: a snapshot of what mid-2026 fluency looks like, against which to audit your own map.

## Level 1 — Securing the Basics

Table-stakes for modern life, not for technologists specifically.

- **An AI-aware investing thesis.** Her contrarian opener: index funds already give you massive, *unchosen* AI exposure, because the funds are stuffed with AI companies or AI-integrated ones. So the skill is deciding your exposure *deliberately*, weighted against how AI-exposed your career already is. (She's heavily exposed via an unstable creator/founder career, so she hedges accordingly.) Flagged explicitly as *not financial advice* — the transferable bit is the framing, not the allocation.
- **Prompting.** The foundation under everything else; she points to two basic frameworks as the floor.
- **Tool minimalism.** Master one general chatbot (Claude / ChatGPT / Gemini / Grok / Poe) deeply — modern chatbots already plan trips, generate media, and prototype apps. Optionally add a specialised research/news tool (Perplexity), a learning tool (NotebookLM), and one or two job-specific tools (e.g. Jasper / Surfer for marketing; a coding tool for engineers).

**Operating note from the source:** *one well-mastered chatbot + solid prompting* is presented as enough to carry the majority of people a very long way. Don't over-build before you've exhausted that.

## Level 2 — Working With Agents

The intermediate jump, and the one with the highest personal payoff.

- **Web-based agents** (her example: Manus decorating an office — brainstorming aesthetics, then sourcing the actual rug/lamp/curtains). Good entry point for learning to *direct* an agent toward a goal.
- **Local AI agents** — agents that run on *your* machine. This is the high-leverage category, because they can touch local files, email, calendar, and notes without shipping sensitive data to a cloud provider. The named examples — **Cowork, OpenClaw, Hermes** — are exactly the layer this synthesis is being produced in.

What local agents unlock (her examples): a daily digest pulling calendar + Gmail + investments + Slack + Notion into Apple Notes; an AI-news tracker that deep-dives topics and drafts video scripts; a custom investments dashboard.

**Two decision factors for picking one:**

```
                     CLOSED SOURCE                 OPEN SOURCE
                  (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini)     (Kimi, MiniMax, Llama)
                  ───────────────────────────   ───────────────────────────
  more capable, gap closing                     cheaper / free to self-host
  data goes to provider                         private if you run it yourself

  NON-TECHNICAL ─→ no-code local agent, closed model = fastest path
  CODE-CAPABLE  ─→ self-host open model = max control, cost, privacy
```

Axis one: how technical you are (no-code vs. code-capable). Axis two: open vs. closed source — the trade is capability (closed, currently ahead but narrowing) against cost and privacy (open, cheap/free and yours).

## Level 3 — Building & AI Coding

For people building things others will use, or who want maximum leverage.

- **Building your own agents.** Commercial pipelines need to be stable, reliable, and low-cost. Her firm's B2B examples: a private-equity reporting pipeline that pulls CRM + analytics + spreadsheets + a custom DB on a schedule and assembles a formatted report; onboarding agents that walk new hires/clients through custom, tracked steps. High, durable demand.
- **Building MCPs.** Connectors that let agents plug into third-party apps and data sources. Called out as independently in-demand.
- **AI coding / agentic engineering.** Using agents to write production software. She claims 10x+ time and cost savings once competent — the single domain AI has already *transformed*.

**The caveat that matters most** — *"lipstick on a pig."* AI coding still requires you to actually know how to code. Bad code + great AI tooling = bad results that fall apart downstream. Every other skill on this ladder is learnable in days-to-weeks; real coding (then AI coding on top) is a 2–3 month commitment. She positions it deliberately last: you can get genuinely far without it, so only commit if you're going deep on AI — or want to clone your own SaaS tools and kill subscriptions.

> Sponsor flag: the "advanced" segment opens with a paid Bolt placement (rapid web-app generation, MCP connector layer). It's ad content, not an independent skill recommendation — discounted accordingly here.

## Operating Mechanism — Audit Your Own Skill Map

A runnable procedure rather than a list to admire. Walk the ladder against your actual position.

1. **Place yourself.** Which level is your *current* working ceiling — 1, 2, or 3? Be honest about where you operate daily, not where you've dabbled.
2. **Level 1 sweep.** Do you have a deliberate AI investing thesis? A prompting baseline? A *named, short* core tool set you've actually mastered — or a sprawl you chase? Cut the sprawl before adding anything.
3. **Find the next rung, not the top one.** If you're solid at L1, the highest-ROI move is a local-agent workflow — not jumping to building MCPs.
4. **Pick one workflow to automate.** Choose a single recurring task (digest, research tracker, dashboard) and assign it to a local agent you already run.
5. **Resolve the two axes once.** Decide your default: technical vs. no-code, open vs. closed — so you stop re-litigating it per project.
6. **Gate Level 3 behind a real reason.** Only invest the 2–3 month coding commitment if you're going deep commercially or replacing paid SaaS with your own builds. Otherwise stay at L2 and compound.
7. **Re-audit on a cadence.** Her own roadmap needed a rewrite in five months. Treat any AI skill map as perishable; date it and revisit.

## Where Wedge Already Sits

- **The local-agent layer isn't aspirational for you — it's your stack.** Cowork and Hermes are named in the video as the Level 2 category. You're not "learning to use" local agents; you're operating two of them, which puts your *floor* at L2.
- **Tool minimalism is already your stated philosophy** — this is external corroboration, not new instruction. The risk it flags for you is sprawl across Cowork / Grok Build / Codex / Hermes; the discipline is mastering the few, not collecting more.
- **Agentic engineering (L3) is a Priority-0 skill for you**, where the video stays shallow. The "lipstick on a pig" caveat is the honest part worth keeping; the 10x claim is marketing-adjacent and should be weighed against your real experience shipping `cos` and wnab.
- **The investing-thesis frame** maps onto wnab and the finances domain — worth a one-time deliberate pass even if you discard her allocation stance.

## Open Questions / Tensions

- How mature is the "local agent" category for *low-maintenance* daily use versus still needing babysitting? Your lived experience with Hermes/Cowork is the better data than her highlight reel.
- Where does "building MCPs" actually sit on your skill map relative to pure agentic engineering — adjacent skill, or subset?
- Does the 10x coding claim survive contact with your real build history, or is it survivorship-flavoured?
- Is there anything on this ladder you *haven't* already cleared? If your floor is L2 and your Priority-0 is L3, the video may function more as a teaching/onboarding artifact for others than as a personal roadmap — worth deciding which.
