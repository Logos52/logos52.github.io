# Opus - It Took Me 30+ Years (Chamath on Success & Focus)

Spend time and money to remove friction from the few things you care about, and refuse the rest, because possessions you don't need invert into things that own your attention. The working definition of success that falls out: work that energizes rather than depletes — a day where the 9-to-5 makes you a better person for the 5-to-9, not one you have to recover from.

## The possessions trap

The mechanism is compounding overhead from a single purchase. One mechanical watch becomes three; the unworn ones need a winder; the winder fails, so you need a jeweler; insurance rises until you store them off-site — a chain of minutiae spawned by an object you bought for pleasure, now spending your attention. The principle: the more you buy, the more those things own you, and the time goes to maintenance instead of making. This is [[wiki/Minimalism/Ownership Cost|ownership cost]] told as a personal failure mode.

The status-symbol version is the same trap socially. The trappings of success have detached from success itself ("all on Instagram with a bunch of mids"); living by other people's definition of success is the distraction. The carve-out he keeps: money spent to buy *time* and remove friction so you can focus on the thing you're excellent at is the opposite of the trap — that's the goat using money to protect attention, not to signal.

## Distraction lowers performance, directly

The causal claim he draws from his own arc: "the more distracted I got, the less successful I became, the more mistakes I made." Divesting the plane, the team ownership, the watches redirected energy and capital into the few things he cared about (making things), and the byproduct was both more success and more happiness. The leverage point is attention, not asceticism.

## Failure is architected in the prior success

The sharpest idea: success and failure are sisters, and the failure is architected by how you perceived yourself during the success. His worked example is the SPAC era: the strategy (route private companies around a bank oligopoly to public markets, the way startups went public faster and cheaper in the '80s–'90s) was sound, but the *results* were amplified by post-COVID free money, and he misread a macro moment as personal skill. The cost: too much personal risk ($100M checks into deals in sectors he shouldn't have touched), and an ego inflated by a million-plus-follower audience he took too seriously. The repair he names: when something works, ask whether it's you or the moment — and when in doubt, keep your head down and say less.

## Find the work that energizes you

The positive program has two parts. First, know enough of your true self to recognize what you'd do for hours and lose track of time — most people are buried under others' stories about what they're good at (a rigid school system, a degree chosen for the wrong reasons). His own constant thread: games, risk, and problem-solving (blackjack → poker → company-building, all "unlock the next level"). Second, apply the energize test as a litmus: at day's end, did the work deplete you (so 5-to-11 is yelling and doom-scrolling) or empower you (so you show up to family as your full self)?

Success itself he reframes as a lagging indicator: becoming a billionaire just means the field you loved happened to be economically rewarded *this* cycle, and that ebbs (a teacher could become a multimillionaire "super teacher" in an AI future; an AI scientist's path could vanish to software). So you can't time or optimize for the money; optimize for what makes you happy, and the rest is byproduct. Success is not zero-sum — everyone can have it without taking from anyone.

## The case against

The advice is delivered from survivorship: a billionaire who already bought the plane and the team telling you possessions don't matter is easy to say from the far side. The carve-outs make it slippery — "Michael Jordan flies private, but he's a goat" lets any purchase be justified by self-declared excellence, which quietly readmits the status game it condemns. And the core instruction, "find your true self and the work that energizes you," is underspecified for anyone trapped by obligations, debt, or a degree already sunk — which the source admits ("I don't claim to give you a simple answer"). The "it's just a byproduct" framing also understates that economic security is a precondition for treating money as optional.

## Price the method

Divesting isn't free: selling down assets, roles, and identity has real switching costs and social friction, and "redirect energy to what you care about" assumes you have the runway to choose. The energize test is cheap to run but expensive to act on if the depleting 9-to-5 is the one paying the bills.

## Checkable expectations

- Run the energize test for two weeks: at each day's end, did the day's work make you better company that evening, or did it require recovery? A persistent "recovery" answer means the work, not your willpower, is the problem.
- Audit one recent "success": list how much was your decision and how much was the moment (market, timing, free money). If you can't separate them, you're exposed to the same ego trap on the next upswing.

## Boundaries

Minimalism here is attention-protection, not deprivation — "not living in a shack and driving a $2,000 car." The claim is about removing friction from what matters, not owning as little as possible. Distinguish buying-to-signal (the trap) from buying-to-buy-time (the tool).
