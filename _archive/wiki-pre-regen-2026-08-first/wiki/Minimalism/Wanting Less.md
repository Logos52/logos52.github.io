# Wanting Less

Sasaki writes that we use objects to tell people how valuable we are. The same broadcast runs at the self. Naming what the object is supposed to say, and to whom, often ends the impulse.

## The question

Naming the motive at the moment of wanting converts an impulse into a legible decision. The question is: what is this purchase saying, and to whom?

Seeing the motive does not cancel spending by magic and does not replace all discipline. Naming turns an impulse into a decision. The want often loses its charge. Often is not a spell. A jacket that still looks good after the question is named is not a failed test.

The signalling purchase fails on its own terms. The audience mostly isn't watching. The self the object was supposed to certify still has to be built by doing, not owning.

## What a non-purchase skips

Every object not acquired skips the entire downstream tax that [[wiki/Minimalism/Ownership Cost|Ownership Cost]] itemizes — space, attention, maintenance, exit.

Wanting less converts directly into money, time, and mobility. That conversion is why the move matters; it is not the move.

This is the demand side of [[wiki/Money/The Savings Rate Is the Master Lever|the savings rate]]: the gap widens at the want, before the budget sees the transaction.

Wanting less is not anti-spending. Purchases that pass [[wiki/Money/Money as Life Energy|the life-energy test]] — fulfilment proportional to hours traded — are the point of having money. The boundary is motive, not amount.

The check is the next non-routine purchase impulse, with the signal named before the decision, and a count of how often the naming ends the wanting. The want is the cheapest place to cut.
