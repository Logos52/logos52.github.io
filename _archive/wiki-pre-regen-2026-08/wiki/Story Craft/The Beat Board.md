# The Beat Board - One Beat per Card, and the Board That Stays True

## Core Thesis

A beat board holds a story as physical structure, and it works only while two disciplines hold: each beat compressed onto a card small enough to move, and the board kept true to the manuscript, so that the board stays the single source of truth about what the story is. The compression is itself the tool — Vince Gilligan's board was a 3'x5' corkboard, 3x5 index cards, and Sharpies, on the theory that forcing a scene down to card size is what produces the breakthroughs — and the movability is protected by writing rules: around seven words, labels generic enough to survive a reorder, cards cheap enough to rewrite rather than amend. A board built that way pays back a set of tests readable at a glance: the connective between adjacent beats must read "but" or "therefore," every unit must turn a value from one polarity to its opposite at a nameable turning point, every card must survive being pulled from the sequence, and each unit hands a named object forward for the next to receive. Every one of those tests reads the board, and drafting moves the manuscript away from the board a little every session — so the second discipline is a sync: bind the card to the document it stands for, or reverse-outline what was actually written and diff it against the plan. A board that skips the sync goes on describing the pre-draft intention, and every diagnostic run on it reads a story that no longer exists.

## Compressed Takeaways

- One beat per physical card, hand-written, compressed to a logline. The compression is the tool, and card size is what forces it.
- Around seven words per card, labeled generically so the card can move; the whole story in under about fifty cards; rewrite rather than amend.
- Two tiers are legal on one board — a season row above episode beats — and so is a placeholder: a named-but-unbuilt beat is a legitimate board object.
- Write the connective between adjacent beats: "but" or "therefore," never "and then." A beat that only accepts "and then" gets cut or rebuilt as a consequence of the one before.
- Every unit turns a value: polarity at open and polarity at close must be opposites, at a discrete turning point, or the unit is not a scene.
- Pull each card and read the sequence without it — cull any card the story survives losing; then reorder the survivors until each card is the consequence of the one before.
- The handoff can be explicit: a Sequel's Decision becomes the next Scene's Goal, so each card names what it passes forward.
- Expansion runs at a fixed ratio — every unit at one level becomes one unit of the next size down.
- While drafting, the board stays authoritative only through sync: card bound to document, or a reverse outline after each session, diffed against the plan.

## The card

The first discipline starts at the unit: one beat on one physical card, hand-written, forced down to a logline. The Breaking Bad room ran it with a 3'x5' corkboard, 3x5 index cards, and Sharpies — Gilligan refused a whiteboard — on the theory that the squeeze down to card size is where the breakthroughs happen. The card is a compression device before it is a storage device, and the claim is checkable in the first transfer session: forcing existing beats down to card size should change some of them in the writing. A story whose every beat survives at seven words unaltered is only being stored; the compression is doing no work there.

John August's card-writing rules exist to keep the rearranging possible, which is the board's whole advantage over a prose outline. A card carries at most about seven words. Its label is generic — "Battle in swamp," never "Final showdown" — because a card named for its position in the sequence has hard-coded its own slot and can no longer move. The whole story fits in under about fifty cards. And a card is cheap: when a beat changes, the card is rewritten, never amended, so every card on the board stays a clean logline.

The physical setup is part of the method. August lays cards horizontally on a table rather than pinning them to a wall: the pin/unpin friction of a vertical board suppresses reordering.

## The board

Cards that small keep the whole story in one view, and the view can hold more than one scale: a board can run two tiers at once. The Breaking Bad board carried thirteen cards across the top — thirteen episodes, the season as a row — with each episode's beats pinned beneath its card. And a tier can hold a promise it has not yet earned: under episode 413 sat a single card reading "BOOM." A named-but-unbuilt beat is a legitimate board object; the placeholder reserves the slot while everything around it gets built.

The counts on record are single-work figures. Blake Snyder's Save the Cat board is exactly forty cards in four rows of ten — Act 1, 2A, 2B, 3 — mapped to his fifteen beats; each card carries a location header, a one-line description, a +/- pair marking the scene's opening and closing emotional charge, and angle-bracket marks for its conflict. Alexandra Sokoloff's port of the board to novels runs forty to sixty scene cards in four columns, the board split into eight sections. August's feature ceiling is under about fifty. Syd Field puts one scene on each 3x5 card and names no total.

Two overlays make the board diagnostic at a glance, no card lifted. Character initials in the corner of each card let a scan show who has dropped out of an episode. A second card color, reserved for one category worth pacing — August uses action — makes that category's distribution visible as a pattern across the whole board.

## The seams: but and therefore

The first test a true board pays back is causality, read between adjacent cards, with the connective written down where the seam is. Trey Parker and Matt Stone map three acts across one wall, fill in the beats, then read the joints: "if the words 'and then' belong between those beats, you're f---ed." Stone's positive form: "'but,' 'because,' 'therefore' — that gives you the causation between each beat, and that's a story."

Writing the connective between the cards turns the seam into a board object with its own pass/fail state. The repair rule is mechanical: any beat that only accepts "and then" is cut, or rebuilt until it is a consequence of the beat before it. The yield is checkable on the first full read of an existing outline: connectives written at every seam should surface joints the prose had smoothed over into "and then"; a board where every seam already takes "but" or "therefore" is reporting that this work was done before the cards went up.

## The turn

The second test is internal to the unit, and it is Robert McKee's rule: a scene turns a value from positive to negative or negative to positive at a discrete turning point, or it is not a scene. Story Grid operationalizes the rule as spreadsheet columns — the value in play, polarity at open, polarity at close, the exact turning point — with the enforcement stated flat: "If there's no turning point, there's no value shift, and if there's no value shift, you haven't written a scene."

The same test appears on the card face and on the wall. Snyder's +/- pair is the per-card form: opening and closing charge, required to be opposites. The Foolscap variant is the whole-board form: each scene's one-line event plotted above or below an x-axis by polarity, so the board reads as a zigzag.

## The cull and the sort

The third test judges whole cards, and Field's sequence runs it as two operations in strict order. First the cull: pull each card and read the sequence without it — a card the story survives losing is cut, whatever it cost to write. Then the reorder: the survivors are rearranged until each card reads as the consequence of the one before it, no card movable without breaking the chain. Necessity before order — a beautiful arrangement of unnecessary cards is still unnecessary.

The sorting itself has mechanics, imported from UX card-sorting research at Nielsen Norman Group: thirty to fifty cards, one item per card, and never duplicate wording across cards, because people who see matching labels sort by matching the words instead of by the relationship underneath. The research names two modes. An open sort lets the sorter invent and name the groups, which discovers the mental model in the material; a closed sort files cards into fixed categories, which validates a structure already chosen. Carried over to beats, the two modes are both useful and do different work: loose scene cards sorted open discover the story's own shape, while dealing the same cards into a pre-printed grid — Snyder's four rows, say — is a closed sort, checking the story against a structure decided in advance.

## The handoff

The fourth test reads the seam from inside the units: where the connectives find dependency after the fact, the scene-sequel chain builds it in. In Dwight Swain and Jack Bickham's system — as taught by September C. Fawkes and by Randy Ingermanson in *Writing the Perfect Scene* — Motivation-Reaction Units chain into Scenes, each shaped Goal → Conflict → Disaster, and Scenes chain into Sequels, shaped Reaction → Dilemma → Decision. The Decision then becomes the next Scene's Goal.

On the board, the chain's last link means each unit's output is the next unit's input, written down. A card that names its Decision has declared exactly what the following card must receive, so the dependency between two adjacent cards is a named object rather than an inference — the "and then" failure cannot hide between them.

## Growing the board

Compression sets up expansion: the board is one level of a ladder, and Randy Ingermanson's Snowflake Method states the ladder's ratio outright — every unit at one level becomes one unit of the next size at the level below. His ten steps, with his own time budgets: a one-sentence summary (an hour), a paragraph of about five sentences (an hour), one-page character summaries (about an hour each), each sentence of the paragraph expanded into its own paragraph for a one-page synopsis (several hours), character synopses (one to two days), each paragraph expanded to a full page for a four-page synopsis (a week), character bibles (a week), a scene-list spreadsheet with one row per scene (up to a week), optional one-to-two-page scene descriptions, and the draft, written off the list.

The budgets are the ladder's price, stated by its own author: the middle steps run a week each, so the ladder costs weeks of structure work before a line of the draft exists. What the price buys is that no level is invented from nothing — a logline card is the compressed form of the paragraph it will become, and the scene row is the compressed form of the scene.

## Keeping the board authoritative

The second discipline answers a decay the first cannot prevent. Every test the board pays back — the seams, the polarity marks, the cull, the handoff — reads the board, and drafting moves the story away from the board a little every session. Unsynced, the board goes on describing the pre-draft intention while the manuscript becomes something else, and the diagnostics keep returning confident readings of a story that no longer exists. Story Grid's rule names the repair in one clause: the spreadsheet is updated after each session so it describes the manuscript, not the pre-draft intention.

Two sync mechanics are on record. Scrivener removes the sync step for order by binding each card to the actual document: dragging a card in the Corkboard reorders the manuscript binder itself, so card order and manuscript order cannot diverge. Content still syncs by hand — the Synopsis field is the living mini-outline, rewritten as the scene changes. On the prose side, reverse outlining is the sync mechanic, per the UNC Writing Center: after a session, write each new paragraph or scene's actual function in about twelve to fifteen words, relist those notes in sequence — an outline of what was actually written — and diff it against the plan. When the diff shows divergence and the cards are not being brought to match, the board no longer describes the manuscript: re-sync before trusting another reading, or put the board away until it is re-synced.

The sync is also where the method charges most of its price. The up-front costs are visible — forty-odd cards hand-written to logline standard, a table or corkboard to hold them, a rewrite rather than an amendment every time a beat changes. The recurring cost is quieter: a twelve-to-fifteen-word line for each scene a session produced, every session, for the life of the draft. That recurring charge is what buys every test the board runs; a board that stops paying it keeps the furniture and loses the diagnostics.

The board is also wrong for some writers. It decides structure before drafting and re-decides it after every session, so it serves a writer who wants the manuscript answering to a plan. A writer who finds the story by drafting gets the relationship inverted: the plan freezes before the discovery happens, and the sync turns each session's discovery into a divergence to manage. For that writer the reverse outline is the piece worth keeping — it maps what emerged without demanding it match a forecast — and the full board is a commitment made too early.

The two disciplines meet in the sync loop. A beat compressed to seven movable words is cheap enough to rewrite, a card cheap enough to rewrite is cheap enough to keep true, and only a true board pays back the tests that make it worth building. The card that can move and the board that stays true are the same investment seen from both ends: structure a hand can rearrange, and a record the manuscript has not outgrown.

## Open Questions

- The card-sort mechanics come from UX research, where a sort discovers groupings; a beat board needs sequence and causation. Whether open and closed sorting behave the same when order is the point rather than membership is untested.
- The two-tier season board carries no published card cap, and the seven-word rule comes from feature screenwriting; whether either holds for a season row is unchecked.
- Snowflake's time budgets are the author's own, for his own process; they remain uncalibrated by a second practitioner's numbers.
- Scrivener automates order sync and leaves content sync manual — the Synopsis still has to be rewritten by hand as the scene changes. Whether automatic order sync makes the manual half more likely to be skipped is open.
- The but/therefore test reads adjacent pairs on one line. How it behaves on an interleaved board, where adjacent cards can belong to different plotlines, is open.
