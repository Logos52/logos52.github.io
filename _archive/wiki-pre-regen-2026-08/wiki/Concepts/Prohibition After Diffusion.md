# Prohibition After Diffusion

Once a capability exists as a file that runs offline, a prohibition cannot reach the party it names — it binds only the actors who comply, taxes their cost base, and taints every derivative work they have already built on the thing being banned. All-In episode 282 (2026-07-25) runs the argument across its first two chapters, 0:18 to 48:29, inside the live version of that problem: Moonshot AI released the open-weight Kimi K3, an administration figure publicly asserted that Moonshot "distilled Anthropic's Fable for the development of its K3 model," reporting followed that a ban on Chinese open-source models was under consideration, and a prediction market put a 2026 ban at 45% — the dated number here, and the one that rots first. The panel argues against a ban for nearly all of that stretch; the one host who breaks what he calls "a lot of violent agreement on this show so far" does it with the only sourced number in the room, and it cuts against the panel's pricing case rather than against the ban. So the mechanics below are what survives and the magnitudes are not. Where [[wiki/Concepts/Regulatory Capture via Doom-Marketing|Regulatory Capture via Doom-Marketing]] owns who ends up holding the gate, this page takes its one-clause diffusion counterforce — a model fits on a USB drive — and works it out into what enforcement actually meets when it arrives.

## Core takeaways

- **A ban after diffusion reaches the compliant, not the accused.** The foreign lab keeps publishing and the rest of the world keeps downloading; the rule lands on domestic firms with auditors, procurement, and counsel, because compliance is what makes an actor addressable.
- **Ask which party is physically able to stop the named harm.** Distillation happens at the point of serving, on the accused lab's own infrastructure, so a remedy pointed anywhere else is doing something other than what it says.
- **An available, unpursued cheap fix reprices the stated motive.** Identity at signup with a bound payment instrument would, on the panel's account, solve distillation on its face; the reason given for its absence is revenue traction.
- **The artifact does not need the publisher.** An open model is a downloadable package that runs with no connection back, so enforcement has to move from distribution to possession and use — which the panel calls near-precedentless and ugly, and which is this page's most contestable claim.
- **Taint travels downstream into domestic work.** On the panel's unsourced account the best American open-source model, Thinking Machines', was distilled off Kimi K2.5, and Cursor shipped Composer 2 by post-training the same Chinese weights; declaring the upstream tainted reaches those, not the foreign lab.
- **The compliance asymmetry gets priced on both sides.** The buyer forced into an approved-seller regime is rerated for an irrational cost structure, and the propped seller is rerated too, since revenue produced by an artificial constraint is not demand-validated.
- **The substitution a ban is meant to prevent is invisible in the evidence used to argue about it.** Self-hosted open-weight inference needs only servers and energy and appears in no revenue series, so "it's not in the data yet" inherits a blind spot by construction.

## Stop it at the source

Distillation, in the panel's definition, is running someone else's model, asking it questions, keeping the outputs, and training your own model on them; multiply that behaviour by tens of millions of interactions and what leaves the building is trillions of questions and answers. The definition fixes where the harm physically happens — at the point of serving, on the accused lab's own infrastructure — and the diagnostic follows in one step. If industrial-scale distillation is a national security threat, the party best positioned to stop it is the one serving the tokens, "because that is the place where distillation occurs. You have to stop it at the source. Once you allow Chinese companies to distill, the horse is out of the barn."

The remedy being pushed points the other way: restricting American access to Chinese models rather than Chinese access to American models. Those two blocks are not interchangeable, and the channel the same speaker describes shows why — accounts bought in Manila and India and run through American IP addresses defeat any rule keyed to nationality, which leaves identity at signup as the only version of stop-it-at-the-source that survives its own evidence. What a domestic-only ban does leave standing is the rest of the world running Chinese open weights, which keeps the usage data and the reinforcement-learning signal flowing to the foreign labs either way, and no rule written afterward recovers a distillation already performed. The question worth carrying out of this particular fight is procedural, and it comes before any prohibition is evaluated on its stated merits: name the party physically able to stop the harm, then check whether the remedy reaches that party. When it reaches someone else instead, the gap between the two is the actual content of the proposal.

## The remedy that was available and unused

Distillation at industrial scale needs accounts at industrial scale, and the way the panel says they get obtained is bulk signup — waves of accounts opened by students in Manila and in India, rolled up, sold on the dark web, and used through American IP addresses. The more industrial the operation, the more visible its pattern, which is the premise the fix rests on. Force an account backed by some form of identification rather than a username and a password, with a credit card bound to it, and that "would solve the distillation problem on its face." What it costs is growth: those steps "slow things down in terms of revenue traction," and the blunter version from the other side of the table is that the lab knows KYC would slow it down, which makes the omission negligence, and that a few points of a 90% gross margin would cover the work.

A complainant who leaves an available, self-funded remedy unpursued while pushing hard for the one that binds competitors has repriced its own stated harm, whatever the harm's merits. That is an inference about motive rather than evidence of it, and the shape is easy to abuse: run the other way, it says any complainant who has not exhausted self-help is lying, which is false often enough to matter.

## After download, there is nothing left to gate

An open model is a downloadable package of software; the analogy offered on air is downloading a book. Once the code is on a machine it runs with no connection to the internet and no dependency on the publisher, so a prohibition arriving after the download has no distribution chokepoint to squeeze and has to reach possession and use instead. Telling people they may not run free, publicly available software they already obtained has, in the panel's reading, little precedent, and the enforcement is called "very ugly" — an assertion the case against below takes apart, because it is the softest load-bearing claim on this page. Meanwhile the file keeps circulating everywhere the rule does not run, which is the whole shape of the thing: a durable copyable artifact, a jurisdictional prohibition, and an enforcement surface made only of actors who can be found.

## Taint runs downstream

A China-aimed rule lands at home because of where American work comes from. What is called on air the best American open-source model at the time of the episode — Thinking Machines' — on that speaker's account "was bootstrapped. It was distilled off a Chinese model, Kimi K2.5." On the same account, Cursor shipped Composer 2 by post-training that same Kimi K2.5 on its own proprietary coding data, which is the ordinary open-source pattern: take what is in the public domain, fork it, make it yours, run it on your own hardware in an American data centre. Declare the upstream weights tainted by IP theft and the foreign lab is untouched; the American derivative inherits the contamination retroactively. Neither provenance claim is sourced on air, and both come from the person arguing hardest against the ban.

That is the reasoning that mobilized the domestic ecosystem rather than the foreign one: per the reporting the show cites, nearly 200 startups urged the president not to ban Chinese models, warning it would kill innovation. The IP-theft framing is being asserted rather than adjudicated — "there's been no evidence of this. There's no evidentiary process" — and a taint that never has to be proved is cheaper to deploy than one that does. The reciprocal exposure the accusation opens up on the accuser's own training corpus belongs to [[wiki/Concepts/The Price of a Training Corpus|The Price of a Training Corpus]].

## The asymmetry gets priced on both sides

Coca-Cola is where the panel takes it: an ordinary company told it may buy AI from two approved American sellers, at a large multiple of what every competitor outside the United States pays for the same job. That input shows up in the cost model, and capital markets do the obvious thing — they rerate a buyer "forced to absorb costs that aren't rational nor market driven." The seller gets rerated with it, on the argument that revenue standing on an artificial constraint is not produced by market demand and carries no future premium, so a protective intervention craters the protected valuation, damages the enterprise buyers, and leaves the country "on an island of overly expensive AI."

The mechanism survives; the multiples do not. Three different numbers stand in for the same price gap inside three minutes and a fourth arrives seven minutes later from a second speaker, none of them attached to a measurement. What is left to carry forward is directional — an approved-seller regime prices the domestic buyer above every foreign competitor for the same capability — and the size of that gap is an open empirical question rather than a known one.

## The substitution is invisible in the data

Self-hosted open-weight inference needs servers and energy and nothing else, so it produces no invoice and appears on no revenue chart — "dark tokens," in the episode's phrase. Every headline series in the argument therefore has the same defect: lab revenue and token-growth charts measure the paid channel, and substitution away from that channel registers as absence rather than as a number. The counterargument offered on the show, that the frontier labs' franchise risk "is not in the data yet," is built on exactly that blind spot, and so is the optimistic read on the other side. What stands in its place is anecdote from an interested party — startups he works with moving off frontier models onto open ones and hosting them locally, which is evidence that something is happening and no evidence at all of how much.

## What a ban actually reaches

A prohibition arriving after the artifact has diffused does not stop the artifact. It relocates cost onto the actors who obey it, reaches backwards into the derivative work they have already shipped, hands the accused party a reason to keep not doing the cheap thing that would address the stated harm, and hides the very substitution it exists to prevent inside a channel nobody bills for. What it does reach is the compliant layer — a smaller claim than "a ban does nothing," and the distance between those two is where the argument below lives.

## Boundaries

This describes what a prohibition can reach after copies exist, and says nothing about whether the underlying accusation is true or whether the conduct is wrongful. The episode's counter-argument on that question — that taking model outputs and learning from them is benchmarking rather than theft, and that stealing the weights themselves would be theft — is a separate claim standing on separate evidence.

Nor is it an argument that nothing is enforceable. The same panel concedes two live chokepoints: terms-of-service enforcement at the point of inference, which is the serving layer the diagnostic points at, and copyright action pursued through legal due process. Prohibition after diffusion is the specific failure shape of a rule written against an artifact that already exists in copies on machines the rule cannot see — not a general claim about regulation.

## Checkable expectations

- If a ban ships, watch where enforcement lands. Action against hosting, model hubs, and distribution with nothing that reaches possession or use is the mechanism operating exactly as described; a regime that does reach possession — audits, procurement rules keyed to weights already on disk, penalties for running a file — is the falsifying case and worth studying closely. Resolves or expires 2026-12-31, the date the prediction market prices.
- Watch whether identity verification with a bound payment instrument appears at the frontier labs while the policy fight is still open. If it ships by 2026-12-31, the growth-over-enforcement reading was too cheap and the fix was harder than a panel of outsiders assumed.
- Watch whether any American product built on foreign open weights loses distribution, funding, or an enterprise contract over provenance, on the same 2026-12-31 horizon. Taint that never bites is rhetoric; taint that bites confirms that derivative exposure, not the foreign lab, is what the rule reaches.
- If a domestic approved-seller regime lands, the gap between approved-seller enterprise inference rates and the cheapest comparable open-weight model self-hosted should come out under 10x, both figures taken from published rates, measured inside 90 days of the regime taking effect. A gap above 10x would be the first real support the 25–100x claim has had.

## The case against

The model's own record is the soft spot, and this page inherited the panel's version of it. "I don't think we have a lot of great precedent for that" is asserted on air, by a non-lawyer, and carried here twice. Outside the episode — and the panel raises none of this — prohibitions written after copies already existed have shaped behaviour repeatedly: strong cryptography regulated as a munition under the US export regime while the source code was already in worldwide circulation, the DeCSS injunctions against posting or linking to code that had spread globally, the DMCA's anti-circumvention rules written after circumvention tools existed, the takedown ordered against 3D-printed gun files that had already been downloaded at scale. None of them recalled a copy. Each of them moved hosting, distribution, procurement, and employment — the compliant layer, which this page reads as the mechanism's failure. Run the other way, binding the compliant actors is simply what a prohibition after diffusion does, and whether that counts as failure turns on how much of the capability anyone cares about actually flows through parties who can be found. Anyone using this page to predict that a ban does nothing owes themselves that fraction first.

The pricing leg is the second weakness, and it carries the market conclusion. One claim about the same price gap comes out four ways across ten minutes and two speakers — "mispriced 25 to 50x the open alternative," things that "cost 50 to 100 times more," beverages that "cost 50 times more than Coke outside of America," open source that "is 100 times cheaper" — with no measurement attached to any of them, and with the episode's own opening description of the same Chinese model as "about 50% cheaper" sitting orders of magnitude away from its own multiples. The conclusion built on top is then declared exempt from argument: "if the United States government intervenes it will tank the stock market. Okay? Period. Not debatable." The one sourced number in the room runs against all of it. Breaking what he calls "a lot of violent agreement on this show so far," one host cites published cost analysis finding that Kimi K3 is "not that much cheaper to run. There's not a significant cost advantage to it," and disputes the capability parity the approved-seller scenario assumes, since the benchmark everyone reacted to is "just one test." That is the strongest evidence in the episode against this page's own pricing section, and it comes from the same person arguing hardest against the ban.

The stop-it-at-the-source diagnostic has a different problem: it is introduced as motive detection — "the tell on this, the way that you know that this whole distillation thing is fake" — and the obvious counter goes unengaged, since restricting Chinese access to American models and restricting American use of Chinese open weights are not mutually exclusive, and a party can pursue both a self-help remedy and a regulatory one without either being a lie. The escalation question is raised and answered in under a minute: how Xi Jinping and the CCP respond to a ban is called escalatory and a move up the ladder, then met with security risks that "can be estimated and addressed," and no estimate follows. The panel also runs the distillation charge close to both ways — the IP-theft framing is called evidence-free when it threatens open source, while the negligence charge proceeds by taking the accused lab's own industrial-scale characterization at face value.

The interests are on the surface and the show says so. The most active advocate against a ban describes himself as the first administration official to talk publicly about distillation, in January 2025, and a co-author of the "winning the AI race" report, asserts insider knowledge that no decision has been made, and frames his advocacy as contributing a voice so the president "hears all perspectives" — a policy participant arguing a policy on his own podcast. Officials on both sides of the split are introduced as "friend of the show." The commoditization case runs through a host whose company sells enterprise open-source implementation; the segment teed his answer up as a question about his sales calls and paid it off with an ad and a promo code. Their own verdict, delivered near the end of the stretch, is "everyone's talking their books," and the cap-table question that follows gets "no" and "not directly." Weight this page as mechanics extracted from advocacy, and treat every number in it as an assertion by an interested party until something outside the room supplies one.

## Open Questions

- Which weights in my own stack could I not re-obtain if a ban landed, and what does pulling local copies now cost me versus pulling them after?
- If derivative taint is the real exposure, does a one-person operation need model provenance recorded anywhere, or is that ceremony borrowed from companies with counsel?
- My own read on whether open models are substituting comes from the same unbilled channel nobody measures — what would I actually instrument locally to know, rather than inferring it from lab revenue headlines?
- If an approved-seller regime lands, what is my switching plan off the frontier labs, and how many working days does it cost to have it ready rather than theoretical?
