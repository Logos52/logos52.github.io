# Encoding and Retrieval

Encoding and retrieval are two sides of one coin: the better you encode, the less you need to retrieve — but you never reach zero, because no memory is leak-proof. Treating them as a balance, rather than as rival techniques, decides how to spend study time at each stage of skill.

## The trade-off

- **Better encoding → less forgetting → less retrieval needed.** High-quality, networked encoding decays slowly.
- **Retrieval never goes to zero.** Some decay is always present, so some retrieval is always required.
- **Encoding skill is slow to build; retrieval pays off fast.** Early on, retrieval carries the system — it addresses decay immediately even while encoding is still weak.

## Sequencing: retrieval before encoding techniques

Before formal encoding techniques (taught later), the right move is to **build interleaved retrieval first** and otherwise just try your best with the encoding you already have. The reasons:

- Retrieval shows results in days; encoding can take weeks before a learner can even judge their own improvement.
- Interleaving forces higher-order learning anyway, which makes the later encoding techniques easier to learn.
- Doing both at once risks **technique overload** — failing to gain competence in either.

## When retrieval feels ineffective

- The early phase legitimately involves many retrieval sessions, many gaps, and high time cost — that's normal while encoding is weak.
- Discovering how much you'd been doing wrong drops confidence sharply — the **conscious-incompetence** dip on the Dunning–Kruger curve. Unlike most learners, once aware of the limitation you can actually grow past it.

## Spacing

Spacing does not need to be ultra-strict. A simple rhythm (e.g. 1 day / 1 week / 1 month) is enough for most; precision matters less than consistency. See [[Spaced Interleaved Retrieval]] and the [[Interleaving Table]] for the fuller method.
