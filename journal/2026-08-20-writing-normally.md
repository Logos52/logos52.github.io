# Writing normally

A sentence is normal if two things are true:

1. You would say it out loud, to me, without bracing.
2. Someone who has only read the sentences above it knows what every word refers to.

If they would stop and ask "what does that mean?", it isn't finished.

That's the whole definition.

It fails when a sentence is doing architecture instead of talking. Architecture is a coined frame ("the named model on the picker"), a metaphor standing in for a distinction ("the product you sit in"), a claim-plus-caveat before any fact ("that's real, it does not mean they swapped the models"), or a stack of careful contrasts that only make sense once you already know the argument. Those moves are fine. They come after the facts that make them necessary.

It also fails when a sentence announces the next sentence. "Here is what that turned up." "This is the mechanism report." "We looked at the logs. Here's what they say." "For several reasons at once." "This section has eight parts." Counting or previewing what you're about to say is the same move as "this prompt has many parts." The reader can see the next sentences. Say them.

Draft the way you'd explain it if we were sitting here. Then cut. Don't build the paragraph out of rules and then try to make it sound like speech. That never works.

The stop is the read-aloud, done for real, before the reader sees it. If you would not say the sentence, rewrite it. Do not dress the notes up as a sentence. Notes sound like "a long writing session started forgetting." Speech sounds like "the model started forgetting." If the subject is an object doing a person's job, the sentence is still notes.

Do not put "you" in a scene the evidence is not about. "You ask for Fable and get Opus 4.8" assumes a request we didn't watch. If the finding is other people's, say other people. If it's this machine, say this machine.
